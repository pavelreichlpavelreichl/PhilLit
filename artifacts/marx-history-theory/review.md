## Introduction

Few bodies of thought have exerted as wide an influence as Marx's theory of history, and fewer still have generated as much interpretive controversy. Historical materialism — the thesis that the social forms of human life are shaped by the material conditions of production — has functioned simultaneously as a research programme, a political doctrine, a philosophy of world history, and a target for philosophical critique. Its influence has been vast; its meaning has never been settled.

This review does not ask whether historical materialism is true. It asks something prior: what has it been taken to mean, by whom, under what theoretical and political pressures, and with what justification? The history of historical materialism is in large part the history of successive attempts to resolve genuine ambiguities in Marx's own texts — ambiguities that no amount of textual scholarship has finally dissolved. Each generation of readers has brought different philosophical resources to bear on those texts, and the readings that resulted tell us as much about the intellectual contexts of interpretation as they do about Marx.

The arc traced here runs from Marx's own formulations in the 1840s through to contemporary ecological and philological reassessments. Marx's texts themselves contain not one theory but a family of related positions. The Development Thesis and the Primacy Thesis — the two premises that Siermiński (2025) identifies as defining the early Marx's historical materialism — generate a framework in tension with the political and conjunctural analysis of class agency that Marx practiced in texts like *The Eighteenth Brumaire*. The late ethnological notebooks and the letters to Vera Zasulich press the theory in yet another direction, toward a non-unilinear account of world history. G. A. Cohen's reconstruction in *Karl Marx's Theory of History* (1978) gave these tensions their sharpest modern formulation by systematizing the theory around the primacy of productive forces, thereby drawing out what a rigorous commitment to one strand of Marx's thought entails — and making the costs of that commitment visible. Vivek Chibber's assessment (2011) of what remains "living" and "dead" in the Marxist theory of history marks a later moment in this same debate, when the question has become not how to defend the classical formulation but whether any successor version retains sufficient explanatory power to be distinctively Marxist.

Three fault-lines recur across the full historical span covered here. The first is between determinism and agency: does historical materialism explain social formations and their transformations by structural forces operating independently of human will, or does it reserve a constitutive role for class struggle and political contingency? The second is between unilinearity and multilinearity: does the theory posit a single sequence of historical stages through which all societies must pass, or does it allow for divergent paths shaped by local conditions and relations? The third is between science and critical method: is historical materialism a positive science of society, subject to the same norms of explanation and confirmation as any other empirical discipline, or is it a mode of immanent critique whose validity is inseparable from its political standpoint? These three fault-lines are not merely academic disputes. Which reading one adopts determines what kinds of evidence are relevant, what counts as a refutation, and what politics the theory can underwrite. They are the interpretive stakes of the field.

## Section 1: Marx's Historical Materialism: Development and Internal Tensions

The central argument of this review — that historical materialism has no single stable meaning — finds its first confirmation not in the divergent readings of later interpreters but in Marx's own corpus. Read across the full span of his writing, from the *German Ideology* of 1845–46 through the ethnological notebooks of 1880–81, Marx's texts do not present one theory of history but a family of related positions whose internal tensions generated every major interpretive controversy that followed.

The canonical formulation appears in the 1859 Preface to *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy*. Here Marx offers his most compressed statement: the economic structure of society — the totality of relations of production — constitutes the real foundation upon which a legal and political superstructure arises and to which correspond definite forms of social consciousness. Forces of production develop, eventually entering into contradiction with existing relations of production, and the result is an epoch of social transformation. This schema has invited a technological-determinist reading: history moves by an internal logic anchored in the growth of productive power, with political, legal, and cultural forms trailing in its wake. G. A. Cohen's reconstruction of historical materialism (Cohen 1978) formalized precisely this reading, arguing that the Development Thesis (productive forces tend to develop over time) and the Primacy Thesis (relations of production are explained by the level of productive forces they optimally utilize) together constitute the defensible core of Marx's theory. The 1859 Preface, on this account, is not a simplification but a rigorous statement.

The problem is that the *Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte* (1852) resists this reading from within the corpus. Tomba (2013) argues that the text stages historical materialism as an interpretive practice rather than a predictive schema — Marx shows how human beings make their own history, but under conditions not of their own choosing, and the analysis foregrounds conjuncture, class alliance, the weight of inherited political forms, and the irreducibility of contingency. Where the 1859 Preface implies a logic of structural contradiction driving epochs forward, the *Eighteenth Brumaire* gives back to political agency and overdetermination a role that a purely structuralist reading cannot accommodate. The two texts produce different pictures of what historical-materialist explanation looks like.

The *Grundrisse* (1857–58), composed in the same decade as the Preface and published in full only in 1939, further complicates the linear-stadial picture. Siermiński (2025b) reads the *Grundrisse* as the site of a genuine intellectual revolution: confronting classical political economy on its home ground, Marx shifted the explanatory anchor from productive forces to relations of production, adopting an explicitly anti-positivist methodological stance centered on the sphere of production and the concept of surplus value. This mature theory, Siermiński argues, accords primacy to relations of production over productive forces in explaining the origins of capitalism and the dynamics of accumulation — a decisive break with the early technological determinism that Cohen's reconstruction takes as canonical. The companion article (Siermiński 2025a) establishes the contrast: the young Marx of the 1840s did indeed rest his historical materialism on the Development and Primacy theses, generating the tension between structural determinism and political agency that Cohen later systematized. The mature theory, beginning with the *Grundrisse*, dissolves that framework rather than completing it.

The late writings make the theoretical stakes unmistakable. Shanin (1983) introduced the interpretive category of "late Marx" to describe a distinct phase in which Marx reconsidered the peripheries of capitalism — his drafts of the letter to Vera Zasulich (1881) and the accompanying ethnological notebooks show Marx holding open the possibility that non-Western societies, including the Russian peasant commune, might bypass capitalism's European trajectory and reach communism through their own path. Anderson (2010) argues, drawing on Marx's journalism, the *Grundrisse*, *Capital*, and these late manuscripts, that the shift toward multilinear theory is not an isolated concession to Russian conditions but reflects a broader rethinking: non-Western social formations need not follow the European stadial sequence, and historical development is genuinely plural. Musto (2023), working from MEGA2 editions of the ethnological notebooks, confirms that Marx's late engagement with anthropological sources led him to treat gender relations, family structures, colonial dynamics, and precapitalist communal forms as irreducible to any single developmental logic.

These textual facts generate two debates that structure the subsequent history of interpretation. The first is whether the corpus contains a single coherent theory or whether there is an early versus mature break — with the young Marx committed to technological-determinist stadialism, and the mature Marx having superseded it. Siermiński's paired articles press this case most analytically, while Stedman Jones (2018) traces three successive theoretical reformulations across the 1840s, 1850s, and 1867, each shaped by different political and intellectual contexts. The second debate is whether the late writings constitute a revision of Marx's philosophy of history or merely its application to new empirical cases. Shanin's framework implies genuine revision; some critics, represented in the discussion assembled by D'Elia (2019), argued that the Zasulich correspondence was a political concession rather than a theoretical shift. Anderson and Musto resist this narrowing, reading the late texts as evidence of a systematic anti-Eurocentric and multilinear commitment. Neither debate has been settled, and together they ensure that any reading of historical materialism that begins with the 1859 Preface alone starts in medias res.

## Section 2: Codification and Orthodoxy: Engels and the Second International

The ambiguities that ran through Marx's own theory of history did not remain ambiguities for long. In the generation after Marx's death, Engels's systematic writings — above all *Anti-Dühring* (1878) and the posthumously assembled *Dialectics of Nature* — consolidated those scattered formulations into a comprehensive naturalistic philosophy. Where Marx's texts moved between immanent social analysis and open-ended historical inquiry, Engels proposed universal laws of dialectics — unity and conflict of opposites, transformation of quantity into quality, negation of the negation — operating through nature and history alike. The result was "dialectical materialism" as a general ontology, with historical materialism as one of its regional applications. This transformation set the terms within which a century of Marxism would be debated.

Blackledge (2017) argues that this reading of Engels as a crude systematizer is itself a caricature. Re-reading *Anti-Dühring* — the only extended theoretical presentation endorsed by both Marx and Engels — as a politically motivated intervention against reformist tendencies in 1870s European socialism, Blackledge recovers an anti-reductionist dimension in Engels's "practical materialism" that the standard distortion narrative suppresses. The political context of the work matters: Engels was not composing a textbook of universal laws but responding to specific theoretical pressures within the socialist movement. In his more recent work on Engels's military writings, Blackledge (2025) extends this argument by showing that even in domain-specific analyses, Engels deployed a version of historical materialism in which military tactics, political decisions, and contingent events interact with underlying economic forces rather than being straightforwardly determined by them.

The most sustained recent reconsideration of *Dialectics of Nature* specifically is Kangal's book-length study (2020), which argues that the text has been systematically misread by both dismissive critics and enthusiastic defenders. Reading *Dialectics of Nature* in relation to Engels's philosophical education and the science of his time, Kangal recovers a more contextually specific and less doctrinally complete naturalism than either side of the debate acknowledges. His earlier article (Kangal 2019) makes the methodological case explicitly: the standard binary — Engels as contaminator versus Engels as self-started genius of dialectical materialism — rests on a failure to distinguish authorial, textual, editorial, and interpretational layers of the manuscript, and on projecting later Soviet codifications back onto Engels's unfinished work. Morfino (2008) offers a complementary philosophical analysis, tracing an internal oscillation in Engels's mature ontology between a Spinozist model of self-causing substance and a Hegelian model of reciprocal causation — a tension that generated both the determinist and the more relational readings that subsequent Marxists drew from him.

Against these rehabilitations, the distortion thesis retains considerable force. Colletti (1971) argues that both the orthodox Kautskyism and the Bernstein revisionism of the Second International share a common naturalist-evolutionist epistemology alien to Marx's own dialectical method. By assimilating historical materialism to Darwinian natural science — universal laws of motion, evolutionary gradualism — the dominant figures of the Second International erased the specifically Hegelian-dialectical elements of Marx's critique and produced a "scientific socialism" whose determinism was already built in at the level of philosophical foundations. Mendelson (1979) makes a closely related argument: Engels's dialectics are "metaphysical" in the technical sense that they impose on history a set of ontological laws derived from a generalized philosophy of nature, and this metaphysical structure became the direct foundation for Kautsky's and Plekhanov's systematic codifications.

Whatever the verdict on Engels himself, the Second International orthodoxy he enabled is not in dispute. Gronow (2016) provides the most thorough scholarly account of how Kautsky's Marxism departed from Marx's immanent critique. Kautsky organized his reading of capitalism around theories of immiseration, monopoly formation, and evolutionary necessity — a "scientific socialism" grounded in Darwinian analogies rather than dialectical analysis of the value-form. Plekhanov, operating in the Russian context, formalized "dialectical materialism" as a general science of reality, with historical materialism as its social-scientific sub-discipline. The result was a picture of history as a law-governed progression driven by the development of productive forces, with socialism as the inevitable outcome — a picture that made determinism not merely a feature but the central theoretical virtue of Marxism.

The revisionist challenge crystallized this orthodoxy by attacking it from within. Bernstein, drawing on neo-Kantian philosophy, argued on empirical grounds that capitalist development had refuted the collapse thesis and that socialist politics required independent ethical foundations rather than derivation from historical laws. As Steger (1992) shows, Bernstein's revisionism amounted to a systematic attempt to replace historical determinism with an ethical socialism in which moral ideals play an autonomous causal role alongside economic forces. Yet Steger (1997) also complicates the standard narrative by demonstrating that Engels himself, in his late correspondence and his 1895 introduction to *The Class Struggles in France*, had opened the interpretive space that Bernstein exploited — emphasizing evolutionary and parliamentarian possibilities in ways that blurred the line between orthodoxy and revision. The collected texts of the 1896–1898 controversy, assembled in Tudor and Tudor (1988), show that Kautsky's and Luxemburg's defenses of revolutionary necessity were responses not only to Bernstein's neo-Kantianism but to ambiguities that the orthodox framework had never resolved.

Two questions remain genuinely contested. The first concerns Engels: whether his systematization constituted a defensible extension of Marx's historical analysis or the introduction of alien positivist and ontological elements that subordinated immanent critique to universal law. The rehabilitation arguments of Kangal and Blackledge challenge the distortion thesis on hermeneutic and textual grounds without fully resolving it; the philosophical tensions Morfino identifies in Engels's own ontology suggest that the tensions may have been internal to Engels's project from the start. The second question concerns the philosophical status of "dialectical materialism" as a framework: Colletti's diagnosis — that it represented a naturalization of history that both orthodoxy and revisionism shared — points toward a deeper problem about whether the Second International's theoretical inheritance was adequate to the tasks Marx's analysis had set.

## Section 3: The Hegelian Retrieval: Western Marxism from Lukács to Sartre

If the Second International codified historical materialism into a deterministic science of historical laws, the tradition retrospectively labelled "Western Marxism" arose precisely as a reaction against that codification — retrieving Hegel, praxis, and consciousness as the concepts that orthodox Marxism had purged. The ambiguity in Marx's own texts between determinism and agency, identified in the previous section, here becomes the positive ground for a thoroughgoing reconstruction. Whether that reconstruction represents an advance or a retreat remains one of the field's enduring disputes.

The founding moment is 1923, when Georg Lukács's *History and Class Consciousness* and Karl Korsch's *Marxism and Philosophy* appeared independently. Lukács's argument turns on the concept of reification: the commodity form, through which social relations assume the appearance of natural, law-governed things, pervades bourgeois society's self-understanding and renders the social totality systematically opaque (Lukács 1971). The proletariat, as the class whose labour-power is itself a commodity, occupies a structural position that makes it in principle the "identical subject-object of history" — the class whose self-knowledge is simultaneously knowledge of the social totality. Historical change is thus not a matter of iron laws working behind agents' backs but of the proletariat achieving class consciousness through the dialectical overcoming of reification. Korsch, working in parallel, argued that orthodox Marxism's error was to treat philosophy as something already superseded by science; the Hegelian dialectic retains a determinate function within the class struggle, and Marxism itself must be understood as a reflexive theoretical practice, not a positivist doctrine standing above it (Korsch 1970). Together, these texts established the foundational claim of the Western Marxist tradition: historical materialism is a philosophy of praxis and consciousness, not a naturalistic science of history's laws.

Gramsci's *Prison Notebooks* (written 1929–35) extended this reorientation in a politically specific direction. His "philosophy of praxis" displaces the base/superstructure metaphor in favour of hegemony: durable historical power requires not merely coercion but intellectual and moral leadership that reshapes the common sense permeating civil society (Gramsci 1971). Economic contradictions become politically transformative only when mediated by ideological and organizational work — the construction of collective will through the "cathartic" translation of economic interests into ethical projects. History is accordingly made through the formation of historical blocs, in which diverse social forces are welded together under the leadership of intellectuals who transform subaltern worldviews. Thomas's reconstruction of this argument shows that Gramsci's hegemony concept is embedded in a wider theory of philosophy as an always-already practical activity, and is not reducible to the manipulation of ideas (Thomas 2009). Gramsci thus decentres economic base without abandoning historical materialism, proposing instead a more fluid and processual account of how historical change becomes possible.

The Frankfurt School — Horkheimer, Adorno, and Marcuse — deepened this trajectory while growing more pessimistic about any privileged revolutionary subject. Marcuse's *Reason and Revolution* (1941) argued that the transition from Hegel to Marx is not a materialist inversion of idealism but a radicalization of Hegel's own critique of facticity: reason stands over against the given social order as a normative standard, not reconciled with it (Marcuse 1941). This Frankfurt reading of the Hegel–Marx relationship — as continuation rather than rupture — established the methodological basis for an immanent critique that opposes both vulgar materialism and abstract idealism. Within this tradition, however, Adorno developed a sharp internal critique of Lukács. As Hall's analysis shows, Adorno rejected both the identification of reification with the commodity form and Lukács's appeal to the proletariat as the class capable of dissolving it; for Adorno, any claim to grasp the totality positively risks reproducing the logic of domination it purports to overcome, and the concept of reification must itself be subjected to negative-dialectical critique rather than deployed as a lever of revolutionary praxis (Hall 2011). The Frankfurt School thus preserves totality as a critical standard — what Adorno called "negative dialectics" — while abandoning the Lukácsian teleology of class consciousness. Jay's intellectual history of the School traces how this move was shaped by the broader trajectory from Marxist political theory toward aesthetic and cultural critique (Jay 1973).

Sartre's *Critique of Dialectical Reason* (1960) represents the most ambitious attempt to ground Marxist history in an existentialist foundation. The basic unit is individual praxis, and history is the field of the dialectical interaction of individual praxes mediated by scarcity and the weight of the "practico-inert" — the sedimented results of prior action that constrain future agency. Through the concepts of seriality (isolated, mutually indifferent individuals) and the group-in-fusion (the moment of collective action against shared constraint), Sartre reconstructs how history achieves a kind of dialectical intelligibility without positing any supra-individual historical subject and without teleological guarantee (Sartre 1976). Boncardo's anniversary assessment argues that despite its anthropological premises requiring revision, the *Critique*'s account of seriality and collective action retains philosophical productivity for thinking political agency under contemporary capitalism (Boncardo 2020).

Perry Anderson's *Considerations on Western Marxism* (1976) provided the classic retrospective critique of this entire tradition. Anderson's argument is that Western Marxism's defining characteristic is its displacement from political economy and revolutionary practice into philosophical and cultural speculation — a displacement produced not by theoretical advance but by the defeat of the European working-class movements of the 1920s and 1930s. Philosophical sophistication, on this account, is purchased at the cost of political engagement; Western Marxism is a Marxism of defeat (Anderson 1976). This critical framing remains the standard reference for situating the tradition, though its evaluative force has been contested. It raises directly the first key debate: does the recovery of agency, consciousness, and praxis represent a genuine advance over Second International mechanism, or does the retreat from political economy leave the tradition politically disabling — rich in diagnosis, poor in strategic guidance?

The second debate concerns whether reification can bear the theoretical weight Lukács placed on it. Gindner's centenary reassessment argues for a selective recovery: what remains valuable in *History and Class Consciousness* is the concept of totality rather than either the reification thesis or the privileged proletarian subject. Reinterpreted in a strictly negative sense — as the coercive structure of capitalist integration to be overcome, rather than as an achieved standpoint of knowledge — totality survives the decline of the industrial working class as the revolutionary agent and retains its critical function as a diagnosis of how capitalist modernity presents itself as natural and inevitable (Gindner 2023). Chari's analysis of the Lukács–Honneth debate indicates a parallel line of critique: both versions of the reification concept risk grounding their critique in philosophical anthropology rather than in the historically specific structures of capitalist domination (Chari 2010). Together, these recent contributions suggest that the Western Marxist tradition's most durable contribution may lie less in any specific category than in its insistence that historical materialism must be a reflexive, critical philosophy rather than a positive science — the claim that unites Lukács, Korsch, Gramsci, the Frankfurt School, and Sartre across their significant differences.

## Section 4: Two Philosophical Reconstructions: Althusserian Structuralism and Analytical Marxism

The ambiguities traced in the preceding sections — between determinism and agency, teleology and contingency, science and critique — did not dissolve with the mid-twentieth century's theoretical resources; they intensified. The 1960s through the 1980s produced two rival projects that each claimed to put historical materialism on rigorous philosophical foundations. Though they drew on entirely different intellectual traditions, Althusserian structuralism and Analytical Marxism converged on one premise: that historical materialism makes theoretical claims subject to philosophical scrutiny, and that its received formulations had not survived it.

### 4.1 Althusser's Structuralist Reading

Louis Althusser's *For Marx* (1965) and the collectively authored *Reading Capital* (1965), written with Étienne Balibar, argued that Marx's mature work constitutes a science of social formations — not a philosophy of history in the conventional sense (Althusser 1969; Althusser and Balibar 2016). The organizing thesis was the "epistemological break" (borrowed from Bachelard): the Marx of the 1844 Manuscripts and the *Theses on Feuerbach* belongs to a humanist-ideological problematic, while the Marx of *Capital* breaks with it to found a new conceptual terrain. This break was simultaneously anti-humanist — the subject is not an explanatory ground, but an effect of structural positions — and anti-historicist: there is no History with a capital H, no linear telos driving development toward a predetermined goal.

The conceptual innovations Althusser deployed to characterize this science were precise and technical. *Overdetermination* (introduced in *For Marx*) denotes the condition in which any social contradiction is always already shaped by the presence of other contradictions; there is no simple, Hegelian negation driving history forward from a single inner principle. *Structural causality* (developed in *Reading Capital*) names the mode in which a social structure is present in its effects without being reducible to any of them — Althusser's answer to the question of how economic determination "in the last instance" can operate without collapsing into mechanism or simple economic reductionism. *Relative autonomy* captures the corollary: political and ideological instances have their own specific effectivity and are not mere reflections of the base.

Balibar's contribution to *Reading Capital* theorized social formations as combinations of articulated modes of production, generating a theory of the forms and temporalities of transition between them. Nicos Poulantzas applied these concepts to state theory: in *Political Power and Social Classes* (1973) the state figures not as a class instrument but as the organizing instance of the power bloc, structurally determined but possessing its own relative autonomy (Poulantzas 1973). His final work, *State, Power, Socialism* (1978), partially revised this framework under the influence of Foucault, reconceiving the state as a "material condensation of a relationship of forces" — a sign of post-Althusserian strain on the original structuralism (Poulantzas 1980). Sotiris (2014) argues, against the standard narrative of Althusser first embracing and then abandoning structuralism, that the concept of overdetermination already implies that structures are irreducibly conjunctural, never fully closed — making the tension between structural determination and historical singularity internal to the framework from the start.

The late Althusser moved in a different direction. His posthumously published writings introduced *aleatory materialism* — the "materialism of the encounter" — which abandons the claim to science in favour of an ontology of contingency: history is the result of contingent encounters between elements that might never have met, rather than the unfolding of any structural logic or teleological drive. Montag (2010) reads this development critically, arguing that aleatory materialism risks evacuating the structural determinations necessary for political analysis and dissolving Marxism into a philosophy of the void. Morfino (2021) refines this by distinguishing two tendencies within the late texts: a genuinely materialist strand focused on encounter and contingency, and an eschatological strand that reintroduces a covert teleology at the level of political hope. Whether these represent continuity or rupture with the structuralism of the 1960s remains contested, but both Montag and Morfino agree that the late work poses the question of teleology with new urgency.

### 4.2 G. A. Cohen and the Analytical Reconstruction

Published in the same decade as the Althusserian high point, G. A. Cohen's *Karl Marx's Theory of History: A Defence* (1978) approached historical materialism from the opposite direction: not French structuralism but the tools of analytic philosophy and philosophy of science (Cohen 1978). Cohen formalized historical materialism around two connected theses. The "development thesis" holds that productive forces (tools, skills, knowledge) tend to develop through history. The "primacy thesis" holds that this development explains the character of relations of production: relations are the form they are because those forms best enable the development of the forces at a given level. The explanation is explicitly functional — relations persist because of their tendency to have certain consequences — and Cohen defended this "consequence explanation" as a legitimate form of causal explanation, drawing an analogy with Darwinian natural selection.

Jon Elster's response, first formulated in a 1980 review and developed fully in *Making Sense of Marx* (1985), challenged this defence at its foundation (Elster 1980; Elster 1985). For Elster, functional explanations are inadmissible unless backed by an explicit causal mechanism — either a selection process or intentional action. Cohen's analogy with biology, Elster argued, smuggles in a mechanism that Marx never specified and that cannot simply be assumed. In place of functional explanation, Elster proposed methodological individualism and rational choice theory as the proper foundations for Marxist social science: macro-level explanations must be grounded in individual intentions, beliefs, and actions. Cohen's reply (1982) defended consequence explanation by distinguishing it from teleological explanation and questioning whether the mechanism requirement is itself philosophically justified.

The Cohen–Elster debate is the sharpest philosophical exchange in the post-1945 literature on historical materialism, and it clarifies stakes that earlier discussions had left implicit. Cohen's position implies that historical materialism makes explanatory claims analogous to those of evolutionary biology: a phenomenon is explained when we show what consequences it was selected for, even without a detailed account of the selection mechanism. Elster's position implies that social theory cannot use the shorthand of biological analogy — the absence of a genuine selection mechanism in social life means functional explanation is a promissory note that can never be cashed. Carling (1993) offers a more fine-grained analysis, distinguishing "intentional primacy" (Cohen's actual version, which Carling finds untenable) from "competitive primacy" grounded in inter-societal selection — arguing that the latter is a genuinely evolutionary doctrine better suited to Marx's historical claims.

### 4.3 Convergence and Divergence

Both projects agreed that historical materialism makes substantive explanatory claims, not merely normative ones, and that those claims are subject to philosophical scrutiny. This shared starting point distinguished them from both the Western Marxist tradition's retreat into philosophy of praxis and the orthodox tradition's retreat into party doctrine. Yet they disagreed fundamentally about what makes explanations acceptable. For Althusser, historical materialism is a science whose concepts — structural causality, overdetermination, mode of production — give it a rigour inaccessible to humanist or empiricist frameworks; the standard is internal to the practice of theory. For Cohen, historical materialism must meet the external standards of analytic philosophy of science; it makes claims that are either true or false by criteria independent of any Marxist framework. For Elster, even Cohen's reconstruction fails those external standards, and the appropriate response is a methodological revolution rather than philosophical defence. Veneziani (2012) surveys the subsequent literature and argues that an anti-reductionist Analytical Marxism — one sensitive to structural constraints and endogenous preferences — remains a productive research programme, though the movement's classic phase has passed. Whether either project produced a stable foundation for historical materialism, or whether both confirmed that the theory's core claims resist rigorous reformulation, is a question that the debates in the following sections have continued to press.

## Section 5: The Central Debates: Teleology, Base/Superstructure, and Forces versus Relations

The ambiguities in Marx's own texts described in earlier sections do not remain merely textual problems: they crystallize into three interlocking theoretical controversies that have shaped every subsequent reconstruction of historical materialism. These debates — over teleology, base and superstructure, and the primacy of productive forces — are not independent; each puts pressure on the others, and together they define the boundaries of what it means to hold any version of the theory at all.

**Teleology and functional explanation.** Cohen's reconstruction in *Karl Marx's Theory of History* (1978) depends on a distinctive explanatory strategy: relations of production are explained by their tendency to develop the productive forces — that is, they obtain because of their consequences. Cohen (1982) codified this as "consequence explanation," drawing an analogy with evolutionary biology to argue that a phenomenon can be explained by its effect even in the absence of a known feedback mechanism. The teleology objection, pressed most sharply by Honderich (1982), holds that this defense fails: consequence explanations are either disguised teleology — explaining earlier events by later outcomes in a way that implies directional necessity — or they quietly presuppose a causal story that Cohen has not told. Honderich argues that each available teleological reconstruction of historical materialism collapses, and that no non-teleological variant adequately preserves the explanatory ambitions of the theory. Cohen's reply insists that consequence explanation is already vindicated as a legitimate scientific practice, but Honderich's challenge identified a real strain: the biological analogy depends on a selection mechanism that Cohen does not provide for history. The outcome of this exchange is significant. Most contemporary reconstructions of historical materialism have conceded the point in practice: they abandon the strong developmental teleology — the claim that history tends necessarily toward socialism — while attempting to retain the explanatory framework. Whether this retreat renders the theory importantly weaker, or productively sharpens its empirical commitments, is itself contested.

**Base and superstructure.** The metaphor of base and superstructure runs from the 1859 Preface through a century of Marxist debate, but what the metaphor means has never been stable. Williams (1973) argued that orthodox readings had solidified a loose spatial image into a rigid two-level determinism in which the economic "base" mechanically generates legal, political, and cultural "superstructures." Against this, Williams proposed understanding determination as the setting of limits and the exerting of pressures, introducing the categories of dominant, residual, and emergent cultural formations to capture the uneven texture of historical experience. On this reading, the base/superstructure model encodes not a simple one-way causation but a field of multiple modes of determination. Wood (1995) accepts the critique of reductive mechanism but resists Williams's culturalist relaxation of the concept. For Wood, the concept of "social relations of production" — the specific property relations and forms of surplus extraction that define a mode of production — must remain the constitutive base, not merely one factor among many. Her argument is that dissolving the economic into a plurality of equally weighted determinations loses precisely what is theoretically distinctive about historical materialism: its capacity to explain the particular form capitalism takes as a system of social power in which economic and political domination are historically separated. The Williams–Wood tension here is not merely interpretive; it marks a genuine theoretical disagreement about how much of the traditional base/superstructure schema must be preserved for the theory to retain its analytical bite.

**Forces versus relations: the Brenner debate.** The most empirically grounded of these controversies was inaugurated by Brenner's (1976) study of agrarian class structure in pre-industrial Europe. Cohen's primacy thesis holds that the character of production relations is explained by the level of productive forces: technology and productive capacity are the independent variables, class relations the dependent ones. Brenner inverted this priority. Examining the divergent trajectories of English and French agriculture in the early modern period, he argued that demographic pressure and market expansion — the independent variables in neo-Malthusian and neo-Smithian accounts — cannot explain why English landlords moved toward capitalist leasehold while French peasants retained customary tenure. The explanatory key is the structure of class relations and property rights in each region. Where landlords had successfully undermined peasant communal rights, the conditions for capitalist agriculture emerged; where they had not, a different logic prevailed. Technology follows class relations rather than generating them. Brenner (1977) extended this argument to the question of capitalism's origins, criticizing world-systems theorists for locating capitalism in expanding trade rather than in transformed social property relations. His 1982 reply to critics consolidated the theoretical framework: the English agrarian class structure — the triadic relation of commercialized landlords, competitive tenant-farmers, and landless wage-laborers — constituted the structural precondition for capitalist development, not a consequence of productive advance. Wood (1995) generalized Brenner's historical findings into "political Marxism" as a systematic theoretical position, arguing that social property relations are the proper object of historical materialism and that Cohen's productive forces primacy is itself a form of technological determinism alien to Marx's best insights.

Chibber (2011) surveys the aftermath of this debate and argues that the attempts to rescue Cohen's framework by Wright, Carling, and others largely fail, but that the failure does not leave historical materialism without resources. Once the strong primacy thesis and the development tendency are abandoned, what remains is a class-centered explanatory framework — closer to Brenner and Wood — that is both defensible and distinctively Marxist. Callinicos (2004), approaching from a different angle, argues that forces and relations are mutually constitutive: productive forces are themselves shaped by the class relations under which they develop, and class struggle is the mechanism through which structural contradictions are resolved. On this view, historical materialism can survive without the strong primacy thesis because the theory was never solely about technological drivers; it was about the contradictions generated by the intersection of productive capacity and social power.

What the Brenner debate clarified — more than any purely philosophical exchange — is that the primacy question is empirically tractable. Cohen's reconstruction invited historical testing; Brenner supplied it. The result was not the refutation of historical materialism but its reformulation: stripped of technological determinism, the theory becomes a framework for analyzing how specific structures of class power shape the conditions under which productive forces develop, stagnate, or transform. Whether this leaner framework retains the explanatory ambition of the original remains the central open question for contemporary defenders of the theory.

## Section 6: Critiques from Without: Popper, Weber, Poststructuralism, and Postcolonialism

The ambiguities internal to historical materialism have attracted sustained criticism from traditions with no investment in resolving them charitably. Four external challenges — Popperian, Weberian, poststructuralist, and postcolonial — each identify a different pressure point in Marx's philosophy of history, and together they have forced defenders of historical materialism to sharpen their claims considerably.

Karl Popper's critique, developed in *The Poverty of Historicism* (1957) and *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945), targets the methodological status of historical prediction. Popper argues that "historicist" doctrines — those claiming that history follows discoverable directional laws — are self-defeating: because the growth of knowledge is itself a factor in social change, and because future knowledge cannot be predicted, no Newtonian-style laws of historical development are possible. Marxist predictions of inevitable proletarian revolution exemplify the genre: they are perpetually rescued from falsification by ad hoc modification. The political stakes, for Popper, are not merely academic. The holism that historicism inspires — the demand to transform the whole social order rather than engage in piecemeal reform — licenses what he characterizes as intellectual irresponsibility and political danger (Popper 1945). The standard Marxist rejoinder is that Popper misreads historical materialism as a theory of unconditional historical prophecy rather than a theory of structural tendencies and conditional possibilities. On this reading, the claim that capitalism generates class antagonisms which eventually destabilize the system is not a prediction that revolution will occur on a given date but a structural analysis of tendencies that may or may not be actualized depending on conjunctural factors. Whether this response fully meets Popper's point about falsifiability is itself contested; what Popper's challenge permanently achieved was forcing defenders of historical materialism to specify precisely what kind of explanatory claims the theory makes — a requirement that shaped the Analytical Marxist debates examined in Section 4.

The Weberian challenge operates at a different register. Weber's argument in *The Protestant Ethic* (1905) — that ascetic Protestant culture generated the psychological dispositions required for sustained capital accumulation, and that this cultural formation could not be derived from material conditions alone — is not a simple idealist inversion of Marx but a demonstration of multicausality. Weber held that religious ideas functioned as historically decisive independent causal factors in the transition to specifically modern Western capitalism. His systematic sociology in *Economy and Society* (1922) generalizes this into a comprehensive alternative framework: rationalization, ideal types, and the typology of legitimate domination (traditional, charismatic, legal-rational) treat political and cultural forms as partially autonomous from economic foundations rather than as superstructural reflections of a material base. Weber did not present himself as simply refuting Marx; he proposed that "elective affinities" between ideas and material interests replace base-superstructure determinism without eliminating structural analysis. This challenge has not been definitively resolved. The relative explanatory weight of cultural versus material factors in the transition to capitalism remains a live question, and the base-superstructure model has been substantially loosened — in part under Weberian pressure — by defenders of historical materialism who acknowledge multiple modes of determination.

Poststructuralist and post-Marxist critics targeted the foundationalist assumptions they found embedded in historical materialism: the privileged historical subject (the proletariat), economic determination of political identity, and the teleological narrative of emancipation. Laclau and Mouffe's *Hegemony and Socialist Strategy* (1985) is the defining text of this tendency. Drawing on Gramsci, Derrida, and Lacan, they argued that social identities are not given by structural position in the relations of production but are constituted through contingent "articulatory practice" — hegemonic chains of equivalence among diverse subject positions. The plurality of new social movements (feminist, anti-racist, ecological) showed that class could not be the privileged antagonism that historical materialism required it to be, and that its priority was an unjustified theoretical assumption rather than an empirical finding. In a direct response to critics, Laclau and Mouffe (1987) defended this as a necessary updating of Marxist theory in light of changed political circumstances rather than an abandonment of socialist politics. Ellen Meiksins Wood (1986) contested this characterization sharply: the retreat from class analysis was, in her view, not a theoretical advance but an accommodation to the ideological pressures of the moment, one that left progressive politics without any structural analysis of capitalism. Perry Anderson (1980) similarly argued, in the context of the Thompson-Althusser debate, that dissolving historical materialism's structural commitments in the name of agency and contingency produced theoretical disorientation rather than political clarity. Derrida's *Specters of Marx* (1993) took a different path: rather than simply rejecting historical materialism, it introduced "hauntology" as a way of inhabiting Marx's legacy without inheriting its teleological metaphysics. The "spirit" of Marxist critique — its ethical demand and its critical orientation toward capitalist injustice — could survive the deconstruction of the ontological presuppositions about historical stages and the proletariat's world-historical role. Derrida thus simultaneously challenged Fukuyama's "end of history" thesis and disarticulated the eschatological dimension of Marx's own philosophy of history, preserving a critical inheritance while refusing its metaphysical foundations.

The postcolonial critique returns to the unilinearity question raised in Section 1, but from the perspective of colonial and postcolonial societies for whom European historical categories have been instruments of subordination as much as analytical tools. Dipesh Chakrabarty's *Provincializing Europe* (2000) offers the most philosophically developed version of this critique. Drawing on Heidegger and the Subaltern Studies collective, Chakrabarty distinguishes "History 1" — capital's universal logic of abstraction and commodification — from "History 2": the heterogeneous life-worlds, communal practices, and forms of sociality that capital encounters but cannot fully subsume. His argument is not that capitalism does not universalize but that the conceptual grammar of historical materialism cannot adequately capture the difference that persists within and against capital's universalizing drive. The categories themselves — "mode of production," "class," "historical stage" — carry irreducibly European philosophical assumptions about secular time and historical subjects that marginalize or pathologize non-Western experience. Vivek Chibber (2013) mounted a direct Marxist rebuttal, arguing that Subaltern Studies rests on a misreading of European capitalism: the claim that capitalist universalism necessarily homogenizes social formations is false, and difference within capitalism can be explained within a broadly Marxist framework without abandoning universal categories or the Enlightenment commitments to which emancipatory politics requires access. The Chakrabarty-Chibber exchange constitutes the sharpest contemporary form of the unilinearity debate, now transposed from the ethnological notebooks and the Zasulich letters to the terrain of postcolonial theory. Bartolovich and Lazarus (2002) document this broader contestation, with contributors arguing both for historical materialism's continued relevance to postcolonial analysis and for its necessary revision — a middle ground that Chakrabarty and Chibber both, in different ways, reject in favor of more decisive positions.

Taken together, these external critiques have permanently altered the burden of proof for defenders of historical materialism. The Popperian challenge required explanatory clarity about what kind of claims the theory makes. The Weberian challenge requires acknowledgment of independent causal efficacy for cultural and ideational factors. The poststructuralist challenge forces theorists to account for the plurality of social antagonisms without class reductionism. And the postcolonial critique has shifted the question of Eurocentrism from a correctable peripheral assumption to a potential flaw in the conceptual grammar of the theory itself — a challenge whose resolution, as the Chakrabarty-Chibber debate demonstrates, remains genuinely open.

## Conclusion

The three fault-lines that have organized this review — determinism versus agency, unilinearity versus multilinearity, and science versus critical method — have not been resolved in the contemporary scholarship, but their terms have been significantly redrawn. Post-2005 work has shifted the interpretive center of gravity in ways that make the classical orthodoxy of the Second International, and even many of the reconstructions of the 1970s and 1980s, difficult to sustain without substantial qualification.

The most consequential development has been the ongoing publication and analysis of the Marx-Engels-Gesamtausgabe (MEGA2), which has made available a range of manuscripts — natural-scientific notebooks, ethnological excerpts, preparatory drafts — that were either unpublished or underread in earlier scholarship. Musto (2020) surveys how MEGA2 has reshaped the contemporary landscape, arguing that the archival record decisively refutes reductive readings of Marx as a unilinear economic determinist. His analysis of the Ethnological Notebooks (Musto 2023) extends this case in detail: Marx's late engagement with Morgan's anthropology and with colonial societies led him to reconceptualize gender, family, and the state as historically variable and irreducible to economic production, and to treat colonialism as producing specific forms of historical regression rather than accelerated development. The "late Marx" that emerges from MEGA2 is more attentive to natural science, historical plurality, and non-Western social formations than either Engels's systematization or Cohen's analytical reconstruction acknowledged.

Saito's work has pushed this reading in an ecological direction. Drawing extensively on MEGA2 manuscripts, Saito (2017) reconstructs Marx's concept of "metabolic rift" — the structural disruption of the interchange between humanity and nature under capitalist production — as a systematic ecological critique that abandons assumptions of unlimited technological mastery. Saito (2023) extends the argument further, proposing that the late Marx shifted toward what he characterizes as "degrowth communism," explicitly subordinating accumulation and technological progress to sustainable metabolic reproduction. This amounts to a significant revision of the stagist and productivist readings that dominated both Second International Marxism and, in subtler form, Cohen's Defence. Moore (2017) has contested the metabolic rift framework on different grounds, arguing that it preserves a Cartesian dualism between Society and Nature as external entities, and proposing instead a "world-ecology" approach in which nature is constitutive of capitalist dynamics rather than a condition they disrupt. This debate — metabolic rift versus world-ecology — is one of the genuinely open questions in contemporary ecological Marxism, with significant implications for how Marx's philosophy of history conceptualizes the human-nature relation.

A second line of contemporary reconstruction has focused not on ecology but on temporality and the critique of abstract domination. Postone (1993), though published before the period under review, has gained renewed influence in contemporary debates. As Jay (2020) reconstructs it, Postone's argument is that Marx's critique of political economy is not a critique made from the standpoint of labor as such, but a critique of capitalism as a historically specific form of social domination constituted by abstract labor and abstract time. This dissolves the base/superstructure model and challenges any reading of historical materialism as grounded in a transhistorical ontology of labor. Tomba (2009, 2013) develops a related but distinct position: historical materialism, he argues, operates with plural and non-synchronous temporal layers rather than a single developmental logic, with different regimes of surplus value extraction producing coexisting temporalities within capitalism rather than successive historical stages. Banaji (2010) pushes this further at the empirical-historical level, showing that "free" wage labor and coerced labor coexist within a single global capitalist mode of production across different periods, which directly challenges the standard Marxist schema of successive modes of production as distinct epochs.

Taking these threads together, historical materialism survives in the contemporary scholarship, but in a form considerably more pluralist, anti-teleological, and ecologically sensitive than its classical formulations. On the first fault-line — determinism versus agency — the consensus has moved decisively against strong technological determinism, though Postone's critique of "traditional Marxism" suggests that labor-ontology readings, not merely determinism, are at stake. On the second fault-line — unilinearity versus multilinearity — the MEGA2 scholarship has reinforced the multilinear reading of the late Marx, and Banaji's revisionism extends this into the history of capitalism itself. On the third fault-line — science versus critical method — the ecological and value-form reconstructions both present historical materialism as an immanent critique rather than a predictive science, though they disagree about what that critique targets.

What remains genuinely open is threefold. First, whether the ecological reconstitution of historical materialism is consistent with its structural core: Saito's degrowth reading arguably abandons the emancipatory promise of expanded productive capacity that motivates Cohen's reconstruction and that underlies much of the Western Marxist tradition. Second, whether "modes of production" retains analytical purchase as a category once Banaji's revisionism is fully absorbed, or whether historical materialism must reconceptualize its basic unit of social analysis. Third, and perhaps most fundamentally, whether a non-teleological historical materialism can do the explanatory work its proponents claim — whether, once directionality and necessity are relinquished, the theory can still account for the structural dynamics of capitalist development that it takes as its central object.

The most durable contribution of the analytical debates initiated by Cohen and Elster may be less their specific theses about productive forces or functional explanation than their insistence on explanatory clarity: on identifying precisely what kind of claim historical materialism is making and what would count as evidence for or against it. That standard has not been widely adopted by ecological or temporality-focused reconstructions, and the resulting lack of common ground between these traditions is itself an index of how far historical materialism remains from theoretical consolidation. Meanwhile, the postcolonial critique — prosecuted most forcefully by Chakrabarty's challenge to the Eurocentric grammar of historical materialism's categories — has permanently shifted the burden of proof. Any account that proceeds as if the European sequence of social development is the template for historical analysis as such must now confront that challenge explicitly. The contemporary state of the field, then, is one of productive but unresolved plurality: multiple sophisticated reconstructions, each illuminating genuine features of Marx's theory and its social objects, none commanding the kind of hegemonic authority that the Second International orthodoxy once held — and perhaps rightly so.
