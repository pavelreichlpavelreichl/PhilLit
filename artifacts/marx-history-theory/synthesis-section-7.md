## Section 6: Critiques from Without — Popper, Weber, Poststructuralism, and Postcolonialism

The ambiguities internal to historical materialism have attracted sustained criticism from traditions with no investment in resolving them charitably. Four external challenges — Popperian, Weberian, poststructuralist, and postcolonial — each identify a different pressure point in Marx's philosophy of history, and together they have forced defenders of historical materialism to sharpen their claims considerably.

Karl Popper's critique, developed in *The Poverty of Historicism* (1957) and *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945), targets the methodological status of historical prediction. Popper argues that "historicist" doctrines — those claiming that history follows discoverable directional laws — are self-defeating: because the growth of knowledge is itself a factor in social change, and because future knowledge cannot be predicted, no Newtonian-style laws of historical development are possible. Marxist predictions of inevitable proletarian revolution exemplify the genre: they are perpetually rescued from falsification by ad hoc modification. The political stakes, for Popper, are not merely academic. The holism that historicism inspires — the demand to transform the whole social order rather than engage in piecemeal reform — licenses what he characterizes as intellectual irresponsibility and political danger (Popper 1945). The standard Marxist rejoinder is that Popper misreads historical materialism as a theory of unconditional historical prophecy rather than a theory of structural tendencies and conditional possibilities. On this reading, the claim that capitalism generates class antagonisms which eventually destabilize the system is not a prediction that revolution will occur on a given date but a structural analysis of tendencies that may or may not be actualized depending on conjunctural factors. Whether this response fully meets Popper's point about falsifiability is itself contested; what Popper's challenge permanently achieved was forcing defenders of historical materialism to specify precisely what kind of explanatory claims the theory makes — a requirement that shaped the Analytical Marxist debates examined in Section 4.

The Weberian challenge operates at a different register. Weber's argument in *The Protestant Ethic* (1905) — that ascetic Protestant culture generated the psychological dispositions required for sustained capital accumulation, and that this cultural formation could not be derived from material conditions alone — is not a simple idealist inversion of Marx but a demonstration of multicausality. Weber held that religious ideas functioned as historically decisive independent causal factors in the transition to specifically modern Western capitalism. His systematic sociology in *Economy and Society* (1922) generalizes this into a comprehensive alternative framework: rationalization, ideal types, and the typology of legitimate domination (traditional, charismatic, legal-rational) treat political and cultural forms as partially autonomous from economic foundations rather than as superstructural reflections of a material base. Weber did not present himself as simply refuting Marx; he proposed that "elective affinities" between ideas and material interests replace base-superstructure determinism without eliminating structural analysis. This challenge has not been definitively resolved. The relative explanatory weight of cultural versus material factors in the transition to capitalism remains a live question, and the base-superstructure model has been substantially loosened — in part under Weberian pressure — by defenders of historical materialism who acknowledge multiple modes of determination.

Poststructuralist and post-Marxist critics targeted the foundationalist assumptions they found embedded in historical materialism: the privileged historical subject (the proletariat), economic determination of political identity, and the teleological narrative of emancipation. Laclau and Mouffe's *Hegemony and Socialist Strategy* (1985) is the defining text of this tendency. Drawing on Gramsci, Derrida, and Lacan, they argued that social identities are not given by structural position in the relations of production but are constituted through contingent "articulatory practice" — hegemonic chains of equivalence among diverse subject positions. The plurality of new social movements (feminist, anti-racist, ecological) showed that class could not be the privileged antagonism that historical materialism required it to be, and that its priority was an unjustified theoretical assumption rather than an empirical finding. In a direct response to critics, Laclau and Mouffe (1987) defended this as a necessary updating of Marxist theory in light of changed political circumstances rather than an abandonment of socialist politics. Ellen Meiksins Wood (1986) contested this characterization sharply: the retreat from class analysis was, in her view, not a theoretical advance but an accommodation to the ideological pressures of the moment, one that left progressive politics without any structural analysis of capitalism. Perry Anderson (1980) similarly argued, in the context of the Thompson-Althusser debate, that dissolving historical materialism's structural commitments in the name of agency and contingency produced theoretical disorientation rather than political clarity. Derrida's *Specters of Marx* (1993) took a different path: rather than simply rejecting historical materialism, it introduced "hauntology" as a way of inhabiting Marx's legacy without inheriting its teleological metaphysics. The "spirit" of Marxist critique — its ethical demand and its critical orientation toward capitalist injustice — could survive the deconstruction of the ontological presuppositions about historical stages and the proletariat's world-historical role. Derrida thus simultaneously challenged Fukuyama's "end of history" thesis and disarticulated the eschatological dimension of Marx's own philosophy of history, preserving a critical inheritance while refusing its metaphysical foundations.

The postcolonial critique returns to the unilinearity question raised in Section 1, but from the perspective of colonial and postcolonial societies for whom European historical categories have been instruments of subordination as much as analytical tools. Dipesh Chakrabarty's *Provincializing Europe* (2000) offers the most philosophically developed version of this critique. Drawing on Heidegger and the Subaltern Studies collective, Chakrabarty distinguishes "History 1" — capital's universal logic of abstraction and commodification — from "History 2": the heterogeneous life-worlds, communal practices, and forms of sociality that capital encounters but cannot fully subsume. His argument is not that capitalism does not universalize but that the conceptual grammar of historical materialism cannot adequately capture the difference that persists within and against capital's universalizing drive. The categories themselves — "mode of production," "class," "historical stage" — carry irreducibly European philosophical assumptions about secular time and historical subjects that marginalize or pathologize non-Western experience. Vivek Chibber (2013) mounted a direct Marxist rebuttal, arguing that Subaltern Studies rests on a misreading of European capitalism: the claim that capitalist universalism necessarily homogenizes social formations is false, and difference within capitalism can be explained within a broadly Marxist framework without abandoning universal categories or the Enlightenment commitments to which emancipatory politics requires access. The Chakrabarty-Chibber exchange constitutes the sharpest contemporary form of the unilinearity debate, now transposed from the ethnological notebooks and the Zasulich letters to the terrain of postcolonial theory. Bartolovich and Lazarus (2002) document this broader contestation, with contributors arguing both for historical materialism's continued relevance to postcolonial analysis and for its necessary revision — a middle ground that Chakrabarty and Chibber both, in different ways, reject in favor of more decisive positions.

Taken together, these external critiques have permanently altered the burden of proof for defenders of historical materialism. The Popperian challenge required explanatory clarity about what kind of claims the theory makes. The Weberian challenge requires acknowledgment of independent causal efficacy for cultural and ideational factors. The poststructuralist challenge forces theorists to account for the plurality of social antagonisms without class reductionism. And the postcolonial critique has shifted the question of Eurocentrism from a correctable peripheral assumption to a potential flaw in the conceptual grammar of the theory itself — a challenge whose resolution, as the Chakrabarty-Chibber debate demonstrates, remains genuinely open.
