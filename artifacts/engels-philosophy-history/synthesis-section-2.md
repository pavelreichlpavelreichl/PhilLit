## Section 1: Engels' Philosophical Corpus and the Problem of Systematicity

### 1.1 The mature works and the late correspondence

Engels' mature philosophy is concentrated in four interconnected texts: *Anti-Dühring* (1878), *Dialectics of Nature* (written 1873–82, published posthumously in 1925), *Ludwig Feuerbach and the End of Classical German Philosophy* (1886), and the popular digest *Socialism: Utopian and Scientific* (1880). To these primary texts must be added a body of correspondence from 1890 to 1894 — letters to Bloch, Schmidt, Mehring, and Starkenburg — which constitutes a distinct and often underweighted stratum of the corpus.

*Anti-Dühring* occupies a singular position. As Blackledge (2017) notes, drawing on Hal Draper's formulation, it is "the only more or less systematic presentation of Marxism" by either Marx or Engels, which means that every interpreter who seeks to revise the received account of Marxism must first reckon with the fact that Marx read and approved portions of the manuscript. The work's three parts — philosophy, political economy, socialism — set out a materialist dialectics applied to both nature and history, articulate the "three laws" (the transformation of quantity into quality, the interpenetration of opposites, the negation of the negation), and defend a realist epistemology grounded in the claim that thought reflects material reality. *Ludwig Feuerbach* supplies the genealogical narrative: the movement from Hegel through Feuerbach to historical materialism, along with the canonical formulation of the "basic question of all philosophy" — the relation of thought to being — that became the organizing schema of Soviet diamat. These two texts, taken together, present the clearest case for reading Engels as a systematic thinker committed to a unified dialectical-materialist world-view.

*Dialectics of Nature* complicates this picture from the outset. Rigby (2024) reconstructs the three laws as Engels formulated them — examining their derivation from Hegel and their application to nineteenth-century natural science — and assesses the coherence of the resulting philosophical framework. The manuscript's scope is ambitious: Engels attempts to show that physics, chemistry, biology, and evolutionary theory all exhibit dialectical transitions, and that Darwin's theory is implicitly dialectical. However, as Rigby (2024) argues, the natural dialectic does not provide an adequate philosophical grounding for the historical dialectic — a judgment that becomes the starting-point for all subsequent critical engagement.

What counterbalances any flat reading of Engels as a closed-system builder is the late correspondence. The letters to Bloch and others explicitly insist on the "relative autonomy" of the superstructure, the "reciprocal action" between base and superstructure, and the role of political, legal, and ideological factors as co-determinants of historical outcomes. These qualifications do not appear as afterthoughts; they are Engels' own retrospective specification of what historical materialism claims. For the "continuity" tradition — which reads Engels as developing, not distorting, a shared materialist project — the late letters are the key exhibit. Any verdict that reduces Engels to economic determinism must explain why, in his own words, he insisted otherwise.

### 1.2 System or torso? The contamination, rehabilitation, and hermeneutic readings

The interpretive division that structures the entire subsequent literature crystallizes around a single crux: what *kind* of text is *Dialectics of Nature*, and what does its textual status imply about Engels' philosophical ambitions? Three positions define the debate.

The *contamination* thesis, associated above all with Carver and Levine, holds that Engels constructed a closed positivist system Marx never authorized. Carver (1980) argues that Marx's use of "dialectics" was a critical method for analyzing social and historical relations, not an ontological claim about the structure of nature; Engels' derivation of universal dialectical laws from natural science involves a category mistake that reduces a critical social methodology to a speculative metaphysics. Levine (1975) extends this into a broader genealogical argument: Engels' substitution of a naturalistic ontology for Marx's Hegel-derived dialectic of historical praxis constitutes what Levine calls a "tragic deception" — the philosophical template for Marxism-Leninism. On this reading, the systematicity of *Anti-Dühring* is precisely the problem: it converts a critical, open-ended mode of inquiry into a closed world-view.

The *rehabilitation* thesis, advanced by Blackledge (2017), contests this characterization at every level. Blackledge argues that caricatured readings of *Anti-Dühring* as crude positivism fail to engage with what the text actually argues. Read carefully, *Anti-Dühring* defends a "practical materialism" that integrates the unity of theory and practice with a naturalistic ontology without collapsing Marxism into mechanistic determinism; far from constructing a closed system, it produces a philosophically non-dogmatic framework adequate to the political tasks of socialist organization. The rehabilitation thesis does not deny that Engels systematized; it denies that systematization is equivalent to distortion.

The *hermeneutic-archival* approach developed by Kangal (2019; 2020) shifts the ground of the debate by interrogating its very terms. Kangal distinguishes four layers that scholars routinely conflate: the *authorial* layer (what Engels intended), the *textual* layer (what the manuscripts actually say), the *editorial* layer (the decisions made by Bernstein, Kautsky, and Soviet editors who assembled and published the texts), and the *interpretational* layer (the readings that subsequent scholars have projected onto them). The "bold line" that shapes the entire Engels debate — contaminator versus self-sufficient genius of dialectical materialism — depends on collapsing these layers. When they are separated, *Dialectics of Nature* appears as what Kangal (2019) calls "less complete than we usually think it is, but" achieving "more than most scholars would like to admit": an unfinished project working through genuine conceptual difficulties, not a finished system whose closure is the problem.

Kangal's methodological move is significant beyond the specific question of *Dialectics of Nature*. By demonstrating that the very objects of the contamination/rehabilitation dispute — "what Engels argued," "what Marx authorized," "what the text claims" — are partly constructed through editorial and interpretive choices, it anticipates the broader archival turn in Section 4, where Carver's work on *The German Ideology* and MEGA2 extends the same logic to the foundations of the Marx-Engels corpus as a whole. The fault-line identified here — between those who read Engels as a systematic distorter and those who read him as a coherent developer of a shared project — does not resolve at the textual level; it propagates into every subsequent domain of the debate, from the dialectics of nature to the determinism problem to the genealogy of Marxist orthodoxy. Whether that fault-line tracks a real philosophical difference or a methodologically induced artefact is, Kangal suggests, itself an open question.
