## Section 2: The Dialectics of Nature — The Strongest Case for Divergence

Of all the pillars of Engels' general philosophy, the dialectics of nature is where the divergence thesis carries the most weight. The charge is not merely that Engels went beyond Marx on a peripheral question; it is that in extending dialectics from social relations to the natural world, Engels committed a category error that recast the foundations of materialism — and that this miscasting is the root from which the determinist philosophy of history later grew. Whether that charge survives scrutiny is the question this section pursues.

### 2.1 The category-error charge

The most precise statement of the charge belongs to Terrell Carver. In his foundational 1980 article, Carver argues that two familiar "facts" about Marx and Engels are actually fictions: that they spoke with a single theoretical voice, and that Marx grew more positivistic with age. Dismantling both, Carver identifies a genuine philosophical divergence at the level of method. Marx's dialectic, on this reading, is a critical tool for analyzing *social* relations — specifically the real contradictions of capitalist production — not a set of ontological laws governing material reality as such. Engels' project in *Anti-Dühring* and the *Dialectics of Nature*, by contrast, proposes three universal laws (transformation of quantity into quality, interpenetration of opposites, negation of the negation) that operate in nature independently of human consciousness. This move, Carver argues, reduces dialectics from a historically specific critical method to a speculative metaphysics Marx never endorsed (Carver 1980).

Alfred Schmidt's *The Concept of Nature in Marx* (1962) supplies the interpretive baseline that makes this charge tractable. Schmidt argues that Marx's naturalism is grounded in the concept of *Stoffwechsel* — the material metabolism between humanity and nature mediated through labour — and that this is a distinctively epistemological naturalism, not an ontological one. Where Marx's nature is always nature as humanly appropriated and transformed, Engels' nature operates according to laws prior to and independent of human activity. The distinction matters for philosophy of history: if the laws of dialectics are really laws of nature, then history becomes a theatre in which those laws are enacted, and the resulting account risks the scientistic determinism that critics from Lukács onward would identify as the essential problem with "Engelsian Marxism." Rigby (2024) delivers an analytical verdict in this vein: Engels' claim that natural dialectics provides the ontological foundation for historical materialism is not adequately defended; the argument moves too quickly from the observation of dialectical patterns in scientific data to the assertion of universal laws, and the explanatory work in the philosophy of history does not in fact require the nature-dialectic as a grounding premise.

The force of the category-error charge depends on whether Marx's silence on dialectics of nature constitutes dissent. Levine (1975) goes furthest in arguing that it does: Engels' systematic naturalism is a "tragic deception" that replaced Marx's Hegelian-praxis-centred philosophy with a positivistic science of laws, thereby providing Marxism-Leninism with its philosophical template. The anti-Engelsian case thus combines a textual claim (Marx did not endorse the project) with a philosophical claim (the project is conceptually defective) and a historical claim (the defect had catastrophic institutional consequences).

### 2.2 Defences — continuity, praxis, and philosophy of science

The defences of the dialectics of nature proceed along three distinct lines, and together they substantially complicate the clean verdict of 2.1.

The first line is continuity. Roland Boer (2022) argues that an underlying philosophical thread runs from Marx's doctoral dissertation on Epicurean atomism — with its focus on the materiality of motion, repulsion, and attraction — through to Engels' *Dialectics of Nature*. Both texts share a concern with the dialectical features of matter in motion and a critique of teleological explanation; Engels' naturalism is not an alien importation but develops themes already present in Marx's earliest philosophical work. Boer's exegesis does not claim the two projects are identical, but it challenges the disjunction thesis at the point where it is most confident: the claim that Marx had no philosophy of nature to develop.

The second line reframes the laws as laws of *human activity in social nature*. Siyaveş Azeri (2022) argues that the Western Marxist critique, above all Lukács' charge that Engels misconstrued the Kantian "thing-in-itself," rests on a dualistic understanding of the practical and the theoretical. For Azeri, Engels' account of labour as a philosophical category anticipates Evald Ilyenkov's concept of "human activity" and provides a praxis-centred reading of the three laws: they are the most general laws of human activity as it unfolds in and through social nature, not abstract laws of a nature external to society. On this reading, the category error dissolves — the laws do not treat nature as a quasi-subject independently of human praxis, but characterize the dialectical structure of the labour process through which humanity and nature mutually transform each other. Blackledge (2025) reinforces the point from a different angle, arguing that the anti-Engelsian critics from Liedman back to Lukács focus one-sidedly on the risks of system-building and overlook what Engels' historical naturalism positively achieves for the project of Marxism as a political and intellectual movement.

The third line shifts the question from metaphysics to philosophy of science. Rodríguez Chiarini (2023) reads Engels' efforts in *Anti-Dühring* and *Dialectics of Nature* through Kuhn's concept of a disciplinary matrix: Engels was not primarily asserting that the three laws are metaphysically true of mind-independent reality, but attempting to crystallize a new scientific paradigm for Marxism — to provide it with the exemplary problems, methods, and vocabulary of a research programme. Evaluated in those terms, the question changes from "are the laws ontologically correct?" to "were they a productive framework for enquiry?" Zwart (2020) pursues a related approach, examining Engels' engagement with cell physiology and thermodynamics and proposing that a dialectical materialist understanding of contemporary technoscience can be built on that foundation, using synthetic cell biology as a test case. Kangal (2019, 2020) occupies a distinctive hermeneutic position that underpins both defences: the *Dialectics of Nature* manuscripts are less a finished system than a series of explorations, and attributing to them the rigid ontological closure the category-error charge presupposes already misreads the textual situation. Engels' dialectics, Kangal argues, is simultaneously less complete and more philosophically substantive than either camp allows.

### 2.3 The Soviet and critical-realist afterlives

The verdict that extending dialectics to nature commits a category error is not a neutral philosophical finding; it is the conclusion of a specifically Western Marxist tradition. That the tradition is Western — and that it had a contemporaneous opponent — matters for how the debate is mapped.

Within Soviet philosophy, the nature/history dialectic was treated not as a problematic extension but as a unity, a necessary condition of coherent materialism. Kuznetsov (1971) argues for a deep connection between Engels' philosophical generalisation of natural science in *Dialectics of Nature* and the dialectical categories of Marx's *Capital*: the natural dialectic is not an external appendage to historical materialism but its ontological foundation, and the categories of the scientific-technological revolution require a return to these foundational categories rather than their abandonment. Arencibia (2021) demonstrates how this tradition was philosophically developed through Ilyenkov, whose dialectics of the ideal drew directly on Engels' hierarchy of forms of matter's movement and whose philosophy was fundamentally hostile to Western Marxism's severance of history from nature. On Arencibia's reading, the misrepresentation of Ilyenkov as a crypto-Western Marxist is itself a symptom of the ideological pressure to domesticate Soviet philosophy within the categories of the Lukácsian critique. Barot (2010), reviewing the French debate on dialectics of nature, shows how the Stalinist dogmatisation of diamat made the entire project suspect even for sympathetic Marxists in France — so that the rehabilitation work in the French tradition had to proceed against two fronts simultaneously: the Western Marxist dismissal and the discredited Soviet institutionalisation.

What these afterlives establish is that the category-error charge, however philosophically acute, has an institutional genealogy. It gained its dominance in Western Marxism partly because of the political need, from the 1920s onward, to distinguish "authentic" Marxism from Bolshevism and diamat — a need that Blackledge (2025) and Foster (2022) both identify as the key condition for the Lukácsian verdict's reception. Whether that verdict is correct is a philosophical question; that its dominance is partly explained by political circumstance is a historical one — and the two questions should not be conflated. The practical consequence for the philosophy of history is significant: if the Soviet and critical-realist traditions are right that nature's dialectic and history's dialectic are internally connected, then Engels' account of historical laws is not the product of a confusion but of a coherent (if contested) ontological programme. The debate remains genuinely open.
