## Section 7: Contemporary Reassessments — Ecology, Gender, and the Bicentenary

The opening decade of this century and, above all, the 2020 bicentenary of Engels's birth produced a substantial reassessment of his philosophical legacy. The dominant impulse is rehabilitative, yet it is not uniform: ecology and social-reproduction feminism drive distinct arguments for recovery, while a residual distortion thesis, now grounded in the late Marx's ecological notebooks rather than in Lukácsian first principles, survives within the rehabilitation camp itself. Together these strands both invert the Western Marxist verdict examined in Section 6 and reopen the question of Marx-Engels continuity on new, empirically richer ground.

### 7.1 Ecological rehabilitation

The most consequential reorientation of contemporary Engels scholarship is the ecological turn associated with John Bellamy Foster and the Monthly Review school. Foster's argument, advanced in his 2008 chapter on the dialectics of nature and Marxist ecology and deepened in his 2022 article in *Historical Materialism*, is that Western Marxism's rejection of the dialectics of nature was not a philosophical advance but a strategic retreat that crippled Marxist ecology at its foundations. As Foster notes, Lukács himself later acknowledged that this rejection "struck at the very heart of classical Marxist ontology — that of Marx no less than Engels" (Foster 2008). The implication is pointed: the category-error charge that Section 6 documented as the cornerstone of Western Marxism turns out to have been conceded by its own originator. Foster's 2022 article argues that the Anthropocene emergency makes a new historical-materialist ecological synthesis, one that reintegrates the dialectics of nature, objectively necessary (Foster 2022).

Blackledge extends and updates this argument. His 2025 article in *Science & Society* takes aim at Sven-Eric Liedman's recent dismissal of Engels as a system-builder who damaged Marxism, arguing that Liedman's emphasis on the negative aspects of system-building obscures what is philosophically productive in Engels's historical naturalism: the recognition that the environmental crisis is simultaneously a natural and a capitalist crisis, requiring precisely the kind of integrated ontology Engels was assembling (Blackledge 2025). Taken together, Foster and Blackledge reframe the dialectics of nature from an embarrassment requiring apologetics into an indispensable philosophical resource.

Two further contributions mark the depth of the ecological rehabilitation. Arboleda (2025) reconstructs an Engelsian theory of ecological development through the lens of Latin American Marxism, recovering a multilinear, anti-teleological reading that resists both mainstream developmentalism and the degrowth current within Western ecosocialism. On this account, the charge that Engels's philosophy of history is Eurocentric and teleological rests on a selective reading that ignores how his naturalism was put to work in anti-colonial and anti-dictatorial struggles in the Global South. Royle (2021) proposes "thinking as an Engelsian" as an active methodological stance, pointing to the work of Levins and Lewontin in *The Dialectical Biologist* as practical evidence that the dialectics of nature functions as a productive scientific research programme rather than mere speculative ontology (Royle 2021). These arguments substantially shift the question from whether Engels's naturalism is philosophically defensible in the abstract to whether it is demonstrably generative in practice — a migration from metaphysics to philosophy of science that parallels the reframing noted in Section 2.

### 7.2 Gender and the family — the contested rehabilitation

*The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State* has long been the most contested text in Engels's corpus for feminist theory. The contemporary debate is genuinely divided between a rehabilitative and a differentiating strand, with both making explicit use of archival resources unavailable to earlier interpreters.

The rehabilitative argument is most fully developed by Blackledge (2018), who defends *The Origin of the Family* as a productive resource for social-reproduction feminism. His argument is conditional but substantive: once the empirical limitations of Morgan's anthropology — on which Engels extensively drew — are acknowledged, the analytical core survives. That core is the historicization of women's oppression as a form of capitalist social reproduction, and it remains, Blackledge argues, necessary for any strategy adequate to women's liberation (Blackledge 2018). Brown's work complements this rehabilitation while simultaneously complicating it. In her 2021 contribution to the Saito-edited volume, Brown reconstructs Engels's theory of gender as internally more complex than the base-superstructure reductionism usually attributed to it, identifying in his attention to the material conditions of reproduction a genuine anticipation of socialist feminist concerns (Brown 2021).

Yet Brown's 2012 monograph, drawing on MEGA notebooks then unavailable to earlier debates, draws a distinction that the rehabilitative reading must accommodate: Marx's late writings reveal an ecological and feminist sensitivity less evident in Engels's schematic account in *The Origin of the Family*, suggesting that on gender, as on ecology, the late Marx was developing positions that diverge from what Engels codified (Brown 2012). This is a moderate version of the distortion thesis applied to a specific domain, and it carries more textual weight than the broader Lukácsian version.

Carver's 2018 article in *Hypatia* raises a different, methodological point: the genre of intellectual biography, which constructs Marx and Engels as heroic paired thinkers, systematically effaces the gendered political partnerships through which their ideas were developed and transmitted (Carver 2018). This observation does not resolve the substantive debate about the content of *The Origin of the Family*, but it does caution against taking the standard "two thinkers" framework as a neutral grid for comparison — a warning that echoes Carver's broader editorial-construction thesis examined in Section 4.

### 7.3 Nuanced and dissenting reassessments

The bicentenary produced not only rehabilitation but also institutional consolidation and methodological refinement. The Carver and Rapic edited volume *Friedrich Engels for the 21st Century* (2022) assembles contributions from multiple traditions — ecology, feminism, philosophy of science, political strategy — and frames its project explicitly as a counterweight to the received Western Marxist dismissal. That it does so from a pluralist rather than a polemical position reflects the extent to which the field has moved beyond the stark rehabilitation-versus-dismissal polarity of earlier decades (Carver and Rapic 2022). Saito's edited volume *Reexamining Engels's Legacy in the 21st Century* (2021) represents a parallel but differently oriented bicentenary intervention: it includes contributions that cross the ecology-gender divide and engages directly with the MEGA2 textual evidence, anchoring reassessment in archival scholarship rather than theoretical reconstruction alone.

Kangal's hermeneutic middle path, introduced in Section 1 as a methodological touchstone, continues to exercise a moderating function here. His argument that both the distortion camp and the rehabilitation camp systematically over-read a fragmentary and editorially constructed torso (Kangal 2019) applies with equal force to ecologically motivated readings: even Foster's productive ecological retrieval presupposes a textual stability in *Dialectics of Nature* that Kangal's layered hermeneutic complicates. The strongest contemporary distortion thesis comes from Saito's *Marx in the Anthropocene* (2023), which reconstructs, from the late MEGA notebooks, a Marx increasingly committed to degrowth communism and increasingly departing from the Promethean productivism that Engels codified. On Saito's account, it is precisely Engels's systematization that locked in a productivist orthodoxy the late Marx was in the process of abandoning (Saito 2023). This argument is notable because it operates entirely on ecological ground: the distortion claim is not about dialectical method or Hegelian heritage but about the direction in which political economy was developing. Hunt's biography (2009), while not primarily a contribution to the philosophical debate, encapsulates the mainstream biographical synthesis: Engels was both an intellectually independent original and, in specific respects, a distorting codifier — a verdict that the philosophical literature neither fully confirms nor refutes but has increasingly been forced to engage on its own terms (Hunt 2009).

The resulting landscape is not a simple convergence. Within the rehabilitation camp, Foster's continuity argument and Saito's differentiating ecology pull in opposed directions; within feminist reassessment, Blackledge's social-reproduction defence and Brown's archival distinction between Marx and Engels point toward different conclusions. What the contemporary literature shares is a shift in the *ground* of debate: away from the a priori arguments about dialectical method that structured the Western Marxist verdict and toward archival evidence, philosophy of science, and the reception history of the ecological crisis. That shift is itself the principal advance of the post-2000 scholarship over the deadlock it inherited.
