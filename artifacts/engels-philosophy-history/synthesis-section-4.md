## Section 3: The Philosophy of History — Determinism, Laws, and Agency

At the heart of any assessment of Engels' thought lies a question about explanatory structure: did his formulation of historical materialism render history a domain governed by discoverable laws on the model of natural science, and if so, did this constitute a distortion of Marx's own account or a defensible systematization of it? Two debates — one exegetical, one analytical — converge on this question, pivoting on the same issue: what kind of explanation historical materialism offers, and whether the causal relations it posits are genuine. The base/superstructure metaphor, the 1859 Preface, the *Theses on Feuerbach*, and the late "reciprocal action" letters of 1890–94 serve as shared textual battlegrounds throughout. Crucially, neither debate can be resolved without confronting the other: the exegetical question of whether Engels distorted Marx depends on getting right what Engels' formulations actually claim, which is precisely what the analytical debate illuminates.

### 3.1 The scientism/determinism charge

The sharpest formulation of the scientism charge begins with a graveside moment: Engels' funeral oration for Marx, in which he compared Marx to Darwin — "just as Darwin discovered the law of development of organic nature, so Marx discovered the law of development of human history." Thomas (2008) takes this comparison as his point of departure, tracing a continuous lineage from this self-consciously "scientific" framing through Kautsky, Plekhanov, Lenin, and Althusser. On Thomas's account, Engels replaced the open, practice-centred texture of Marx's own inquiry with a positivist schema in which "laws of motion" of history operate independently of human will and in which the scientific/utopian dichotomy forecloses normative reflection. The base/superstructure model becomes, in this reading, a causal mechanism with history as its product rather than a heuristic for critical analysis.

Carver (2003) sharpens the philosophical edge of this argument: Engels' framing of the "materialist conception of history" as a finished system — a set of fixed laws awaiting application — has no secure warrant in Marx's texts, where the dialectical method functions as a critical procedure for social analysis rather than an ontological scheme. Levine's earlier account (1975) was the first to give this line of argument systematic form, identifying the contrast between Marx's *Theses on Feuerbach* — with their insistence on *Praxis* as the criterion of truth — and Engels' scientific systematization as the crux of the divergence. Where Marx's dialectic is animated by human activity transforming both world and consciousness, Engels' "laws of development" render historical agents the vehicles of forces operating behind their backs.

The late correspondence letters present the most serious complication for this charge. In the letters to Bloch, Schmidt, Mehring, and Starkenburg (1890–94), Engels insists on the relative autonomy of the superstructure and the concept of "reciprocal action" (*Wechselwirkung*) between base and superstructure. Economic factors are determinant only "in the last instance," Engels argues, and the critics who have reduced historical materialism to crude economic determinism have "distorted it." Berger (1977) drew early attention to the methodological precondition for taking these letters seriously: treating Marx and Engels as distinct authors with distinct emphases rather than as a single unit ("Marxandengels"). The letters, however, also constitute a challenge the critics must accommodate: if Engels himself qualified the determinism charge, the question shifts from whether he held a crudely mechanistic view to whether the qualifications are theoretically coherent or merely ad hoc concessions.

### 3.2 The continuity defence

The most sustained scholarly challenge to the scientism/determinism charge comes from Rigby. In his 1992 study and the revised 2024 edition, Rigby argues that the alleged divergence between Marx and Engels is overstated and rests on a selective comparison of texts. On Rigby's account, the two thinkers shared productive-force primacy, the base/superstructure model, and dialectical method, even where they differed in formulation and emphasis. Rigby's sharpest argumentative move is the "displacement" claim: counterposing Marx to Engels, he argues, is "a strategy designed to forestall a confrontation with the problems which lie within Marx's works themselves" (Rigby 2024). The implication is significant — many of the difficulties attributed to Engels' scientism (mechanistic causation, teleological structure, limited space for agency) are present in Marx's own writings, most obviously in the 1859 Preface, which is Marx's alone. Blaming Engels for what is already in Marx's canonical self-summary inverts the actual textual evidence.

Thomas (2007) provides a complementary route that acknowledges some Engelsian determinism while arguing it can be corrected from within the Marxist framework rather than by abandoning it. His analysis of Gramsci's concept of "passive revolution" shows how the *Theses on Feuerbach* — published by Engels but authored by Marx — enables a reconstruction of historical materialism that integrates structure and agency as a dialectical unity. On this reading, the *Theses* represent precisely the practice-centred dimension that critics attribute only to Marx against Engels, yet they are part of the received canon partly because Engels preserved and published them. The irony is pointed: the texts most often mobilized against Engels' determinism are themselves in the archive because of Engels' editorial work.

Blackledge (2017) reinforces the continuity defence from a different angle, arguing that *Anti-Dühring* — the only systematic presentation of Marxism by either Marx or Engels — is internally coherent as Marxist philosophy rather than a positivist caricature. Treating the "caricatured criticisms" of Engels' mature work as responses to a straw position, Blackledge defends the dialectical materialism of *Anti-Dühring* as a genuine philosophical achievement continuous with Marx's own theoretical commitments. Jiménez Contreras (2021), focusing specifically on *Ludwig Feuerbach and the End of Classical German Philosophy*, similarly argues that Engels articulates historical materialism as a revolutionary praxis oriented toward overcoming alienation, finding substantial continuity with Marx's humanist concerns rather than positivist rupture.

### 3.3 The analytical test — Cohen, Elster, and functional explanation

The exegetical contest between these positions gains genuine traction only when it is pressed into analytical engagement with what Engels' explanatory claims actually commit to. Cohen's *Karl Marx's Theory of History* (1978) provides the framework that makes this possible. Cohen offers an analytic reconstruction of historical materialism centred on the primacy thesis — that productive forces determine relations of production, which in turn determine the superstructure — and defends functional explanation as the appropriate logical form for Marx's causal claims. On Cohen's account, social forms persist because they serve productive development, and this explanatory structure is formally analogous to selectionist explanations in Darwinian biology: a trait persists because of the functional role it plays, not merely because of prior efficient causes. The base/superstructure metaphor, reconstructed through "rights-free" characterization of productive relations as powers, becomes precise and defensible.

What Cohen's framework illuminates for the Engels question is precisely what is at stake in Engels' appeal to "laws of motion" of history. If the explanatory structure of historical materialism is functional — claims about what persists because it serves the development of productive forces — then Engels' naturalizing language is either a dispensable rhetorical flourish or a substantive philosophical claim about the mechanisms underwriting those functions. Cohen's reconstruction brackets the dialectical apparatus while preserving the explanatory core, suggesting that the scientism charge targets the apparatus rather than the content.

Elster's challenge (1980) sharpens this considerably. Elster argues that Cohen's functional explanations are logically defective unless backed by explicit feedback mechanisms — causal pathways through which effects loop back to sustain their causes — which neither Marx's nor Engels' texts supply. Without such mechanisms, functional explanation collapses into what Elster calls "consequence explanation": the claim that something is explained by its propensity to have a certain kind of effect, which is not genuine explanation at all but ideological narrative dressed in scientific language. Elster's critique applies with equal force to Engels' own invocations of historical "necessity" and "dialectical laws": both rely on unexplained functional connections between base and superstructure. If Elster is right, Engels' "scientific socialism" — the claim that history follows discoverable laws — is not merely a distortion of Marx but an explanatory failure in its own right.

Cohen's reply (1980) does not concede the point. He insists that functional explanation is legitimate even without specified micro-mechanisms, provided the relevant tendencies are real and stable, and that the demand for micro-foundations confuses explanation with reduction. The exchange demarcates the analytical boundary of the determinism debate: the live question is not whether Engels believed in historical laws — he clearly did — but whether that belief is philosophically defensible, and the answer turns on contested issues in the philosophy of explanation that neither exegesis nor intellectual history can resolve alone.

The significance of this exchange for the research project is double. First, it converts what is otherwise an interminable contest between anti-Engelsian and continuity readings into a tractable analytical question: do the explanatory claims of historical materialism, as Engels formulated them, have the structure of genuine explanations or of consequence laws? Second, it reveals that the determinism problem is not uniquely Engels' creation. Cohen's reconstruction is built on the 1859 Preface — Marx's own text — and Elster's critique applies no less forcefully to it. Rigby's displacement argument is thus confirmed at the analytical level: the explanatory difficulties critics attribute to Engels' scientism are structural features of historical materialism as such, present in the canonical texts of both thinkers. Whether those difficulties are fatal, or can be answered by Cohen's selectionist analogy, remains genuinely open.
