## Conclusion

The literature surveyed across seven domains returns, without exception, to a single axis of disagreement: whether Engels' systematizing, naturalizing reformulation of historical materialism constitutes a faithful development of a shared project or an alien imposition that distorted the tradition bearing Marx's name. What is striking is not that this fault-line persists, but that its character has changed. The debate the 1970s inherited — centred on a clean verdict of distortion or continuity — has been substantially transformed by two theoretical moves that the most productive recent scholarship holds in common. The first, associated with Carver and Blank (2014) and extending through the German Ideology editorial controversy, shifts the question from what Engels argued to how the very texts and categories through which that question is posed were constructed — editorially, politically, and institutionally. The second, articulated most fully by Kangal (2020), applies a hermeneutic-archival discipline that distinguishes authorial intention, textual achievement, editorial assembly, and interpretational overlay: once these layers are separated, both the "closed system" reading and the crude "philosophically bankrupt" dismissal become untenable. The Carver-Kangal axis does not resolve the fault-line; it relocates it at a higher level of methodological self-consciousness.

The contemporary literature has also substantially rehabilitated Engels, above all through ecology. Foster (2022) argues that the rejection of the dialectics of nature — inaugurated by Lukács and canonized by Anderson — crippled Marxist ecology by severing historical materialism from any coherent account of natural processes, and that developments in complexity theory and systems ecology have given Engels' project renewed philosophical traction. Yet this rehabilitation does not go uncontested even within ecosocialism: Saito's argument that Engels' codification locked in a Promethean productivism the late Marx was abandoning shows that a textually grounded distortion thesis survives the ecological turn, now resting on archival evidence from Marx's late notebooks rather than on Lukácsian first principles. The rehabilitation and the distortion thesis thus now operate at the same level of textual and philosophical specificity, which makes their confrontation more tractable than the older polemic.

Three questions nevertheless remain genuinely unresolved and mark the research space this project enters. First, the relation between Engels' epistemology and his philosophy of history as a unified system has not been adequately examined: most scholarship treats the reflection theory of knowledge, the three laws of dialectics, and the determinism of the historical laws as separable pillars, whereas Engels' own texts — particularly the late letters on historical materialism — suggest they are mutually supporting. Cohen (1978) provides the analytical framework for assessing what explanatory commitments historical materialism entails, but his reconstruction brackets the epistemological dimension that Engels treats as foundational. Whether those commitments survive the late-letter qualifications on "reciprocal action" and the relative autonomy of the superstructure, under the scrutiny Cohen's framework enables, remains an open question. Second, the late-Marx and late-Engels divergence that Saito and Brown identify — the claim that the mature Marx was moving away from the productivist and schematic tendencies that Engels canonized — has not been subjected to the same editorial-construction scrutiny that Carver and Kangal apply to the earlier texts; it is possible this divergence is partly an artefact of selective reading rather than a robust textual finding. Third, the ecological rehabilitation literature (Foster) and the older anti-Engelsian analytical and Western Marxist traditions (Lukács, Carver, Levine) have rarely been brought into direct confrontation; the result is two largely self-contained conversations that share almost no citations, a gap that reflects the broader institutional fragmentation Blackledge (2020) diagnoses as a legacy of Cold War framing.

The research project proposed here occupies precisely the intersection these gaps define. By treating Engels' philosophy of history in the context of his general philosophy — holding nature, history, and epistemology together rather than examining each in isolation — it directly targets the systematic question the literature has posed but not answered. The contribution it can make is a synthetic adjudication that takes both the editorial-construction insight (Carver, Kangal) and the philosophy-of-science reframing (Foster, Rigby 2024) seriously, rather than operating within either alone. That adjudication requires, first, reconstructing Engels' actual philosophical system on its own terms and, second, assessing its coherence and its relation to Marx through the disciplined hermeneutic the best recent scholarship has developed. This is the work the review has shown to be necessary and the existing literature has shown to be achievable.
