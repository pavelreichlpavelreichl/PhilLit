## Section 5: Engels and the Making of Orthodox Marxism

How "Marxism" became a systematic philosophical worldview — a comprehensive *Weltanschauung* encompassing both a metaphysics of nature and a science of history — is one of the more consequential questions in intellectual history, and Engels stands at its center. The two subsections that follow trace, first, the case for Engels as the principal architect of that worldview, and then the substantial recent literature that has complicated the easy inference from "Engelsian system" to "vulgar orthodoxy."

### 5.1 Engels as inventor of "Marxism" as system

The most direct statement of Engels' foundational role in constructing "Marxism" comes from Byron (2022), who argues that the two defining doctrines of Marxist orthodoxy — Dialectical Materialism and Historical Materialism — were coined and systematized by Engels and the Second International, not by Marx, who used neither phrase and who, on a close reading of his mature works and correspondence, appears to have rejected their content. For Byron, the Second International's limited access to Marx's actual texts made Engels the de facto theoretical guide: his *Anti-Dühring*, *Socialism: Utopian and Scientific*, and the editorial apparatus he constructed around Marx's posthumous writings collectively determined what counted as authoritative "Marxism." Carver (2017) reinforces this thesis from the standpoint of textual politics, arguing that "Marx" as a canonical figure — the author of a unified and consistent body of doctrine — was substantially Engels' editorial creation. The reprinting of *The Communist Manifesto* in 1872, Engels' prefaces to new editions of Marx's works, and Franz Mehring's subsequent biography all participated in a process of canon-formation that Carver describes as making "Marx" a coherent ideological object rather than recovering a set of historically situated arguments.

This constructivist account carries a specifically philosophical charge in Levine's (2007) genealogy of what he calls the "corruption" of Left-Hegelianism. Levine argues that Engels, hampered by an inadequate grasp of Hegel's mature logic and influenced by his friendship with the chemist Carl Schorlemmer, fused dialectics with natural science into a quasi-positivist ontology that replaced Marx's critical-dialectical method with a contemplative mirror-theory of knowledge. The corruption then passed through Plekhanov to the Stalinist codification of *diamat* — a philosophical genealogy in which Engels is not merely the expositor of Marx but the original source of a systematic distortion. Smith (2009) adds a further dimension by examining the political consequences of Engels' *Socialism: Utopian and Scientific*, tracing how the scientific/utopian dichotomy established there contributed to a pattern of Second International fatalism: the very insistence that socialism is a science of historical laws, rather than a normative political project, closed off certain forms of activist imagination and helped produce the wait-and-see passivity that critics would later associate with orthodox Kautskyism.

Taken together, these accounts construct a coherent intellectual genealogy: Engels authors a systematic, naturalistic "Marxism"; the Second International inherits and amplifies its determinist features; Lenin and ultimately Stalin codify it as *diamat*. Yet this genealogy is also, as Section 5.2 will show, substantially a retrospective construction.

### 5.2 Reassessing Kautsky and Plekhanov — was the orthodoxy "vulgar"?

The "vulgar Marxism" verdict has faced sustained revisionary pressure from a series of historically careful reassessments of the Second International figures who supposedly perpetuated Engels' distortions. Gronow's (2016) detailed reconstruction of Kautsky's theory of capitalism argues that his work before 1910 was considerably more sophisticated than the caricature of mechanical determinism allows. Kautsky's engagement with Marx's *Critique of Political Economy* produced a theory of capital that tracked tendencies of concentration, imperialism, and crisis in ways that are not reducible to simple stage-schematism, even if his political practice reflected persistent weaknesses. Blackledge (2006) reaches a parallel conclusion through Kautsky's historical writings — on the early Church, the Reformation, and contemporary social formations — contending that the political failures of Kautsky's later career should not be allowed to obscure the analytical power of his earlier historical work. The political weaknesses and the theoretical achievements are distinguishable, and conflating them produces the "mechanical determinist" label rather than discovering it.

Nygaard (2009) approaches the question from the standpoint of Kautsky's writings on the French Revolution, showing that Kautsky was not merely applying a pre-given Engelsian schema but actively constructing "Marxism" as a comprehensive theoretical project through historical research. His treatment of class and state was, Nygaard finds, more nuanced and context-sensitive than standard accounts suppose, incorporating the results of detailed historical study rather than deriving conclusions from social theory alone. Lih (2018) extends the revisionary picture by demonstrating that *The Road to Power* — routinely cited as a paradigm of Kautskyan passivism — was received in fundamentally different ways in Germany (as an attack on reformism) and Russia (as a template for revolutionary strategy), suggesting that "orthodox Kautskyism" is itself partly a label fashioned by later political controversies rather than an accurate description of a single theoretical stance.

For Plekhanov, the revisionary literature is smaller but points in the same direction. Shaw (1988) examines Plekhanov's account of the individual in history and identifies real philosophical content in his determinist position, including a genuine attempt to reconcile structural historical forces with the exercise of political will — a tension Plekhanov never fully resolved but also never ignored. Oizerman (2017), writing from within the tradition he surveys, distinguishes the genuine philosophical work in Engels and Plekhanov from the doctrinal impoverishment of the Stalinist codification: the laws of dialectics, the concept of matter, and the theory of truth as formulated in the Engels-Plekhanov line were philosophically serious contributions that Stalinist *diamat* subsequently flattened into dogma. Sockol (2023) makes a related rehabilitation argument for Plekhanov's aesthetics, showing that his anti-Kantian polemics and his theory of art-in-class-conflict constitute a substantive philosophical program that the "vulgar Marxism" label obscures and forecloses.

Blackledge (2019) takes the revisionary argument back to Engels himself, arguing that his political writings after 1848 show a consistent combination of long-term historical perspective with concrete attention to political conjuncture and socialist organization — a combination that does not map onto the fatalism and passivity subsequently attributed to him. The recurring meta-point across this rehabilitation literature is the one that mirrors the editorial-construction insight discussed in earlier sections: "vulgar Marxism," as a category, was fashioned retrospectively — above all by Western Marxists seeking to insulate a sanitized "authentic Marx" from the taint of Stalinist outcomes — and does not neutrally describe what Kautsky, Plekhanov, or Engels actually argued. The charge, on this reading, is itself a historiographical artefact rather than an analytic finding, and taking it at face value risks reproducing the distortions it claims to identify.
