## Section 6: Twentieth-Century Reception — The Anti-Engelsian Consensus and Its Cracks

The twentieth century forged what amounts to a dominant verdict on Engels: that his extension of dialectics to nature, his reflection theory of knowledge, and his scientistic account of historical laws had introduced a positivist deformation into Marxism that Marx himself had not authorized. This consensus was not uniform — it was contested from the outset by Leninist and Althusserian appropriations running in the opposite direction — but by mid-century it had hardened into the baseline against which all subsequent debate was conducted. Recent scholarship has begun to crack the foundations of this consensus, not by reverting to Engels-as-faithful-expositor, but by showing that its canonical sources — above all Lukács — were more internally complex, more embedded in Soviet-adjacent politics, and more philosophically unstable than the consensus narrative acknowledged.

### 6.1 The Western Marxist rejection and its canonization

The founding moment of the twentieth-century anti-Engels consensus is Georg Lukács's *History and Class Consciousness* (1971 [1923]), which argues that Engels illegitimately extended Marx's dialectical method from the historical-social realm to the realm of nature. For Lukács, Marx's dialectic operates through the reciprocal transformation of knowing subject and known object — a mediation that requires the subject-object relation to be constitutive of the content of knowledge. Engels's reflection theory, by treating the knowing subject as a passive mirror of an independently existing nature, forecloses this mediation and replaces a critical method with a contemplative ontology. The reification analysis that follows — capitalism producing the illusion of nature-like objectivity in social relations — depends on this methodological separation. Karl Korsch (1970 [1923]), writing in parallel, framed the argument in terms of the suppression of Marxism's philosophical dimension in the Second International: where Marx's materialist dialectic was a critical theory of bourgeois society, Engels's systematization converted it into a positive science, a Weltanschauung available for institutional deployment. Together, Lukács and Korsch established the proposition that Western Marxism would inherit: Engelsian orthodoxy is the philosophical problem, not the solution.

Lucio Colletti (1973) supplied a distinct route to the same destination. Where Lukács criticized Engels from a Hegelian-humanist standpoint, Colletti argued from a Kantian-materialist position that Engels's dialectics of nature committed a category error by identifying dialectical contradiction — a logical relation — with real material opposition. This move imported Hegelian identity philosophy into a materialist framework that structurally cannot sustain it, producing an incoherent synthesis. Perry Anderson (1976) then provided the reception-historical diagnosis: Western Marxism as a tradition is defined precisely by this anti-Engelsian turn, which Anderson traces to the defeat-driven retreat of socialist theory from political economy and natural science into philosophy and aesthetics. Anderson's account functions as both historiography and critique — he accepts the diagnosis that Western Marxism marginalised Engels while treating that marginalisation as symptomatic of its broader political retreat.

### 6.2 Leninist and Althusserian appropriations

Against the Western Marxist rejection, two major countervailing appropriations constructed a very different Engels. Lenin's *Materialism and Empirio-Criticism* (1909) deployed Engels's reflection theory as a polemical weapon against Machist revisionism within Russian Social Democracy, canonizing Engels's epistemology as the authoritative formulation of Marxist materialism: matter is objective reality independent of consciousness, knowledge is its reflection, and idealism is a form of fideism. Thomas (2008) traces how this move institutionalised a positivist line from Engels through Lenin into Soviet diamat, making Engels the epistemological foundation of a system Marx had never assembled. The dialectical materialism codified in Soviet philosophy — with its three laws, its reflection theory, and its naturalistic ontology — finds its proximate origin in this Leninist appropriation of Engelsian texts.

Althusser's intervention in *For Marx* (2005 [1965]) and *Reading Capital* (2016 [1965]) is structurally different and philosophically more sophisticated. Althusser's "epistemological break" thesis — positing a rupture between the humanist young Marx and the scientific mature Marx — repositioned Engels not as contaminator but as co-founder of the Marxist science of social formations. Overdetermination and structural causality, as Althusser developed these concepts, drew on Engels's insistence on the relative autonomy of the superstructure and determination "in the last instance" by the economy, while rearticulating them in anti-humanist, structuralist terms. Kain (1986) offers an important corrective to both the Lukácsian and Althusserian framings: the real Marx-Engels difference lies not in epistemology — where both Western Marxists and Althusserians have concentrated their fire — but in the theory of history itself, where Engels's account of base-superstructure relations is more mechanistic and determinist than Marx's own. Kain's relocation of the problem from epistemology to historiography directly anticipates the section's broader arc: whatever verdict is delivered on Engels's nature-dialectic, a distinct question remains about the causal structure of his philosophy of history.

### 6.3 Analytical Marxism and the dissolution of the dialectic

Cohen's *Karl Marx's Theory of History* (1979) represents a third and formally distinct reception: the analytical Marxist reconstruction that accepts the content of Engels's historical materialism — technological determinism, productive-force primacy, functional explanation — while stripping the dialectical apparatus entirely. Cohen uses functional explanation and the analogy with Darwinian selection to reconstruct the primacy thesis with analytic rigour, bracketing both Engels's ontological vocabulary and the Western Marxist critique of it. The result is a peculiar double movement: Cohen vindicates what might be called the Engelsian explanatory core (laws of historical development, functional determination of superstructure by base) while rendering the philosophical scaffolding — the three laws of dialectics, the reflection theory, the nature-dialectic — technically irrelevant.

Carling (1993) maps the internal debates within analytical Marxism about Cohen's reconstruction, proposing a typology of three primacy theses and arguing that Cohen's "intentional primacy" — the functionalist version — is untenable, while a "competitive primacy" reading along evolutionary-selectionist lines may survive. Tarrit (2006) provides the critical external account: analytical Marxism's methodological individualism and rejection of the dialectical method means it "turned Marxism into its opposite," accepting Engels's scientistic ambitions while abandoning the philosophical framework within which those ambitions were embedded. The analytical tradition thereby both confirms and dissolves the Engelsian legacy in a single gesture.

### 6.4 Cracks in the consensus

The scholarly consensus that identifies Lukács as the founding figure of an anti-Engelsian Western Marxism — defined in opposition to Leninist Eastern Marxism — has been substantially complicated by two studies published together in *Historical Materialism* in 2013. Fracchia (2013) argues that Lukács's project in *History and Class Consciousness* was continuous with Leninism rather than opposed to it: Lukács's absorption with proletarian subjectivity was motivated by a Leninist concern with the immaturity of the class, and his own retrospective account in *Tailism and the Dialectic* confirms that the purpose of the text was to demonstrate that "the organisation and tactics of Bolshevism are the only possible consequence of Marxism." The West-versus-East, critical-versus-orthodox taxonomy that has organized the reception of Lukács — and through him, the reception of Engels — proves, on this reading, to rest on a misidentification of Lukács's actual political coordinates.

Burkett (2013) pursues a parallel complication at the level of Lukács's textual development. The standard reading of *History and Class Consciousness* treats Lukács's critique of the dialectics of nature as a settled, career-defining position. Burkett shows that Lukács's later writings — especially the *Ontology of Social Being* — substantially revised the early critique, acknowledging natural preconditions for social reproduction and a limited role for natural-scientific knowledge. The trajectory is one of partial convergence with Engels, though Burkett reads it as a tragic arc: the early critique was philosophically rich but politically disabling, the later reconciliation overdue and philosophically incomplete.

The most philosophically distinctive challenge to the Western Marxist consensus came earlier, from outside the tradition itself. Timpanaro (1974) defends a "biological materialism" that insists on the irreducibility of natural constraints — aging, illness, death — on human existence, and criticizes Western Marxism for dissolving nature into society through what he calls "practicism" and various forms of idealism. His defence of Engels's naturalism against Lukács, Korsch, Della Volpe, and Colletti is the most sustained non-Soviet pro-Engels philosophical position produced in the twentieth century, arguing that the severance of society from nature that Western Marxism requires is both philosophically incoherent and politically disabling. Timpanaro's intervention anticipates the ecological rehabilitation of Engels examined in Section 7, and gives it philosophical grounding that is independent of either Soviet orthodoxy or ecological pragmatics.

The combined force of Fracchia's historiographical revision, Burkett's textual recovery, and Timpanaro's biological materialism is not to dissolve the anti-Engelsian consensus but to show that it rests on simplified accounts of its own canonical sources. The Lukács who founds Western Marxism's rejection of Engels turns out to be a Leninist whose later work significantly qualifies the early verdict; the biological necessity that Western Marxism excluded turns out to be philosophically indispensable. The twentieth-century consensus that "Engels is the philosophical problem in Marxism" was real and consequential — it shaped the agenda for a century of Marxist theory — but its textual and historiographical foundations have proved less stable than the tradition assumed.
