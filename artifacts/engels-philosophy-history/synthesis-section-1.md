## Introduction

A peculiar asymmetry runs through the foundations of Marxist thought. The most explicit canonical formulations of its central claims — the dialectics of nature, the base-superstructure model, the three laws of materialist dialectics, the self-understanding of "scientific socialism" — come not from Marx but from Engels. It was Engels who composed the only approximately systematic philosophical statement either thinker produced (*Anti-Dühring*, 1878), who assembled and published *Dialectics of Nature* posthumously, who delivered the graveside eulogy comparing Marx's discovery of "the law of development of human history" to Darwin's discovery of organic evolution, and who edited and prefaced the posthumous volumes of *Capital*. As Hal Draper's formulation, cited approvingly across the secondary literature (Blackledge 2017; Rigby 2024), has it, *Anti-Dühring* is "the only more or less systematic presentation of Marxism" by either author. Engels is, at once, Marxism's indispensable expositor and its prime suspect for distortion.

This double status defines the research space to which this review is addressed. The central question is whether Engels' philosophy of history — his account of historical development, material determination, the base-superstructure relation, and the "laws of motion" of human societies — constitutes a coherent extension of a shared project with Marx, a complementary division of intellectual labour, or an alien naturalistic-determinist construction that was subsequently canonized as "Marxism" without Marx's full authorization. Carver's sustained case (2003; 2020) is that the scientistic and positivist framing Engels imposed on historical materialism represents a distinct theoretical tradition that shaped how "Marxism" was received in ways Marx did not intend. Blackledge's rebuttal (2020) is that this attribution is empirically and textually mistaken — the alleged fundamental divergences on dialectics, nature, and historical materialism do not survive close examination.

What makes this debate productive rather than merely polemical is that it has three irreducibly entangled layers. First, there is a *metaphysical* layer: did Engels commit a philosophical error — perhaps a category mistake — in extending Marx's dialectical method from social relations to natural processes? Second, there is a *historiographical* layer: does Engels' account of history embody a determinism that eliminates the open, practice-centred agency that Marx's own texts preserve? Third, there is a *textual-editorial* layer: what exactly did Engels write, what did he intend, what did he complete, and to what extent have the texts through which we pose these questions been shaped by posthumous editorial decisions — including Engels' own? As Kangal (2019) has shown, even the central text most frequently cited in the debate, *Dialectics of Nature*, is simultaneously an authorial project, an unfinished torso, an editorial construction, and an accumulated interpretive tradition. Progress in the literature has come precisely from disentangling these layers rather than collapsing them into the single question of "fidelity versus distortion."

The review that follows addresses this question through seven domains of scholarship, organized around the fault-line described above rather than as a topic survey. It proceeds from the textual foundation (what Engels actually argued in his mature works and late correspondence) through the most philosophically contested pillar of his general philosophy (the dialectics of nature), to the philosophy of history proper, the dedicated Marx-Engels debate, the historical consequences for Second International orthodoxy and Soviet diamat, the twentieth-century reception that hardened the anti-Engels consensus, and finally the contemporary reassessments — ecological, feminist, and bicentenary — that have substantially reopened it. One methodological point deserves emphasis at the outset: the most significant recent contributions to this literature, across multiple domains, have shifted the question from "did Engels distort Marx?" to a prior question about how the texts, categories, and canonical figures through which we pose that question were themselves historically constructed (Kangal 2019; Carver 2020). This meta-level move is the principal way the contemporary literature has advanced beyond the deadlock of the 1970s and 1990s, and it will be tracked throughout.

A note on the state of the scholarly terrain is also warranted. No dedicated entry in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy or the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy exists for Engels. The field is defined by monographs and journal debates rather than any encyclopedic consensus, and the interpretive stakes are correspondingly higher: there is no neutral summary to default to, and every synthetic account is itself a position in the dispute.
