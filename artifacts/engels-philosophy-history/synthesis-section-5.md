## Section 4: The "Marx-Engels Problem" and the Editorial Turn

The "Marx-Engels problem" names a dedicated scholarly debate about whether the two thinkers constituted a unified theoretical tradition or whether Engels introduced systematic distortions into Marx's thought — distortions that then became canonized as "Marxism." By the 1990s this debate had generated a substantial literature organized around two poles: an anti-Engelsian divergence thesis and a revisionist defense of continuity. What distinguishes the most significant recent contributions is not a new adjudication between these poles but a meta-level move that calls into question the stability of the objects being compared — the unified "Marx" and the unified "Engels" — by tracing how those objects were editorially and historiographically assembled in the first place.

### 4.1 Anti-Engelsians vs. revisionist defenders

The Anglophone anti-Engelsian tradition draws its sharpest formulations from Norman Levine and Terrell Carver. Levine's two major studies argue that Marx and Engels held genuinely divergent philosophical outlooks rooted in their divergent appropriations of Hegel. For Marx, the Hegelian dialectic remained a critical method embedded in human praxis and historical self-activity; for Engels, it became a set of ontological laws governing nature and history alike. The upshot, in Levine's formulation, is that "Marxism" and "Engelsism" are two distinct theories: where Marx derived history from the praxis of self-producing human beings, Engels transposed dialectics onto nature and treated historical development as the expression of law-governed processes (Levine 2006). The earlier study, *The Tragic Deception* (1975), pressed this claim into a causal genealogy: Engels' philosophical revision supplied Marxism-Leninism with its template. Carver's contribution, beginning with *Marx and Engels: The Intellectual Relationship* (1983), adds a methodological dimension: not only did Engels diverge philosophically, but the persistent fiction of a seamless "Marx and Engels" — two voices, one doctrine — has itself distorted both thinkers by obscuring the specific intellectual moves each made.

Recent focused textual work has supplied additional evidence for divergence at the level of individual problems. Koryakovtsev (2024) compares Engels' account of the origin of private property and the state in *The Origin of the Family* with Marx's treatment in the *Grundrisse* manuscripts, concluding that the two authors assign different roles to labor in explaining the emergence of the state: Engels attributes the state's rise primarily to class contradictions understood as a socio-political mechanism, while Marx emphasizes an active labor-mediated process in which labor creates its own conditions of domination. Similarly, Jiménez Contreras (2021), while ultimately defending Engels, marks the need to specify how *Ludwig Feuerbach and the End of Classical German Philosophy* reads the Hegel-Feuerbach-Marx transition differently from the way Marx himself described it.

Against the divergence thesis, the revisionist literature argues that the alleged divergences are overstated and that close examination reveals a shared framework. Hunley (1991), in a comprehensive intellectual biography, contends that Engels' naturalistic and dialectical commitments were largely shared by Marx himself and that the two thinkers pursued a coherent joint project. Rigby's *Engels and the Formation of Marxism* (1992) offers the most methodically sustained revisionist account, treating the Marx-Engels problem as an empirically resolvable historical question rather than a matter of philosophical interpretation. Rigby argues that many core features of historical materialism — productive-force primacy, base/superstructure, the relationship between class and historical change — were formulated most explicitly by Engels but not independently of Marx. Jiménez Contreras (2021) extends this revisionist line into the specific case of *Ludwig Feuerbach*, arguing that Engels' reading of the Hegel-Feuerbach transition articulates historical materialism as a revolutionary praxis oriented toward overcoming alienation — a position continuous, rather than discontinuous, with Marx's humanist concerns. Blackledge (2017) reinforces the revisionist position philosophically: Anti-Dühring, as the only approximately systematic presentation of Marxism by either author, articulates a coherent "practical materialism" whose caricatured reception by critics has substituted a dismissal of a position Engels never actually held.

### 4.2 The editorial-construction thesis

The most significant recent reorientation of the debate comes from a body of work — principally by Carver, and in collaboration with Daniel Blank — that shifts attention from comparing two doctrines to historicizing how the comparison's very objects were produced. The key move is the claim that "Marx and Engels" as a unified textual corpus, and "Marx" as a bounded intellectual identity, are partly editorial constructions rather than natural givens.

Carver and Blank's *A Political History of the Editions of Marx and Engels's "German Ideology" Manuscripts* (2014) provides the most detailed account of how the manuscripts that scholars cite as the founding text of historical materialism came to be published as *The German Ideology*. Since the 1920s, editors have assembled and imposed coherence on manuscripts that Marx and Engels themselves abandoned, selecting, combining, and titling fragments according to interpretive priorities that varied across Soviet, East German, and Western publishing contexts. What readers have engaged as a canonical jointly-authored text is, on this account, an editorially produced artifact. Different political moments shaped which manuscripts counted as the authoritative statement of historical materialism.

Carver pursues the same logic at a higher level of generality in "Making Marx Marx" (2017), arguing that "Marx" as an intellectual identity — the figure whose thought scholars reconstruct, contest, and refine — was significantly shaped by Engels' posthumous editorial practices: his republication of Marx's works with new prefaces, his own summarizing texts, and his selection of what to present under Marx's name. Engels' editorial decisions "promoted Engels' projects and ideas as following directly from, and intentionally supplementary to, Marx's 'thought,'" while erasing what Carver calls the "everyday Marx" — the more contingent, politically embedded, and theoretically plural figure recoverable from the manuscripts.

The MEGA2 critical edition has become the material basis on which these arguments are tested and refined. Carver's "Whose Hand is the Last Hand?" (2019) uses the 2017 MEGA2 volume to revisit handwriting attribution in the German Ideology manuscripts. Rather than resolving the authorship question, the new critical edition — which presents the manuscripts non-linearly, without artificial chapter divisions — leaves open which passages are attributable to Marx and which to Engels, and what this uncertainty implies for any reading that takes the manuscripts as evidence of a unified historical-materialist theory.

This editorial-construction thesis is the principal advance beyond the 1970s-1990s deadlock. Where the older debate asked which thinker was philosophically correct, this line of inquiry asks how the objects of that comparison — "Marx's philosophy of history," "Engels' philosophy of nature," the jointly-authored doctrine — came to be stabilized as objects in the first place. It parallels, at the level of textual history, the move Kangal (2019) makes in the interpretation of *Dialectics of Nature*: distinguishing authorial, textual, editorial, and interpretational layers so that the question of what Engels "argued" cannot be answered without first specifying at which layer one is operating.

### 4.3 Was the divergence thesis itself a Cold War artefact?

The third dimension of the contemporary reassessment turns the critical lens on the anti-Engelsian tradition itself. Blackledge's bicentenary survey (2020) argues that the claims that Engels fundamentally distorted Marx arose from a specific ideological conjuncture: the need, particularly from the 1950s onward, to disentangle an "authentic" or "humanist" Marx from Stalinism and Soviet diamat. Once that political pressure was the primary driver, it became convenient to locate the source of distortion in Engels — whose systematic presentations had indeed supplied the philosophical raw material for Marxism-Leninism. The divergence thesis, on this reading, is not a discovery about Engels' texts but a retrospective construction generated by the politics of de-Stalinization. Blackledge does not deny that real interpretive differences exist between Marx and Engels, but argues these have been dramatically amplified and given a teleological shape — culminating in Stalinism — that is ideologically motivated rather than philologically grounded.

Carver's bicentenary introduction (2020) approaches the same terrain from a different angle. Rather than defending Engels against the divergence thesis, Carver proposes what he calls an "Engels-centric" approach to intellectual biography: reading Engels on his own terms, as an independent thinker with his own archive and concerns, rather than as Marx's junior partner or as the vector through whom Marx's thought was transmitted or distorted. This proposal implicitly concedes something to the revisionist critics — treating Engels as a serious independent figure rather than a contaminant — while preserving the core methodological point that "Marxism" as a unified tradition was constructed rather than discovered. Steger and Carver's edited volume *Engels After Marx* (1999) documented the state of the debate at the end of the twentieth century, assembling contributors from multiple interpretive positions and revealing both the depth of disagreement and the extent to which the disagreement was shaped by disciplinary and political contexts outside the texts themselves.

The net result of these three strands is that the "Marx-Engels problem," while it has not disappeared, has been substantially reconstituted. The question is no longer simply whether Engels was faithful to Marx — a question that assumes stable, independently accessible versions of each thinker's thought — but how the categories through which that question has been posed were themselves assembled. The live research frontier concerns the editorial history of the canonical texts and the historiographical conditions under which the divergence or continuity verdict came to be seen as answerable in the first place.
