## Introduction

Karl Leonhard Reinhold occupies a curious position in the historiography of German Idealism: central enough that Fichte, Schelling, and Hegel all began their careers by engaging with him, yet marginal enough that most contemporary surveys treat him primarily as a popularizer of Kant rather than a philosopher in his own right. That marginalization is in part an artifact of the very historical narrative Reinhold himself helped to construct. His Elementary Philosophy (*Elementarphilosophie*), his engagement with Bardili's rational realism, and his late philological turn have long been read as a sequence of retreats from positions he could not sustain. Recent scholarship has increasingly challenged this picture, recovering Reinhold as a thinker with a distinctive and consequential theory of what the history of philosophy is and why it matters — a theory that mediates, more than has been recognized, between the eighteenth-century tradition of *Historia Philosophica* and Hegel's mature dialectical historiography.

The core contribution this review reconstructs is what Reinhold called the *Streit der Systeme* — the conflict of systems. For Reinhold, the history of philosophy is not a chronicle of incremental progress toward truth, nor a neutral catalogue of opinions organized by sect or chronology. It is a history driven by systematic conflict: competing philosophical systems arise, contend, and fall without resolution because none of them has secured a genuine first principle. The demand for such a principle is simultaneously a systematic and a historiographical claim. It explains why philosophy has always disagreed, and it defines what a successful philosophy would have to do: not merely add another system to the conflict, but dissolve the conditions under which conflict recurs. The *Elementarphilosophie*'s principle of consciousness — the *Satz des Bewusstseins* — is offered as exactly this: the terminus of the *Streit* (Reinhold 1790; 1791).

The stakes of recovering this thesis extend beyond Reinhold scholarship. The *Streit der Systeme* idea establishes a model of philosophical history as inherently conflictual and teleologically oriented toward its own resolution — a model that Fichte appropriates and reworks, that Schelling and the early Romantics radicalize by denying any terminal resolution, and that Hegel absorbs and transforms into his dialectical account of philosophy's self-unfolding through contradiction. Understanding how post-Kantian philosophy conceived the history of philosophy as a philosophical problem requires situating Reinhold's contribution at the centre of that development, not at its margins (Beiser 1987; Pinkard 2002).

The secondary literature has undergone a notable shift in this direction since roughly 2000. Karl Ameriks's work on the "historical turn" in Kant and post-Kantian philosophy placed Reinhold within a broader narrative about foundationalism and the fate of autonomy, establishing an influential Anglophone framework (Ameriks 2006). Martin Bondeli's sustained developmental reconstruction of Reinhold's phases offered the most detailed account of the continuities and discontinuities in his career (Bondeli 2015; Bondeli 2018). More recently, Elise Frketich, Silvan Imhof, and Ezequiel Posesorski have pressed further, arguing that Reinhold's historiographical contribution is a philosophically distinct achievement separable from — and not simply superseded by — the fate of his systematic programs (Frketich 2016; Imhof 2020; Posesorski 2010).

This review maps the terrain of these debates in five stages. Section 1 reconstructs the eighteenth-century background — the *Historia Philosophica* tradition of Brucker and Tennemann, and Kant's own scattered reflections on a "philosophizing history of philosophy" — as the inherited context Reinhold responded to. Section 2 presents Reinhold's *Streit der Systeme* thesis and its internal logic, showing how the demand for a first principle is inseparable from a diagnosis of philosophy's historical situation. Section 3 traces the phases of Reinhold's career and the scholarly debate over continuity and rupture across them. Section 4 surveys the main positions in contemporary scholarship. Section 5 traces the afterlife of Reinhold's historiographical thesis in Fichte, Schelling, the early Romantics, and Hegel, arguing that the thesis's influence outlasted the specific systematic vehicles through which it was first expressed.

## Section 1: The Eighteenth-Century Background: Historia Philosophica and Kant's Philosophy of History

The philosophical historiography that Reinhold inherited was shaped by two distinct and partially competing models: the eclectic-doxographical tradition culminating in Brucker's *Historia Critica Philosophiae*, and the transcendental-teleological model that Kant's critical philosophy implied and that his student Tennemann attempted to execute. Understanding Reinhold's *Streit der Systeme* thesis requires placing it against both.

### 1.1 Brucker and the Eclectic Tradition

Johann Jacob Brucker's five-volume *Historia Critica Philosophiae* (1742–44) was the authoritative work of philosophical historiography in the German Enlightenment and remained a standard reference well into the period of Reinhold's activity (Brucker 1742). Its ambition was encyclopedic and its method eclectic: to survey all philosophical opinions from antiquity to the present, organized by school, sect, and chronological succession, with critical apparatus drawn from a variety of philosophical traditions without commitment to any single one. Brucker's organizing unit was the philosophical "sect" (*Sekte*) and, with increasing prominence in his later discussions, the philosophical "system" — a term he deployed to describe any internally consistent body of doctrine.

Leo Catana has shown that Brucker's use of "system of philosophy" as a historiographical category was itself a significant conceptual innovation (Catana 2008). By treating each major philosopher's contribution as a *Systema* — a coherent, self-enclosed theoretical whole — Brucker imposed a structural unity on the history of philosophy that made comparison and evaluation possible. But this same move created a problem that Brucker's own eclecticism could not solve: if philosophy's history is a succession of competing systems, what criterion determines which system is correct, and why does the conflict not terminate? Brucker's implicit answer was that enlightened eclecticism — selecting truths from each system — offers an approximation of the whole. Mario Longo's detailed study of Brucker confirms that this eclecticism was simultaneously the strength and the limitation of the *Historia Critica*: it enabled comprehensive coverage but foreclosed any account of how philosophical disagreement might be resolved rather than merely catalogued (Longo 2010).

The eclectic model received its widest European dissemination through William Enfield's 1791 English abridgement, *The History of Philosophy from the Earliest Periods: Drawn Up from Brucker's Historia Critica Philosophiae*, which stripped away much of the critical apparatus and presented the historical narrative in condensed form. What Enfield preserved, however, was precisely the organizing idea that mattered for subsequent historiographers: philosophy's history as a succession of systems standing in unresolved competition (Enfield 1791).

Reinhold was deeply familiar with this tradition. When he speaks of the *Streit der Systeme*, he is not inventing a concept from scratch; he is radicalizing a category that Brucker had already placed at the centre of philosophical historiography. The difference is that for Brucker, the *Streit* was a descriptive feature of philosophy's history that eclecticism could partially mitigate. For Reinhold, it is the structural problem that philosophy must solve in order to become a genuine science.

### 1.2 Kant's Philosophizing History of Philosophy

Kant's relationship to the history of philosophy is more complicated than a simple contrast between critical philosophy and its predecessors. His remarks on philosophical historiography — scattered across the Prize Essay fragments, the *Critique of Pure Reason*, and the posthumously published *Fortschritte der Metaphysik* — suggest the outlines of what he called a "philosophizing history of philosophy": an account of philosophy's development reconstructed not from the external record of opinions but from the internal logic of reason's self-examination.

The key move, elaborated in the *Fortschritte* (1793/1804), is a transcendental one: the history of metaphysics can be understood a priori from the nature of reason itself, since reason's constitutive problems generate a determinate sequence of positions as it confronts its own limits (Kant 1793). Pure reason, Kant suggests, is not simply wrong in its speculative ambitions; its errors are systematically necessitated by the structure of reason, and the critical philosophy makes this necessity intelligible for the first time. On this view, the history of philosophy is not merely an empirical narrative but a rational reconstruction of reason's self-knowledge.

This proposal proved enormously influential but left open the question of the relationship between the a priori reconstruction and the actual historical record. It also introduced a teleological element — the history of philosophy culminates in the critical philosophy — that Reinhold both absorbed and modified. Where Kant situates the terminal point at the critique of pure reason's limits, Reinhold locates it at the discovery of a universally acceptable first principle. The transcendental criterion survives in Reinhold's work, but it is reoriented around the practical problem of ending philosophical conflict rather than around the theoretical problem of diagnosing reason's limits. Ameriks has emphasized how much of Reinhold's project is driven by this ambition to complete Kant's work by providing what Kant himself had not: a single, universally compelling starting point from which the entire critical philosophy could be derived (Ameriks 2006).

### 1.3 Tennemann and Post-Kantian Historiography

Wilhelm Gottlieb Tennemann's eleven-volume *Geschichte der Philosophie* (1798–1819) represents the most systematic attempt before Hegel to execute Kant's suggestion about a philosophizing history of philosophy. Tennemann adopted the Kantian critical philosophy as the explicit standpoint from which to evaluate all preceding philosophical positions: the history of philosophy is the progressive development of reason's self-clarification, culminating in the critical standpoint that makes philosophical history fully comprehensible for the first time (Tennemann 1798).

The result was a historiography with a clear teleological structure and a definite terminal point — the Kantian philosophy — but one that left Reinhold's central problem unaddressed. Tennemann's account explained why earlier philosophers failed to achieve the critical standpoint; it did not explain why even philosophers who accepted Kant's results continued to disagree about first principles. It was precisely this continuing disagreement — the persistence of the *Streit* even within the post-Kantian camp — that Reinhold's historiographical thesis was designed to explain. From Reinhold's perspective, Tennemann's progressivist account mistook the critical philosophy's identification of reason's limits for the resolution of the conflict among systems. The conflict persisted because the critical philosophy itself lacked a secure first principle, and its lack was what generated the competing systems of Reinhold's own contemporaries (Piaia and Santinello 2011).

Tennemann's *Grundriss der Geschichte der Philosophie* (1812) — a condensed textbook version of the larger work, widely used in German universities — disseminated this Kantian-teleological model of philosophical history to several generations of students (Tennemann 1812). It is the immediate foil against which Reinhold's own historiographical position can be measured: both accept Kant as a turning point, but where Tennemann reads the post-Kantian situation as one of ongoing progress, Reinhold reads it as one of renewed and structurally analogous conflict.

The historiographical field Reinhold entered in the late 1780s was thus structured by a tension between two inadequate models: Brucker's eclectic-doxographical cataloguing, which described the *Streit* without explaining it, and the Kantian-teleological progressivism of Tennemann, which explained the pre-critical *Streit* without accounting for its post-critical recurrence. Reinhold's *Streit der Systeme* thesis is best understood as a third position that acknowledges both the structural character of philosophical conflict and the need for a genuinely non-circular resolution — a resolution that Tennemann's teleology merely assumed rather than provided.

## Section 2: Reinhold's Streit der Systeme Thesis and the First-Principle Solution

Reinhold's most distinctive contribution to philosophical historiography is his diagnosis of the history of philosophy as a *Streit der Systeme* — a persistent conflict among philosophical systems — and his claim that this conflict has a determinate structural cause whose removal would constitute the terminus of philosophy's history as a sequence of failures. This claim appears first in embryo in the *Briefe über die Kantische Philosophie* (1790–92) and receives its fullest elaboration in the *Beyträge zur Berichtigung bisheriger Mißverständnisse der Philosophen* (1790–94) and *Ueber das Fundament des philosophischen Wissens* (1791).

### 2.1 The Diagnosis of Conflict

Reinhold's starting point is an observation about philosophy's history that he treats as philosophically significant rather than merely sociologically unfortunate: philosophers have always disagreed. The dogmatists and the skeptics, the rationalists and the empiricists, the materialists and the idealists — none of these disputes has reached resolution, and none shows signs of doing so. For Reinhold, this persistence of disagreement is not a contingent feature of philosophy's history attributable to the limitations of individual thinkers. It is a structural feature that reveals something about how philosophy has hitherto been practised.

The structural diagnosis is this: every philosophical system attempts to derive its conclusions from a starting point that it takes to be self-evident but that its opponents contest. Since each system grounds itself in a different and contestable first assumption, none can claim the assent of all rational interlocutors; hence, the *Streit* continues indefinitely. What philosophy needs, and what it has always lacked, is a first principle so fundamental and self-evident that no rational person could dispute it — a principle whose acceptance is presupposed by any act of philosophical inquiry whatsoever (Reinhold 1791; Reinhold 1790).

This diagnosis makes the history of philosophy not merely a chronicle but an argument. The recurrence of systematic conflict is evidence for a philosophical claim: that a universally acceptable first principle has not yet been found. The correct history of philosophy is one that can explain why previous systems failed — and the explanation is always the same, namely the absence of a true *Fundamentalsatz*. Posesorski (2010) has argued that this diagnostic function of philosophical history is what distinguishes Reinhold's approach from both his Bruckerian predecessors and his Kantian contemporaries: it makes the history of philosophy an internal component of philosophical method rather than an external propaedeutic to it.

The implication is a model of philosophical progress quite different from Tennemann's teleological progressivism. For Tennemann, progress is measured by approximation to the critical standpoint. For Reinhold, progress — such as it is — is measured by the accumulation of failed attempts to find a true first principle, each failure sharpening the diagnosis of what is lacking. The history of philosophy is, on this view, philosophy's repeated experiment with its own foundations, an experiment whose results converge not on truth but on the specification of the conditions that truth must satisfy (Ameriks 2006; Beiser 1987).

### 2.2 The First-Principle Solution

The *Elementarphilosophie* presents itself as the solution to the problem the *Streit* diagnosis has posed. The principle of consciousness — the *Satz des Bewusstseins* — states that "in consciousness, representation is distinguished by the subject from both subject and object, and is related to both" (Reinhold 1789). This principle, Reinhold argues, is one that any philosopher, regardless of prior commitments, must accept: it is a description of what every act of representing involves, and its denial would undercut the possibility of any philosophical inquiry whatsoever. It is thus offered as a genuinely neutral starting point — neutral as between rationalism and empiricism, as between idealism and realism — from which the contested results of the critical philosophy can be derived in a non-circular way.

The historiographical significance of this move is reflexive: if the *Satz des Bewusstseins* succeeds, then philosophy has for the first time a starting point that removes the conditions under which the *Streit* arises. History can be read, retrospectively, as the sequence of approximations to this principle — each previous system capturing some aspect of consciousness while failing to articulate the full structure. The correct philosophical history is thus not an empirical narrative but a rational reconstruction that can be performed only from the standpoint of the achieved principle. This is the point at which Reinhold's historiography anticipates the Hegelian gesture of reading the history of philosophy from its culmination, though Reinhold's culmination is a formal principle rather than an absolute concept (Ahlers 2005).

Reinhold was aware that this self-certification creates a problem. Any philosopher who disputes the *Satz des Bewusstseins* can claim that Reinhold's own system is simply one more contestant in the *Streit*. His response — developed across the *Beyträge* volumes — is to argue that opponents who reject the principle of consciousness are inevitably committed to it in practice: they must represent their own arguments to themselves, and this representing already instantiates the structure the *Satz* articulates (Reinhold 1790; 1794). This appeal to a performative self-refutation of the *Satz*'s denial is the most philosophically ambitious element of his position, and it is precisely the element that Fichte, Schelling, and later Hegel will each contest and transform.

The first-principle solution also carries a specific conception of what philosophical agreement would look like. It is not the consensus of an educated elite, nor the convergence of a scientific tradition over time, but the immediate and universal recognition of a proposition whose denial is self-undermining. This is a high bar, and the failure to clear it — as Reinhold came to recognize through his engagements with critics — was what drove the subsequent phases of his career (Imhof 2020; Bondeli 2020). The *Streit der Systeme* thesis survived these phases, however, even when the specific first principle changed. What persisted across Reinhold's transitions was the conviction that philosophy's history is intelligible only as the history of its own foundational failures, and that its task is to find a starting point adequate to ending that history.

The internal logic of the *Streit der Systeme* thesis is thus more resilient than any of its specific instantiations. Goh (2014) has noted that Reinhold's ambition to transform philosophy into a science — to give it the methodological security of mathematics — is the constant thread running through his apparently discontinuous career. What changes across the career phases is not the aspiration but the identification of where the foundational gap lies: in the representation-theory of the *Versuch*, in the logical ground of the Bardili period, in the terminological confusions of the philological phase. The *Streit* diagnosis adapts, but it does not disappear.

## Section 3: Reinhold's Career Evolution: Elementarphilosophie, Bardili, Philology

Reinhold's philosophical career is conventionally divided into three phases: the *Elementarphilosophie* period (approximately 1787–1797), centred on the *Satz des Bewusstseins* and the *Versuch einer neuen Theorie des menschlichen Vorstellungsvermögens*; the Rationalist or Bardili period (approximately 1799–1803), marked by his adoption of Christoph Gottfried Bardili's logical approach to first principles; and the late philological period, in which Reinhold increasingly turned to questions of language and terminological clarification as the key to resolving philosophical disputes. The question of how these phases relate — whether they represent fundamental discontinuities or developmental variations on a persistent underlying project — is not merely a biographical question but a directly historiographical one, since the answer determines how one reads the *Streit der Systeme* thesis across the career as a whole.

### 3.1 The Elementarphilosophie Phase

The *Elementarphilosophie* was simultaneously Reinhold's most systematic achievement and his most contested. The *Versuch einer neuen Theorie des menschlichen Vorstellungsvermögens* (1789) presented the principle of consciousness as the universal foundation from which the truth of Kant's critical results could be derived without presupposing them (Reinhold 1789). The *Beyträge* volumes extended this program in two directions: into the theory of cognition and practical philosophy in volume one, and into more detailed replies to critics and elaborations of the theory of representation in volume two (Reinhold 1790; 1794). The *Ueber das Fundament des philosophischen Wissens* (1791) provided the clearest and most direct statement of what Reinhold took the first-principle requirement to demand and why the principle of consciousness satisfied it (Reinhold 1791).

The reception of the *Elementarphilosophie* was mixed from the start. Reinhold had secured enormous public influence through the *Briefe über die Kantische Philosophie* (1790–92), which introduced Kant's philosophy to a wide educated readership in a vivid epistolary form (Reinhold 2005; Bondeli 2015). This popularizing success was not matched by philosophical acceptance of the *Versuch*'s systematic ambitions. Critics from Maimon to Schulze argued that the principle of consciousness was either question-begging or insufficient as a foundation; Fichte argued that it was derivable from a still more fundamental principle. Marx (2011) has shown that Reinhold's Enlightenment commitments — his conviction that philosophy must be publicly accessible and practically relevant — shaped the very form of his presentation in ways that complicated its reception as a strictly systematic achievement.

Bondeli (2015) has argued that the *Elementarphilosophie* period should be read as an attempt to mediate between Kant's transcendental idealism and the demands of a philosophically educated public, and that this mediating function was itself a historiographical role: Reinhold was not simply explaining Kant but situating him within a narrative of philosophy's progress toward a universally communicable science. Posesorski (2010) has reinforced this point by showing that the systematic history of the early *Elementarphilosophie* — how Reinhold understood his own project's place in philosophy's development — is internally complex and cannot be reduced to a simple claim about completing Kant.

### 3.2 The Bardili Turn and Rational Realism

Around 1799, Reinhold publicly endorsed Christoph Gottfried Bardili's *Grundriss der ersten Logik*, which proposed to ground philosophy not in a principle of consciousness but in pure logical thinking (*reines Denken*) conceived as the self-application of thought to itself. This endorsement was widely received as an abandonment of Kantian transcendental idealism and a reversion to a pre-critical rationalism — a judgment that persisted in the literature for most of the twentieth century (Baum 1973).

Recent scholarship has substantially complicated this reading. Bondeli (2018) argues that Reinhold's "rational realism" — his name for the position that results from combining Bardili's logical starting point with his own prior commitments — is better understood as a development of the *Elementarphilosophie* project than as a repudiation of it. The problem Reinhold had identified in the *Elementarphilosophie* was precisely that the principle of consciousness, though universal in its application, was difficult to secure against the charge of psychologism: it described the structure of conscious representation but did not explain why representation itself should be philosophically fundamental rather than derivative. Bardili's pure thinking offered a way to ground representation in something that was not itself a fact of consciousness.

Imhof (2020) interprets the transition differently, arguing that the "weak side of critical philosophy" that Reinhold identified in Kant — the absence of a fully self-grounding account of reason — is what drove him to Bardili. On this reading, the Bardili turn is continuous with the *Streit* diagnosis in its motivation but represents a genuine shift in the proposed solution. The persistence of the *Streit* within post-Kantian philosophy — the fact that Fichte, Schelling, and Jacobi disagreed as radically as any pre-critical thinkers — confirmed for Reinhold that the critical philosophy had not genuinely resolved the foundational problem. Bardili's logical starting point appeared to offer a more secure footing.

The historiographical implication of this debate is direct: if the Bardili turn involves genuine continuity with the *Streit der Systeme* framework, then Reinhold's career has a coherent underlying logic that transcends its apparent breaks. If it involves fundamental discontinuity, then the *Streit* diagnosis belongs specifically to the *Elementarphilosophie* phase and does not survive it. The balance of recent scholarship — Bondeli, Imhof, Frketich (2016) — inclines toward the continuity reading, though with important qualifications about what exactly persists.

### 3.3 The Philological Turn

Reinhold's late work, culminating in the *Beyträge zur leichteren Übersicht des Zustandes der Philosophie beim Anfange des 19. Jahrhunderts* (1801–06) and in subsequent writings, increasingly emphasized the role of language and terminological clarification in philosophical disputes (Reinhold 1801). The thesis that emerges in these texts is that a significant portion of what appears as substantive philosophical disagreement is in fact a product of terminological equivocation: philosophers use the same words for different concepts and different words for the same concept, generating apparent conflicts that would dissolve under careful synonymical analysis.

This philological turn has often been read as a retreat from systematic ambition — an admission that the foundationalist project had failed and that only the more modest goal of conceptual clarification remained achievable. Fabbianelli's work on the contemporary reception of the *Elementarphilosophie* provides important context here, showing that Reinhold's critics consistently pressed the question of whether his terminological apparatus was genuinely neutral or already committed to contested philosophical positions (Fabbianelli 2003). The philological turn can be read as Reinhold's belated attempt to address this criticism by making the linguistic conditions of philosophical argument themselves the object of philosophical inquiry.

A more sympathetic reading, suggested by the broader pattern of his career, is that the philological turn is a deepening of the *Streit* diagnosis rather than an abandonment of it. If the history of philosophy is driven by conflicts among systems, and if those conflicts are at least partly constituted by terminological confusion, then clarifying philosophical language is a necessary step toward the resolution of the *Streit* — not a substitute for systematic philosophy but its prerequisite. On this reading, Reinhold's late work extends his historiographical thesis into the domain of philosophical semantics, where the *Streit* finds one of its most persistent sources (Bondeli 2023).

The three phases of Reinhold's career thus constitute not a sequence of failures but a progressive specification of where the foundational problem lies: in representation-theory, in logic, in language. The *Streit der Systeme* thesis provides the consistent motivating framework, while the specific diagnosis of philosophy's foundational deficit shifts with each phase. Whether this constitutes genuine continuity or a series of evasions remains the central question in Reinhold scholarship.

## Section 4: Contemporary Scholarly Debates

The revival of scholarly interest in Reinhold since the late 1990s has produced a set of distinct and partially competing interpretive frameworks. Three positions are particularly significant for the question of Reinhold's historiography: the Ameriks framework, which situates Reinhold within a broader account of post-Kantian foundationalism; the Bondeli developmental reading, which reconstructs the continuities across Reinhold's phases; and the more recent work of Frketich, Imhof, and Posesorski, which foregrounds the historiographical contribution as such.

### 4.1 Ameriks on Post-Kantian Historiography

Karl Ameriks's work on Kant and the post-Kantians has provided the dominant Anglophone framework for understanding Reinhold's place in the history of philosophy. In *Kant and the Historical Turn* (2006), Ameriks argues that the proper way to understand post-Kantian philosophy is neither to read it as a series of failed attempts at what Hegel eventually achieved nor to read it as anticipating contemporary concerns, but to situate each figure within the specific historical situation — the "historical turn" — that the reception of Kant's critical philosophy created.

Within this framework, Reinhold's significance lies in his role as the first systematic interpreter of Kant who attempted to provide the critical philosophy with a foundational grounding it had not provided itself. Ameriks (2009) reads the *Elementarphilosophie* project as simultaneously a philosophical contribution and a symptom: it reveals that post-Kantian thinkers felt that Kant's results required a more secure foundation than Kant himself had offered, but it also shows, through its rapid succession of failures, that the foundationalist aspiration was itself problematic. The *Streit der Systeme* thesis, on this reading, is more a diagnostic tool for understanding post-Kantian anxiety than a positive philosophical contribution in its own right.

This reading has the virtue of integrating Reinhold's historiographical claims with his systematic failures: both express the same underlying tension between the aspiration to a philosophically secure foundation and the impossibility of achieving one without circularity. Its limitation, as subsequent critics have pointed out, is that it makes Reinhold's historiography philosophically derivative — intelligible primarily as an expression of post-Kantian anxieties rather than as an independent intellectual achievement. Sekundant (2024) has similarly noted that reducing Reinhold's historical project to its symptomatic function understates the degree to which his analysis of philosophical conflict has structural content that could survive the failure of the *Elementarphilosophie* itself.

### 4.2 Bondeli and the Continuity Thesis

Martin Bondeli has produced the most sustained and philosophically detailed reconstruction of Reinhold's development. His work across several articles and book chapters argues for a fundamental continuity underlying the apparent transitions of Reinhold's career. On Bondeli's reading, the thread running from the *Elementarphilosophie* through the Bardili period to the philological turn is the persistent problem of finding a philosophical starting point that is genuinely non-circular and universally acceptable — precisely the problem that the *Streit der Systeme* diagnosis poses (Bondeli 2015; Bondeli 2020; Bondeli 2018).

The continuity thesis has several important implications for Reinhold's historiography. First, it suggests that the *Streit der Systeme* framework is not a feature of a particular phase but a standing commitment that structures Reinhold's philosophical thinking throughout. Second, it implies that each of Reinhold's phase-transitions should be read as a response to internal difficulties in the previous phase's solution — difficulties that were themselves generated by the stricter requirements of the *Streit* diagnosis. The move to Bardili, on this reading, was not an abandonment of the principle-of-consciousness approach but a recognition that the principle of consciousness was not sufficiently fundamental to undercut the *Streit* at its root.

Bondeli's reading has been particularly valuable in reconstructing the German-language reception of Reinhold, which remains less accessible to Anglophone readers. Fabbianelli's work on the contemporary reviews of the *Elementarphilosophie* provides important complementary evidence for the internal dynamics of this reception: the critics who challenged the principle of consciousness consistently did so on grounds related to the foundationalist requirements that Reinhold himself had identified, confirming that the *Streit der Systeme* thesis set the terms within which the debate was conducted (Fabbianelli 2003).

The principal challenge to the continuity thesis comes from scholars who see the Bardili turn as representing a more radical shift than Bondeli acknowledges. Frketich (2016) has argued that Reinhold's embrace of Bardili involves a reconception of what a first principle must be — from a principle of consciousness grounded in the structure of representation to a purely logical principle grounded in the self-application of thought — that is not simply a refinement within a continuous project but a change in the understanding of philosophy's subject matter.

### 4.3 Frketich, Imhof, and Posesorski: Historiography as Such

The most recent wave of Reinhold scholarship has moved toward treating his philosophy of the history of philosophy as a distinct and recoverable contribution rather than as an appendix to his systematic programs. Frketich (2016), Imhof (2020), and Posesorski (2010) converge on the claim that the *Streit der Systeme* thesis has systematic content that is separable from any particular instantiation of the foundationalist program it motivates.

Posesorski's reconstruction of the "systematic history of the early *Elementarphilosophie*" makes the strongest version of this claim (Posesorski 2010). His argument is that Reinhold's account of philosophy's history is not simply a narrative backdrop to the *Elementarphilosophie* but a sophisticated historiographical theory that specifies the criteria a successful philosophy must satisfy, explains why previous philosophies failed to satisfy them, and situates the *Elementarphilosophie* within the resulting framework. This theory has internal structure and makes substantive claims that are neither trivially true nor obviously dependent on the specific principle Reinhold endorsed.

Imhof's work on the "weak side of critical philosophy" contributes to this picture by showing that Reinhold's transitions across career phases are themselves driven by historiographical reasoning: each phase is abandoned when Reinhold concludes that its proposed solution to the *Streit* fails to meet the conditions that the diagnosis requires (Imhof 2020). This means that the *Streit* framework is not only the motivation for Reinhold's systematic projects but the criterion by which he evaluates and eventually abandons them. The history of his philosophy is itself an instantiation of the philosophical-historical pattern he was trying to understand.

Frketich's examination of Reinhold's engagement with intellectual intuition and the scholastic dimension of the *Elementarphilosophie* provides a further perspective on this historiographical distinctiveness (Frketich 2016; Frketich 2023). By situating Reinhold's systematic choices within specific historiographical options — between Scholastic and critical models of philosophical science — Frketich shows that Reinhold's philosophical historiography was not a neutral background but an active determinant of his systematic decisions. The question of what kind of philosophy can end the *Streit* depends on the question of what kind of philosophy the tradition has so far failed to produce.

The significance of this line of scholarship is that it positions Reinhold's historiographical contribution as philosophically autonomous: it can be evaluated and reconstructed independently of whether the *Elementarphilosophie*, the Bardili system, or the philological program is considered philosophically viable. The *Streit der Systeme* thesis is, on this reading, one of the first sustained theories of what the history of philosophy is for — why it matters philosophically rather than merely historically — and recovering it changes how one reads not only Reinhold but the entire arc from Kant to Hegel.

The fault lines in contemporary scholarship can thus be mapped as follows. Against the Ameriks reading, which makes Reinhold's historiography symptomatic, the recent scholarship argues for its systematic autonomy. Against the Bondeli continuity thesis, Frketich and Imhof introduce nuances about what persists and what changes across the career phases. And underlying both debates is a deeper question about the relationship between philosophical historiography and philosophical system — whether a theory of the history of philosophy can stand independently of the system it was designed to vindicate, and whether Reinhold's answer to that question has anything to teach a tradition that has largely moved on from foundationalism.

## Section 5: Reinhold's Afterlife: Fichte, Schelling, the Romantics, and Hegel

Reinhold's influence on subsequent post-Kantian thought has generally been assessed through his role as a systematic foil: the thinker whose foundationalist program each major successor refuted and transcended on the way to a more sophisticated position. This assessment is not false, but it is incomplete. What Reinhold transmitted to his successors was not only a systematic program to be superseded but a historiographical framework — the idea that the history of philosophy is constituted by systematic conflict and oriented toward a resolution — that shaped the form of post-Kantian philosophical self-understanding across its major variants. The *Streit der Systeme* thesis, transformed and in some cases inverted, runs through Fichte's account of competing standpoints, Schelling's successive system-programs, the early Romantics' philosophy of philosophical history, and Hegel's mature dialectical historiography.

### 5.1 Fichte's Reception

Fichte's relationship to Reinhold is characteristically double: he acknowledged Reinhold as the thinker who had most clearly identified the problem of philosophical first principles, and he argued that Reinhold's solution to that problem was fundamentally inadequate. The key text is the essay "Concerning the Concept of the *Wissenschaftslehre*" (1794), in which Fichte proposes to derive philosophy from the self-positing of the I — a principle he argues is more fundamental than Reinhold's *Satz des Bewusstseins* precisely because it explains why there must be a principle of consciousness at all (Fichte 2021).

The historiographical dimension of this move is often overlooked. By arguing that the *Wissenschaftslehre* supersedes the *Elementarphilosophie* as the true first-principle philosophy, Fichte implicitly endorses Reinhold's historiographical framework: the history of philosophy is the history of successive attempts to find a genuine starting point, and the *Wissenschaftslehre* is the next and — Fichte implies — more adequate candidate for that position. Martin (2007) has reconstructed the dynamic of this inheritance in his analysis of Fichte's response to Hegel's *Differenzschrift*, showing how Fichte's commitment to the first-principle framework persisted even in the face of Hegelian critique. Bondeli (2020) situates Fichte's response to Reinhold within the broader pattern of Reinhold's philosophical transitions, arguing that the *Wissenschaftslehre*'s self-positing I was both the most philosophically sophisticated response to the problems Reinhold had identified and the source of the new *Streit* it generated among Reinhold, Schelling, and the Romantics.

What Fichte retained from Reinhold, then, was not the specific content of the first principle but the structural logic of the historiographical argument: the history of philosophy is intelligible as a sequence of insufficient foundations, and a new philosophy is justified precisely by its claim to have found the foundation the tradition lacked. This logic is itself a Reinholdian inheritance that Fichte did not subject to critical scrutiny.

### 5.2 Schelling and the Early Romantics

Schelling's relationship to the Reinhold-Fichte problematic is more complex and more ambivalent. His early *System of Transcendental Idealism* (1800) accepts the first-principle framework while attempting to complete it by supplementing Fichte's subjective idealism with a philosophy of nature. His *On the History of Modern Philosophy* (1994) — the late Munich and Berlin lectures — provides his most direct philosophical-historical assessment, tracing the development of modern philosophy from Descartes through Kant and the post-Kantians to his own positive philosophy. In these lectures, Schelling reads the history of modern philosophy as a progressive working-out of the relationship between freedom and nature, system and existence — a narrative that both inherits and transforms the Reinholdian idea that philosophical history is driven by systematic conflict toward a resolution (Schelling 1994).

The early Romantics — Friedrich Schlegel, Novalis, and Schleiermacher — offer a more radical response to the *Streit der Systeme* problem. Where Reinhold, Fichte, and Schelling each proposed to terminate the *Streit* through a definitive first principle, the early Romantics argued that the aspiration to terminal resolution was itself the problem. Nassar (2013) has argued that the Romantic conception of the Absolute — infinite and inexhaustible — was developed precisely in response to the post-Kantian foundationalist tradition: if the Absolute cannot be fully grasped in any finite system, then the history of philosophy is not a series of insufficient approximations to an achievable terminus but an open-ended dialogue among finite perspectives on an inexhaustible whole. Frank (2012) traces the philosophical genealogy of this position through Schlegel's and Novalis's engagement with Fichte's *Wissenschaftslehre*, showing how their rejection of philosophical systematicity was motivated by specifically philosophical arguments about the limits of self-grounding reason.

Schlegel's early writings on the history of philosophy — developed in the 1790s lectures and fragments — are particularly relevant to the *Streit der Systeme* problematic. Schlegel explicitly engages with the question of whether philosophy's history is progressive or cyclical, and his answer — that philosophical conflict is permanent because philosophical truth is accessible only in fragments, through what he called "intellectual intuition" in a radically non-Fichtean sense — amounts to a direct inversion of Reinhold's thesis. The *Streit* is not a symptom of foundational failure; it is the appropriate mode of philosophical inquiry for beings who cannot achieve systematic closure (Millan 2012; Görner 1986). Schelling's later confrontation with Hegel over the limits of conceptual philosophy can be read as a delayed version of this Romantic response: Hegel's dialectical resolution of the *Streit* is, from Schelling's perspective, a philosophical hubris that the history of philosophy's actual complexity should have chastened (Ng 2024).

The Fichte-Schelling break of 1800–1802 is itself a vivid instantiation of the post-Reinholdian *Streit*: two thinkers who had shared a common departure point — the primacy of the first principle over empirical contingency — found themselves constructing incompatible systems in response to the same questions, and the exchange of letters that accompanied the break (Fichte and Schelling 2012) is a document of the *Streit* as a live philosophical confrontation rather than a retrospective historical diagnosis. Kreines (2024) has reconstructed the philosophical stakes of this confrontation, showing how the post-Kantian demand for systematic completeness — itself a Reinholdian inheritance — generated the conditions for a new and no less radical *Streit* than the one Reinhold had diagnosed.

The Romantic response to this situation was to embrace the *Streit* rather than to resolve it — to treat philosophical dialogue, fragmentary expression, and irony as the appropriate forms for a philosophy that has abandoned the foundationalist aspiration (Beiser 1992; Beiser 2002). This embrace was not a capitulation to scepticism but a positive philosophical position: the inexhaustibility of the Absolute is itself a philosophical claim, and the history of philosophy's persistent conflict is evidence for it rather than against it. In this way, the Romantics appropriated Reinhold's historiographical diagnosis while radically reversing his proposed solution.

### 5.3 Hegel's Dialectical Historiography

Hegel's relationship to Reinhold's historiographical thesis is the most philosophically complex, and the most historically consequential. The *Lectures on the History of Philosophy*, in their various transcripts and editions (Hegel 1892; Hegel 2006), present the history of philosophy as the progressive self-development of the Concept — the becoming-explicit of what reason implicitly contains — through a sequence of philosophical systems that each represent a determinate but partial expression of the whole. The driving motor of this sequence is internal to reason itself: each philosophical position generates its own negation through the internal tensions of its standpoint, and the history of philosophy is the exhibition of this dialectical movement.

The connection to Reinhold's *Streit der Systeme* thesis is apparent but asymmetrical. Both Reinhold and Hegel treat the history of philosophy as structured by systematic conflict, and both treat that conflict as not merely episodic but philosophically necessary. Both read the history of philosophy from a terminal standpoint that retrospectively makes the preceding sequence intelligible. Both treat philosophical historiography as internal to philosophical method rather than external to it.

The crucial difference is in the status of the conflict. For Reinhold, the *Streit* is a symptom of foundational failure — it would not occur in a philosophy that had secured a true first principle. For Hegel, the conflict among philosophical systems is an expression of reason's own internal structure: it is not a sign that previous philosophers lacked a first principle but that the Absolute itself unfolds through determinate negations (Hegel 1807; Forster 1989). The history of philosophy is not the history of insufficient foundations but the history of reason's self-realization through opposition. Motroshilova (2020) has argued that this is the decisive point at which Hegel's system incorporates and supersedes the Reinholdian framework: the *Streit* becomes internal to the dialectical movement rather than a problem to be resolved before genuine philosophy can begin.

Kalatzis (2025) has recently argued that Hegel's *Lectures* embody a "metaphilosophical" framework that goes further than any of his post-Kantian predecessors in making the history of philosophy philosophically constitutive. For Hegel, to understand what philosophy is, one must understand what the history of philosophy is — and the history of philosophy is not simply the empirical record of philosophical opinions but the self-exhibition of reason in its temporal dimension. De Boer (2017) traces how Hegel applies this framework to the modern philosophical tradition specifically, showing how his treatment of Reinhold, Fichte, and Schelling in the *Lectures* positions each as a necessary but partial expression of the developing concept.

The Hegelian appropriation of Reinhold's historiography is thus simultaneously a fulfilment and a dissolution. Hegel preserves the structural insight — philosophical conflict is constitutive of philosophy's history and intelligible from its culmination — while radically transforming its philosophical basis. The *Streit der Systeme* is no longer a diagnostic description of philosophy's historical situation but an internal feature of reason's dialectical self-movement. Ahlers (2005) has mapped this transition through a comparative analysis of Reinhold's and Hegel's respective accounts of the philosophical first principle, showing how Hegel's critique of foundationalism is at the same time a transformation of the foundationalist problem rather than its abandonment.

What the Hegelian absorption obscures, and what recent scholarship has begun to recover, is the distinctive character of Reinhold's position prior to its dialectical transformation. Volf (2021) traces how the Kantian and Hegelian "a priori" models of philosophical history dominated nineteenth-century historiography, effectively marginalizing alternative models — including Reinhold's — that did not fit either the transcendental or the dialectical mould. Recovering Reinhold's version requires reading against the grain of the Hegelian success narrative that his historiographical insights helped to make possible.

## Conclusion

The foregoing review has traced a philosophical-historical arc from the eighteenth-century tradition of *Historia Philosophica* through Reinhold's *Streit der Systeme* thesis to the post-Kantian and Hegelian transformations of that thesis. The arc is not one of simple progress — each step involves genuine philosophical loss as well as gain — but it is coherent: the history of philosophy became a philosophical problem in its own right at a specific and reconstructible moment, and the shape of that problem was substantially determined by Reinhold's contribution.

Three findings warrant particular emphasis. First, the *Streit der Systeme* thesis is more philosophically substantive than the transitional-figure narrative suggests. By locating the cause of philosophy's persistent disagreements in the absence of a genuinely universal first principle, Reinhold does not merely redescribe what Brucker had already catalogued. He offers a structural explanation of philosophical conflict that carries normative implications: it specifies what a successful philosophy would have to achieve, and it makes the history of previous failures philosophically informative rather than merely anecdotally instructive. This is a genuine contribution to the philosophy of philosophical history, not simply an expression of foundationalist anxiety.

Second, the question of continuity and rupture across Reinhold's career phases remains open and philosophically significant. The balance of recent scholarship — Bondeli, Frketich, Imhof — inclines toward a continuity reading in which the *Streit* framework persists while the specific diagnosis of philosophy's foundational deficit shifts. This reading is more plausible than the discontinuity account, but it is incomplete without a clearer account of what exactly the continuity consists in. If the *Streit* framework is sufficiently abstract to accommodate the transitions from representation-theory to logical grounding to terminological clarification, then a further question arises: what would falsify the thesis? A fully adequate account of the continuity reading must specify this.

Third, the relationship between Reinhold's historiography and its post-Kantian transformations is not one of simple supersession. Fichte appropriates the first-principle logic while arguing for a deeper starting point. The early Romantics invert the thesis's direction by embracing permanent conflict rather than seeking resolution. Hegel incorporates the structural insight — conflict as constitutive of philosophical history — while radically transforming its ontological basis. None of these transformations is straightforwardly a refutation of Reinhold; each is a philosophical development that could not have occurred without his initial articulation of the problem. The post-Kantian historiography of philosophy is not a departure from Reinhold's insight but its elaboration under conditions of increasing philosophical sophistication.

The unresolved questions that remain are correspondingly significant. Whether Reinhold's historiography is separable from his foundationalism — whether the *Streit* diagnosis could survive a principled abandonment of the first-principle solution — is a question that the recent scholarship of Frketich, Imhof, and Posesorski has opened but not closed. Whether the Hegelian dialectical appropriation of the *Streit* framework is continuous with Reinhold's version or involves a fundamental philosophical transformation is a question that requires more fine-grained comparative analysis than has yet been provided. And whether the Romantic counter-model — permanent *Streit* as the appropriate form of philosophical inquiry — offers a philosophically defensible alternative or merely an evasion of the foundationalist challenge is a question whose contemporary relevance extends well beyond the history of German Idealism.

What Reinhold's case shows, above all, is that the historicization of philosophy — the turn toward the history of philosophy as itself a philosophical datum rather than a mere propaedeutic — was not simply a feature of the Hegelian maturity of German Idealism. It was already present, in a specific and recoverable form, in the *Elementarphilosophie* and its motivating framework. Recovering that form requires reading Reinhold not as the predecessor of something better but as the articulator of a philosophical problem whose consequences are still being worked out.
