## Introduction

Kant's political philosophy is most systematically presented in the *Doctrine of Right*, the first part of the 1797 *Metaphysics of Morals*, and supplemented by a series of shorter essays on history, cosmopolitanism, and perpetual peace. Read as a field, this body of work centers on a single foundational claim: that human beings possess one innate right, the right to freedom understood as independence from being constrained by another's choice, and that realizing and securing this freedom requires a categorically (not merely prudentially) grounded transition from a rightless state of nature to a civil condition under public law. Ripstein (2009) reconstructs this architecture as a freestanding legal-political philosophy in which rightful coercion is not a limitation on freedom but its condition, since hindering a wrongful hindrance to another's choice leaves overall freedom undiminished. Byrd and Hruschka (2009) complement this systematic reconstruction with a section-by-section commentary that situates Kant's arguments within the German natural-law tradition of Achenwall and Baumgarten, tracing the logical structure of Kant's derivation of the innate right, the postulate of practical reason regarding external objects, and the passage from provisional to peremptory possession in the civil state.

Running beneath nearly every subsequent theme in this field is a foundational interpretive fault line concerning the methodological status of Right itself: whether the *Doctrine of Right* and its Universal Principle of Right stand independently of Kant's ethics, or whether Right remains continuous with, and ultimately derived from, the categorical imperative. Willaschek (2009) argues for methodological independence, contending that the analytic connection between right and coercion cannot be secured by the categorical imperative alone and instead requires the innate right to freedom as an independent argumentative resource. Seel (2009), replying directly in the same journal issue, resists this "independence thesis" on textual and systematic grounds, arguing that the Universal Principle of Right's claim to unconditional, a priori validity depends on grounding in the categorical imperative. This dispute recurs, in varying guises, across debates on the innate right's content, the justification of coercion, the status of citizenship, and the relation between right and virtue.

This survey proceeds in nine parts, moving from foundational concepts (innate right, the original contract, the Universal Principle of Right) through property and the state of nature, republican government and citizenship, resistance and revolution, punishment, cosmopolitan and international right, the philosophy of history, the systematic relation between right and virtue, and finally the reception of Kant's political thought in liberal, feminist, and race-critical scholarship (cf. Kleingeld 2012). Each section identifies live debates and unresolved tensions rather than a single settled reading, consistent with the field's own lack of consensus on Right's foundations.

## Section 1: Foundations: Innate Right, the Original Contract, and the Universal Principle of Right

Kant's *Doctrine of Right* opens from a single postulate: there is exactly one innate right, "freedom (independence from being constrained by another's choice), insofar as it can coexist with the freedom of every other in accordance with a universal law" (6:237). Two questions organize the secondary literature built on this sentence: what this innate right contains and whether it alone can ground the system of right Kant derives from it; and how the resulting Universal Principle of Right relates to the categorical imperative.

On content and sufficiency, Ripstein (2009) reconstructs the innate right as a formal relation of independence — being "one's own master" — on which rightful coercion is the condition of freedom rather than its limitation. Faggion (2015) reads independence more narrowly as freedom from fraud and violence, tying the innate right's status as belonging to every person "by virtue of his humanity" to the Formula of Humanity rather than severing right from ethics. Sangiovanni (2012) argues against Ripstein that the distinction between subjecting another's will and merely affecting the context of another's choice cannot bear the weight the Kantian framework requires, so that additional normative premises beyond formal independence are needed. Flikschuh (2021) revises positions associated with her own earlier work, arguing the standard "free choice" reading conflates Kantian external freedom with liberal negative freedom and rests on foundationalist assumptions at odds with Kant's critical method; she proposes instead that innate right conditionally affirms subjects' capacity for legal accountability and so depends on, rather than grounds, acquired right.

On derivation, Willaschek (2009) argues an analytic connection between right and coercion blocks deriving the Universal Principle of Right from the categorical imperative, making Right methodologically independent of Kant's ethics. Seel (2009), replying in the same volume, insists the Principle's unconditional validity must be grounded in the categorical imperative. Tomassini (2023) stakes intermediate ground: the categorical imperative is necessary but not sufficient, since the principle of right arises from applying the imperative to the concept of right specifically. Baiasu (2016) surveys the "Independentist" solution and concludes it captures something correct but cannot stand without qualification, while Pallikkathayil (2010) inverts the direction altogether, arguing political considerations about coercion help explain and refine the Formula of Humanity rather than the reverse.

This dispute sits within a denser textual context. Byrd and Hruschka (2009) read the *Rechtslehre* as continuous with eighteenth-century German natural jurisprudence rather than a standalone application of the categorical imperative. Hruschka (2004) isolates the "permissive law of practical reason," licensing provisional possession prior to the civil condition; Durão (2024) traces Kant's own vacillation between analytic and synthetic-a-priori formulations of the principle of right, resolved by introducing the united will of the people as the judgment converting empirical into rightful possession — linking this debate to the original contract. Pinheiro Walla (2014) argues, against Byrd and Hruschka's "presumption of badness" reading, that Kant's justification of coercive entry into the civil condition retains an empirical, human-nature-involving component that formal reconstructions suppress. McKean (2019) presses the formalist, choice-based conception of coercion toward its practical costs, arguing it structurally resembles Hayekian and Friedmanite justifications of market inequality by failing to register severe social and economic pressure as unjust coercion.

Neither the innate right's sufficiency nor its relation to the categorical imperative commands consensus. Both questions recur throughout the survey, resurfacing directly in Section VIII's treatment of Right's place within Kant's practical philosophy as a whole.

## Section 2: Property, the State of Nature, and Private/Public Right

The *Doctrine of Right* moves from the innate right to freedom discussed in Section I toward an account of how persons can rightfully claim external objects — property, contracts, and persons "akin to things" — before any public authority exists to adjudicate such claims. Kant's postulate of practical reason holds that a unilateral act of acquisition binds others only provisionally, pending an omnilateral will capable of rendering the claim peremptory. Three debates organize the secondary literature on this transitional architecture: how strictly "provisional" such claims are, whether leaving the state of nature is obligatory as a matter of right, and how domestic right fits within private right.

On provisionality, Hasan (2018) rejects both the "weak" reading, on which state-of-nature claims are already rightful but merely insecure, and the "strong" reading, on which making them wrongs others, proposing instead "anticipatory provisionality": such claims wrong no one but anticipate a condition in which their authority can no longer be unilaterally fixed. Messina extends the question in two directions: against the "standard view" confining provisionality to the state of nature and the moment of transition, he argues textually that acquired rights — including sovereignty and territory — remain provisional even within a civil condition (Messina 2019), and he separately defends the postulate against the charge that it begs the question in favor of private ownership, arguing its actual ambition is only to establish that *someone* must be capable of dominion over objects, leaving the specific property regime to be settled historically rather than a priori (Messina 2020). Tomassini (2023) supplies a comparative anchor for the unilateral/omnilateral distinction, showing that Kant's source Achenwall grounded ownership in an act of the private will, whereas Kant insisted the relevant act must be one of the general will, possible only within a rightfully constituted state.

A second debate concerns whether leaving the state of nature is obligatory as a matter of right rather than prudence. Varden (2008) argues that civil society is constitutive of rightful relations, since justice is conceptually impossible without a shared, omnilateral authority; she extends this architecturally by arguing that public right does not merely protect an antecedently complete system of private right but "concludes" it (Varden 2010), and revisits the thesis in light of subsequent objections while defending a qualified, two-layered account (Varden 2024). Hodgson (2010) independently reaches a structurally similar, freedom-based conclusion specifically with respect to property: the state is necessary not because it promotes security but because persons living side by side need it to be free at all. This near-consensus is contested by Slenzok and Trzcionka (2025), who argue that Kant's notion of provisional ownership is logically incoherent and so cannot itself generate a duty to submit to the state; turning to the innate right to freedom instead, they contend public law constrains freedom more than private law does, yielding, if anything, a prima facie duty to *stay*. This dissent sharpens the stakes of the Locke/Kant contrast traced by Baynes (1989), who shows Kant abandoning an early labor-theory commitment in favor of grounding consent in the obligation to institutionalize provisional property, and by Angell (2019), who frames the broader divide as Lockean property as natural and pre-political versus Kantian property as a legal convention nonexistent outside civil society — terms first set, on property specifically, by Shell's (1978) early reading.

A third debate concerns domestic right — the "right to a person akin to a right to a thing," covering marriage and household relations — within this architecture. Wilson (2004) treats the marriage right as a genuine interpretive problem, arguing it can be reconciled with the freedom-based foundations of the innate right despite initial appearances. Varden (2012) extends the private/public architecture to family law, arguing household relations require public, enforceable structures rather than private moral sentiment. This category has become a resource for theorizing labor: Pascoe (2024) argues modern employment resembles domestic right more closely than contract right, since its hierarchical structure better captures the dependency underlying nominally "free" contract work. Williams (2024), responding directly, agrees employment resembles domestic right but argues Pascoe's typology underplays the tension with Kantian innate equality by neglecting professional work and employment's hierarchical legal form, calling for stronger formal counterbalancing.

Across these debates, the transitional architecture proves less settled than its formal presentation suggests, and Slenzok and Trzcionka's dissent unsettles what otherwise looks like a settled "duty to exit" consensus — a tension that bears directly on the state-of-nature arguments examined in Section III.

## Section 3: Republicanism, the Rechtsstaat, and the Critique of Despotism

Kant's theory of the state turns on a republican constitution defined not by who rules but by how legislative and executive functions relate to one another, and this structural criterion generates a contested question: does Kant's republicanism point toward, or away from, democracy? Caranti (2023) argues that Kant's warning against confusing the republican constitution with the democratic one targets a structural flaw — the fusion of legislative and executive will in "the people" — rather than merely the scale or directness of popular participation, making the objection to democracy apply even to representative forms resembling contemporary ones. Harel (2022), responding to Cordelli's Kantian defense of democratic legitimacy, similarly treats the connection between Kantian legitimacy and democratic authorization as contingent: democratic decision-making is on his reading neither necessary nor sufficient, and can even be detrimental to, legitimate rule. Against this skeptical pole, Shell (2025) argues that the mature *Metaphysics of Morals* shifts from the hypothetical consent of Kant's mid-1780s writings toward the people's actual consent, yielding an internally coherent, if "organic," argument for representative democracy. Hanisch (2016) takes a mediating route: reconstructing Kant's typology of sovereignty and government, he grants that Kant's own remarks fail to articulate a satisfying procedural conception of democratic legitimacy, yet argues that the innate right of humanity — independence, equality, and rightful honor — nonetheless makes democratic procedures a non-optional feature of a genuinely Kantian republic.

This structural conception of republicanism depends on the separation of powers, which Guyer (2012) contrasts with Hobbes's unitary sovereignty: where Hobbes locates all authority in a single will, Kant insists on necessarily divided legislative, executive, and judicial powers, even while denying any right of rebellion. Weinrib (2019) extends this into contemporary constitutional theory, arguing that Kant's account generates an ongoing public duty to maintain and refine liberal democratic constitutional orders rather than treating the Rechtsstaat as a static achievement.

The active/passive citizenship distinction sharpens the despotism question by asking whether Kant's exclusions are principled or merely convenient. Vrousalis (2022) reads civil self-sufficiency as "interdependent independence," explaining — without endorsing — the disenfranchisement of women, wage laborers, and landless farmers as a function of a republic of interdependent commodity producers, and positions this against republican, libertarian, and proprietarian rivals. Moran (2021) instead finds, through close attention to Kant's traveling-blacksmith examples, that some exclusions track pragmatic concerns about itinerancy rather than a consistent political principle, while Davies (2023) relocates the criterion from economic dependence to freedom from private authority, framing the distinction as anti-corruption rather than purely economic. Widmer (2025) and Gregory (2023) push the debate toward neo-republican appropriation: Widmer argues the distinction's underlying aim — securing non-arbitrary law — is continuous with contemporary non-domination theory even where its historical application is not, and Gregory assesses whether the Kantian state can avoid both state and majoritarian domination. Marey (2018) supplies conceptual ground for this cluster, reading popular sovereignty as an a priori "omnilateral will" rather than an empirical procedure, distancing Kant from both Hobbes and Rousseau. Commentators thus divide over whether Kant's exclusions are principled, ad hoc, or salvageable through reconstruction — a division that resurfaces in Section IX's reception debates.

## Section 4: Resistance, Revolution, and the Limits of Political Obligation

Kant's *Doctrine of Right* holds that subjects have no right to resist, still less to overthrow, the sovereign, even a tyrannical one — a position that sits uneasily beside Kant's own enthusiasm for the events of 1789. This apparent tension between Kant's juridical prohibition and his broader moral and historical commitments structures the secondary literature on resistance.

Korsgaard (1997) offers a revisionist reading, arguing that Kant's own moral premises — particularly the publicity requirement and the prohibition on maxims that could not be willed as universal law — generate resources for a qualified, morally constrained permission to resist a sufficiently unjust sovereign that Kant himself did not draw out. Waldron (1996) defends the strict-prohibition reading on different grounds, characterizing Kant's position as a form of legal positivism: the authority of positive law derives not from its moral content but from its status as the actual, effective embodiment of a public, general will capable of resolving disputes, so that any existing legal order is preferable, as a matter of Right, to contested claims about where true sovereignty lies.

Maliks develops two mediating positions between these poles. In an earlier article, Maliks (2013) argues that while no right of resistance exists within a genuine Kantian state, an extremely tyrannical regime can fail to qualify as a "state" at all in the relevant juridical sense, leaving citizens with a moral responsibility to judge whether a given regime meets the threshold conditions of rightful authority — a reading Maliks presents as compatible with, rather than a revision of, Kantian authority. In a later, historically grounded study, Maliks (2022) reconciles Kant's stated enthusiasm for 1789 with his continued rejection of a right of revolution by distinguishing the lawful transfer of sovereignty to the people in the Estates-General from the lawless trial and execution of the monarch in 1792–93, arguing that Kant judged the first to manifest popular sovereignty rightfully and the second to pervert that same principle.

Hill (2017) takes a more exploratory approach, posing a series of critical questions about whether Kant's several arguments against a right to resist — from the concept of sovereignty, from the social contract as an idea of reason, and from the dangers of anarchy — are individually sufficient or mutually consistent, without settling on either a strict or a revisionist verdict. Arntzen (1996) probes a related textual resource, asking whether duties to oneself, particularly the duty to preserve one's own moral integrity, might license limited non-compliance short of a juridical right to rebel, even granting that Kant's conceptual argument against a right of resistance is otherwise sound. Golob (2021) addresses a distinct but related puzzle: how Kant can treat the French Revolution as a historical "sign" of moral progress while condemning the revolutionary act itself. Golob argues that the evidential work is done by the enthusiastic reaction of disinterested spectators rather than by the revolutionaries' own conduct, so that moral endorsement of the revolution's significance and juridical condemnation of the act are not merely compatible but structurally linked. Ypi (2014) extends the comparative frame beyond Kant, arguing that Kant and Marx share a structural commitment to revolution's indispensable role within a philosophy of history oriented toward freedom's realization, differing chiefly over whether that role licenses individual revolutionary agency. Curtis-Wendlandt (2012) situates Kant's position within its immediate intellectual context, showing that the correspondent Elise Reimarus independently developed a structurally parallel rejection of violent resistance paired with an appeal to public expression as the legitimate channel for political grievance.

Across this literature, no single reading resolves the tension between Kant's categorical prohibition and his historical sympathies; strict, threshold, and reconciliationist positions instead offer competing accounts of where — in the concept of sovereignty, in the structure of the state, or in the distinction between lawful and lawless popular action — the apparent inconsistency is best located.

## Section 5: Retributivism and Kant's Theory of Punishment

Kant's remarks on punishment in the *Metaphysics of Morals* — the jus talionis, the "island" case of a dissolving society still obligated to execute its last murderer, and the defense of capital punishment against Beccaria — have long been read as the paradigm statement of retributivism in the Western canon. Byrd (1989) reoriented this consensus by distinguishing the *threat* of punishment, a general legislative measure addressed to would-be offenders and justified by deterrence, from the *execution* of punishment on a convicted individual, which alone must satisfy strict retributive equality. This threat/execution distinction reframed subsequent scholarship around "mixed theory" readings that combine deterrence and retribution rather than treating Kant as a pure retributivist. Hill (2000) extends this defense, arguing that apparent textual tensions dissolve once the question of who may be punished (guilt as a necessary condition) is separated from the question of why the state punishes at all, which can appeal to social utility. Brooks (2003, 2005) proposes an alternative axis for the same mixed-theory conclusion, distinguishing punishment for transgressions of the moral law (retributive) from punishment for violations of positive law (consequentialist), and defends this distinction against Clark's (2004) contrary proposal that a coherent Kantian theory should abandon retribution altogether, appealing instead to what hypothetical rational contractors, on Rawlsian or Scanlonian lines, would endorse.

The mixed-theory consensus is not unanimous. Shell (1997) defends a retributivist reading against revisionist interpretations, arguing that Kant's insistence on equality and the jus talionis as the sole legitimate measure of punishment expresses a considered commitment rather than an inconsistency requiring explanation via deterrence. Pickering (2020) renews this challenge, arguing the textual evidence for a consequentialist side of the hybrid reading is weak and that evidence for a retributivist side of any genuine hybrid is largely absent. Merle (2000) offers a distinct internal critique, contending that both the pure-retributivist and mixed-theory readings are textually defensible yet that Kant's theory of punishment, on either reading, is inconsistent with his own concept of law; Merle proposes that a genuinely Kantian justification should instead appeal to special deterrence and rehabilitation. Holtman (1997) pursues a related reformist strategy, reinterpreting Kant's penal theory through civic respect and situating the law of retribution as an empirically contingent component of an otherwise ideal theory of justice that can be revised in light of social and economic circumstance. Vilhauer (2017) affirms that Kant's mature theory is genuinely retributivist but argues that resources internal to Kant's first-Critique account of imputation support a non-retributive "ideal abolitionist" alternative built on original-position reasoning. Vilhauer's (2023) later taxonomy of five distinct Kantian standpoints on deserved retribution — ranging from "theoretical zero" to "practical infinity" to the "practical equality" invoked in public-law punishment — suggests Kant's texts support multiple, imperfectly reconciled positions rather than a single unified theory, with Vilhauer arguing that Kant's own doctrine of strict right in fact entails a fifth, unacknowledged standpoint of "practical zero."

Capital punishment sharpens these interpretive stakes. Yost (2010) reconsiders Kant's reply to Beccaria's social-contract objection — that no one would consent in advance to their own execution — probing whether Kant's distinction between the rational, contracting self and the empirical, criminal self can bear the justificatory weight he places on it. Ataner (2006) identifies a further tension: Kant's anti-suicide argument grounds a duty to preserve one's own rational nature, raising the question of whether this commitment is consistent with endorsing state-imposed death as the uniquely proportionate response to murder. Corlett (2001) situates these Kant-specific disputes within a broader retributivist framework concerned with desert, proportionality, and the state's authority to inflict suffering, providing comparative terms against which Kant's version of retributivism can be assessed. Taken together, no single interpretation of Kant's penal theory commands consensus: the mixed-theory reading inaugurated by Byrd remains the most frequently cited departure point, but it continues to be contested from both a renewed pure-retributivist direction and from reformist and abolitionist directions that draw on other parts of Kant's practical philosophy.

## Section 6: Cosmopolitan Right, International Right, and Perpetual Peace

Kant's threefold division of public right in "Toward Perpetual Peace" and the "Doctrine of Right" — the right of a state, the right of nations, and cosmopolitan right — has generated sustained disagreement over each of its three levels: the institutional form owed to interstate relations, the precise content of the hospitality right that governs relations between persons and foreign states, and the doctrine's bearing on Kant's own remarks on colonialism and race.

The first debate concerns whether a non-coercive league of states or a coercive world republic represents Kant's genuine institutional recommendation. Kleingeld (2004, 2012) argues that Kant advocates the pacific federation not because he abandons the rational ideal of a world republic, but because he judges a world state practically unrealizable; the league thus functions as a deliberate "negative surrogate" that approximates the ideal's function without its coercive apparatus. Caranti (2022) contests this developmental reading, arguing that the league constitutes Kant's considered and philosophically defensible position in its own right, since national sovereignty is, on Caranti's reconstruction, indissolubly linked to a state's full autonomy over the use of military power — any coercive supranational authority would erode the very sovereignty peace is meant to secure. Höffe (2006), by contrast, defends a "maximalist" reading on which only a minimal, subsidiary world republic can institutionalize the "right to right" that Kant's threefold system of public right ultimately requires, placing his interpretation in tension with both Kleingeld and Caranti.

A second debate concerns the content of the hospitality right itself. Ganesh (2018) argues that Kant's bracketed use of "Wirtbarkeit" (innkeeping) alongside "Gastfreundschaft" tracks a substantive legal analogy to Roman innkeeping law, grounding cosmopolitan right strictly in the innate right to freedom and yielding a narrow right to temporary commerce and presence rather than settlement. Benhabib (2004) treats this narrowness as a normative gap between the "rights of man" and the "rights of the citizen" that contemporary cosmopolitan theory must close through "democratic iterations" extending toward migrants and refugees. Valdez (2011) argues that Kant's own account of colonial injury and the original common possession of the earth licenses a more expansive reading of hospitality once historical injustice is taken into account. Wolthuis (2025) tests a contemporary candidate against Kant's narrow definition directly, concluding that EU free-movement law exceeds cosmopolitan right in Kant's technical sense, since it grants a stronger status of residence and integration than the peaceable-treatment right Kant specifies.

A third strand concerns colonialism and race. Kleingeld (2014) documents a substantive reversal in Kant's position: his forceful condemnation of European colonialism in the 1790s tracks an abandoned commitment to racial hierarchy that had earlier underwritten his acceptance of colonial practice, evidence for a developmental rather than continuous reading of Kant's cosmopolitanism.

Finally, the doctrine's reception in international relations theory constitutes a distinct controversy. Doyle (1986) founds the "democratic peace" research program on Kant's three definitive articles, treating them as an empirical as well as normative theory of interstate peace. Simpson (2018) argues this tradition substituted commerce and trade for Kant's third article on hospitality, producing a drift from Kant's original triad. Tesón (1992) and Stone Sweet and Ryan (2018) extend Kantian international right toward, respectively, intervention doctrine grounded in the republicanism requirement and an institutionalist account of EU constitutionalism as a partial realization of the cosmopolitan constitution.

## Section 7: History, Progress, and Teleology in Kant's Political Thought

Kant's political essays on universal history and perpetual peace supplement the *Doctrine of Right*'s a priori architecture with an account of how the rightful condition might actually come about, driven by "unsocial sociability" and secured, Kant suggests, by a teleological "guarantee" of nature. Commentators disagree about the epistemic status of this apparatus: is it a regulative idea licensed practically, or does it carry theoretical or even metaphysical weight? Kleingeld (2001) resists collapsing this question into a clean either/or, arguing that Kant's fluctuating use of "nature" and "providence" in the historical essays reflects a philosophy of history that operates simultaneously at the theoretical and practical levels rather than belonging exclusively to one. Flikschuh (2007) and Allison (2009) instead read the teleological argument of "Idea for a Universal History" as subordinate to, and constrained by, the critical epistemology developed elsewhere in Kant's system, treating the essay's regulative Ideas as continuous with the first *Critique*'s account of reason rather than an ad hoc excursion.

The mechanism said to drive this progress, unsocial sociability, is examined by Schneewind (2009), who situates Kant's "good out of evil" account within the broader tradition of unintended-consequences arguments exemplified by Adam Smith, asking how much explanatory weight a naturalistic mechanism operating independently of moral motivation can bear. Honneth (2007) sharpens the normative stakes by distinguishing "system-conforming" from "system-bursting" versions of the progress thesis, defending the latter—which treats history as a conflict-ridden, illusion-free learning process—as the only version retaining contemporary philosophical meaning.

Nature's "guarantee" of peace generates further disagreement over its compatibility with moral freedom. Ypi (2010) critically probes whether the providential development invoked in the "Guarantee" section of *Toward Perpetual Peace* can be justified on Kant's own critical terms, while Ertl (2020) defends a more robust reading on which the interplay of human and divine rational agency is compatible with, rather than opposed to, human freedom. Cureton (2018) and Papish (2024) reframe this guarantee/hope relationship in moral-psychological terms, asking what a virtuous agent may reasonably hope for; Papish further traces a revision within Kant's own corpus, arguing that his 1797 essay "An Old Question Raised Again" remedies problems present in earlier formulations, complicating any treatment of "Kant's view" as a single fixed doctrine.

Ostaric (2023) connects this domain to the third *Critique*, arguing that aesthetic and teleological judgment together exhibit the highest good as if obtaining in nature, thereby unifying the philosophy of history with Kant's broader critical system—a systematic ambition also reflected in the edited volume of Formosa, Goldman, and Patrone (2014) on politics and teleology in Kant. Huseyinzadegan (2019) draws the widest methodological conclusion, arguing that the historical writings constitute a distinct "nonideal theory" of politics, governed by the regulative principle of purposiveness rather than the deductive a priori method of the *Doctrine of Right*. Across this literature, the regulative reading of historical progress predominates but remains contested, particularly regarding whether nature's guarantee can be reconciled with the moral agency Kant elsewhere makes central to right.

## Section 8: Right, Virtue, and Autonomy: Political Philosophy within Kant's Practical Philosophy

This section functions as connective tissue rather than a self-contained topic. It revisits, from a different angle, the independence-thesis debate introduced in Section I — this time framed not as a question about the content of the innate right but about the systematic place of the Doctrine of Right within Kant's practical philosophy as a whole. The available bibliography does not support extensive independent treatment of this angle: nearly every dedicated contribution to the "systematic place" question — a direct rejoinder to Willaschek, a stronger architectonic claim that the Doctrine of Right may not belong within the *Metaphysics of Morals* as Kant conceived it, a treatment of the paradox of self-legislating autonomy, and grounding monographs on Kantian ethics — lacks a resolved abstract and is therefore excluded from synthesis under the project's citation rules. What follows is accordingly a brief reprise rather than a new debate.

The reprised cluster from Section I already stakes out the relevant positions: Willaschek (2009) argues that rightful coercion cannot be derived from the categorical imperative and so requires independent juridical grounding; Seel (2009) responds that the Universal Principle of Right's unconditional validity must ultimately rest on the categorical imperative; Baiasu (2016) qualifies the independence thesis without fully rejecting it, arguing Right's relation to ethics is more complex than either strict derivation or strict independence allows; Tomassini (2023) proposes a third way on which the categorical imperative is necessary but not sufficient to generate the principle of right; and Pallikkathayil (2010) inverts the direction of derivation entirely, treating political considerations about coercion as explanatorily prior to aspects of the Formula of Humanity.

Zhang (2017) adds a complementary argument to this cluster, one built from case analysis rather than architectonic reconstruction. Examining two instances of Kantian criminal adjudication (linking back to Section V's punishment debates), Zhang argues that Right and Virtue belong to "incommensurable realms of freedom and necessity," so that juridical decision-making is properly insulated from independent ethical considerations. This is a qualified, case-grounded version of independence rather than Willaschek's fuller architectonic claim. Weighed against it is the derivation-friendly current running through Section III's citizenship literature and Section VI's cosmopolitan-right literature, both of which treat external freedom as continuous with, rather than severed from, Kant's broader account of rational agency. The systematic relation between Right and Virtue thus remains as unresolved from this angle as it was in Section I.

## Section 9: Reception and Controversies

A field survey would ordinarily close by asking whether Kant's political philosophy is, on balance, a liberal-republican achievement or one harboring authoritarian, exclusionary, and hierarchical commitments. The bibliography assembled for this domain presents an unusual obstacle to that task: nearly every dedicated reception source — foundational liberal-republican recoveries, edited collections establishing the field's critical range, and the central texts of the race- and gender-critical debates — lacks a resolved abstract and is excluded under this project's evidentiary standards. This section is therefore built, as the outline anticipates, primarily from material already introduced in Sections I, III, IV, and VI, read here for its bearing on reception specifically. The result is narrower than the controversy itself warrants; the gap reflects a limitation of the assembled evidence base, not an absence of relevant scholarship.

One directly reception-oriented paper is citable. Basevich (2022) extends Charles Mills's category of moral personhood into an explicitly intersectional critique, arguing that moral personhood is necessary but insufficient for a reparative critique of Kant's theory of justice, since it does not by itself capture the structural domination of racialized and gendered labor; civic equality, on this view, requires the state to dismantle race- and gender-based labor relations rather than treat them as incidental. This connects to Section III's active/passive citizenship debate: against Vrousalis's (2022) structural "interdependent independence" account of Kant's disenfranchisement of women, wage laborers, and landless farmers, and Moran's (2021) and Davies's (2023) more pragmatic and authority-relational qualifications, Basevich presses whether any such reading — including Widmer's (2025) neo-republican reconstruction of the exclusion's rationale — adequately registers the racial and economic structure of the domination involved.

A second axis concerns cosmopolitanism and race. Kleingeld (2014, Section VI) documents a mid-1790s reversal in which Kant's condemnation of colonialism tracks an abandoned racial hierarchy, positioning the corpus's egalitarian cosmopolitanism as a late achievement rather than a stable feature of the whole — a finding that bears on, without settling, how far structural critiques of Kant's earlier raciology can be answered by developmental readings.

A third axis extends the authoritarian-strand question into political economy. McKean (2019, Section I) argues that Kant's formal, choice-based conception of coercion — recognizing only direct interference with choice as unjust, never structural economic power — leaves the doctrine of right resembling Hayekian and Friedmanite justifications of inequality, complicating contemporary appropriations of Kant as an egalitarian alternative to neoliberalism.

The remaining two axes are themselves live instances of the liberal-versus-authoritarian controversy rather than external commentary on it. Maliks's threshold (2013) and contextual-historical (2022) readings of the anti-resistance doctrine bear on whether that doctrine is conservative or qualifiedly liberal-republican. And Section III's democracy debate is constitutive of this section's question directly: Caranti (2023) and Harel (2022) read Kant's hostility to democracy as structural and principled, while Shell (2025) and Hanisch (2016) find in Kant's mature texts, or in his own premises regarding the innate right of humanity, grounds for a proto- or non-optionally democratic reading. No consensus unifies these threads; the available literature converges on describing Kant's legacy as internally contested, though its thinness here means this section cannot adjudicate the fuller feminist- and race-critical scholarship a comprehensive treatment would require.

## Conclusion

A single methodological fault line recurs, in different guises, across this survey. One pole treats Kant's political philosophy as grounded in a formal relation of independence that requires no supplementation from empirical or additional normative premises: Ripstein's (2009) reconstruction of innate right as rightful independence, Willaschek's (2009) independence thesis separating right from the categorical imperative, and — in Section III — the reading of active citizenship as a function of formally specified "interdependent independence" (Vrousalis 2022). The other pole holds that formal freedom alone cannot bear the explanatory or justificatory weight placed on it, and must be supplemented by further resources: Sangiovanni's (2012) argument that additional normative premises are needed to ground the system of right, Flikschuh's (2021) revision of her own earlier formalism, Pinheiro Walla's (2014) insistence on an irreducible empirical component in the justification of coercive entry into the civil condition, and McKean's (2019) demonstration that a purely formal conception of coercion cannot register structural economic domination.

This same tension reappears, transposed, in nearly every subsequent domain. Section III's dispute over whether Kant's republicanism is proto-democratic (Shell 2025; Hanisch 2016) or structurally opposed to democratic authorization (Caranti 2023; Harel 2022) turns on whether formal non-domination exhausts legitimate rule or requires actual popular consent. Section V's mixed-theory reading of punishment (Byrd 1989 and successors) against renewed pure-retributivist challenges (Shell 1997; Pickering 2020) asks whether retributive equality alone, or equality supplemented by deterrence and social purpose, best explains Kant's penal texts. Section VI's dispute between the pacific federation as Kant's genuine position (Caranti 2022), as a negative surrogate for an unrealizable world republic (Kleingeld 2004, 2012), and as a station toward a maximalist world republic (Höffe 2006) reprises the question of how much institutional supplementation formal sovereignty tolerates. And Section VII's disagreement over whether historical progress is a strictly regulative idea (Flikschuh 2007; Allison 2009) or resists that clean division (Kleingeld 2001), with its guarantee defended (Ertl 2020) or critically questioned (Ypi 2010), asks whether Kant's formal architecture needs — or can bear — a teleological supplement at all.

Section IX's reception debates show that this same fault line carries normative stakes beyond exegesis: whether Kant's formalism yields a defensible egalitarian core or obscures structural domination along race, gender, and class lines (Basevich 2022; McKean 2019), and whether the anti-resistance doctrine's formalism is conservative or qualifiedly liberal (Maliks 2013, 2022). Given the reception literature's evidentiary thinness in the assembled bibliography relative to the exegetical literature, this is the area most urgently requiring further critical attention. This review is offered as an orientation map to these live, unresolved debates rather than an adjudication of any one of them.
