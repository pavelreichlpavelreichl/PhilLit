## Introduction

Kant's political philosophy is most systematically presented in the *Doctrine of Right*, the first part of the 1797 *Metaphysics of Morals*, and supplemented by a series of shorter essays on history, cosmopolitanism, and perpetual peace. Read as a field, this body of work centers on a single foundational claim: that human beings possess one innate right, the right to freedom understood as independence from being constrained by another's choice, and that realizing and securing this freedom requires a categorically (not merely prudentially) grounded transition from a rightless state of nature to a civil condition under public law. Ripstein (2009) reconstructs this architecture as a freestanding legal-political philosophy in which rightful coercion is not a limitation on freedom but its condition, since hindering a wrongful hindrance to another's choice leaves overall freedom undiminished. Byrd and Hruschka (2009) complement this systematic reconstruction with a section-by-section commentary that situates Kant's arguments within the German natural-law tradition of Achenwall and Baumgarten, tracing the logical structure of Kant's derivation of the innate right, the postulate of practical reason regarding external objects, and the passage from provisional to peremptory possession in the civil state.

Running beneath nearly every subsequent theme in this field is a foundational interpretive fault line concerning the methodological status of Right itself: whether the *Doctrine of Right* and its Universal Principle of Right stand independently of Kant's ethics, or whether Right remains continuous with, and ultimately derived from, the categorical imperative. Willaschek (2009) argues for methodological independence, contending that the analytic connection between right and coercion cannot be secured by the categorical imperative alone and instead requires the innate right to freedom as an independent argumentative resource. Seel (2009), replying directly in the same journal issue, resists this "independence thesis" on textual and systematic grounds, arguing that the Universal Principle of Right's claim to unconditional, a priori validity depends on grounding in the categorical imperative. This dispute recurs, in varying guises, across debates on the innate right's content, the justification of coercion, the status of citizenship, and the relation between right and virtue.

This survey proceeds in nine parts, moving from foundational concepts (innate right, the original contract, the Universal Principle of Right) through property and the state of nature, republican government and citizenship, resistance and revolution, punishment, cosmopolitan and international right, the philosophy of history, the systematic relation between right and virtue, and finally the reception of Kant's political thought in liberal, feminist, and race-critical scholarship (cf. Kleingeld 2012). Each section identifies live debates and unresolved tensions rather than a single settled reading, consistent with the field's own lack of consensus on Right's foundations.
