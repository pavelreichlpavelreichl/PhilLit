## Section 3: Negativity, Totality and the Self-Critique of Reason: First-Generation Critical Theory

### 3.1 From reification to critical theory

Antonio (1981) treats the passage from Hegel and Marx to the Frankfurt School as continuous. The first generation does change what immanent critique is directed at. Marx's object is a historically specific economic form. The first generation takes as its object a whole social order, and eventually reason itself. Gindner (2023) shows the first step in Lukács's *History and Class Consciousness*. She argues that its central concept is totality rather than class or reification, and that totality should be understood "in a strictly negative sense", as a feature of capitalist coercion to be overcome. In Lukács the standpoint of the totality supplies the standard for criticising capitalist society, and the proletariat is the transformative subject. Chari (2010) contrasts this structural, practice-oriented critique of reification with Honneth's recognition-theoretic reformulation. She argues that the reformulation sacrifices the link Lukács forged between capitalist domination and the spectatorial stance of human beings toward the world. The contrast anticipates the opposition between transformative and reconstructive models in Section 4.

Horkheimer's distinction between traditional and critical theory gives the programme its self-description. Brincat (2016) reads it as a claim that no theory of society lacks political motivations, and sets it against Cox's later problem-solving/critical divide. The comparison turns on three points: the epistemological relation between critical and traditional theory, the emphasis on transformation and historical process, and intersubjectivity. Reynolds (2023) finds the same structure in the Popper-Adorno positivism dispute. He describes a conflict between critique as a practice of social rationality (Popper) and critique of social rationality itself (Adorno), and reads both as dimensions of a reflexive practice of immanent critique.

### 3.2 Adorno: immanent critique, non-identity, constellation

Adorno's work is where the relation between negativity and immanent critique is most disputed. O'Connor (2004) reads the negative dialectic as the theoretical foundation of the critical rationality that critical theory requires. On his account the experience of the object's non-identity corrects concepts, giving a rational yet non-foundational account of knowledge. Stone (2014) keeps the link to Hegel. Adorno, she argues, shares Hegel's view that dialectical contradictions require reconciliation but rejects reconciliation as unification, since for Adorno reconciled differences co-exist as differences. Contradiction is retained as a sign of the non-identity of concepts and objects, while closure is dropped. Athanassiadis (2026) contests the other half of this relation. Athanassiadis accepts that the two dialectics have different aims but argues that Adorno's charge that Hegel's dialectic is identitarian misfires on several counts, so that the real question is the viability of Adorno's post-Hegelian model.

Finlayson (2014) goes further and denies that the genealogy is secure. He asks whether Adorno practises immanent criticism and whether he endorses the term as a description of his own practice, and he concludes that the Continuity Thesis should be rejected. If he is right, the label applied to this tradition (including Antonio's) is a retrospective construction. The work that adopts it is therefore of two kinds: work on Adorno's actual procedures, and work that uses "immanent critique" to describe them. Martinson (2014) belongs to the first kind. He treats immanent critique as a philosophical performance, so that the critique is enacted in the criticism and no theory of foundations stands behind it. Skirke (2024) reconstructs Adorno's "mediation argument" against Husserl's phenomenology, on which phenomenological description is mediated by social conditions and so reproduces the status quo. The standard in that argument is the criticised position's own claim to immediacy. Skirke also argues that the argument fails against phenomenology. Ziegelmann (2025) argues that the "standpoint of redemption" in *Minima Moralia* is a methodological self-description that does not conflict with immanent critique and can be read as "inverse utopianism" (see Section 6).

The methodological disputes extend to the Adorno-Benjamin relation. Helmling (2003) examines the tension between Adorno's constellation and Benjamin's dialectical image, and argues that it is also a tension with the programme Adorno calls immanent critique. Feldman (2011) reconstructs Adorno's charge that Benjamin is "not dialectical enough" as a dispute about autonomy, subversion and redemption. In both readings, Adorno's immanent critique depends on a mediation that, on his view, other first-generation procedures omit.

### 3.3 Marcuse, and the Dialectic of Enlightenment problem

Marcuse develops a transformative version with a positive conception of liberation. In Kellner's (1984) reconstruction, the standards are the unrealised potentials of technology and freedom in advanced industrial society. González (2018) reads the same trajectory as the repudiation of dialectics: between *Reason and Revolution* and the theory of one-dimensionality, Marcuse moves from a Hegelian-Marxist standpoint to a rejection of dialectics and class struggle. If contradiction is absorbed by the social order, the resources of immanent critique shrink. The Marcuse literature therefore divides over whether the first generation's totalising diagnosis preserves a standard or removes it.

*Dialectic of Enlightenment* (Horkheimer and Adorno 2020) poses this problem for the whole programme. It argues that enlightenment, in its drive to dominate nature, reverts to myth and domination, and it applies determinate negation to the concept of enlightenment itself. Habermas's response is the charge of performative contradiction. Finlayson (2009) quotes its formulation in *The Theory of Communicative Action*: critical theory "labored over the difficulty of giving an account of its own normative foundations". He reconstructs it as the "normative problem" of how Frankfurt School criticism can claim authority without moral foundations, and he argues that none of the immanent or externalist solutions fully resolves it. Finlayson (2003) gives a qualified defence of Habermas's verdict that Adorno's ideology critique lacks a normative standard. The charge is that a critique of reason that uses reason must either presuppose the validity claims it denies or lack grounds. (The locus is Habermas 1985, *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity*; here the charge is characterised through Finlayson, Stone and Morris.)

The replies take different routes. Stone (2014) argues that Adorno can criticise enlightenment consistently because of how he reshapes the Hegelian dialectic. Cook (2004) argues that Adorno's appeal to reason's unrealised potential avoids the charge, and that Habermas's communicative theory risks affirming existing rationalised structures. Morris (1996) accepts that the critique may embody a performative contradiction but denies that this makes it incoherent: total critique of reason need not be hopeless or aporetic, because it pursues consistency in its own radical form. On the first axis, then, defenders locate the standard in reason's unrealised possibility, in non-identity, or in the enactment of critique itself. Hammer and Rush's (2025) guidebook is a recent entry point for how the text's self-critical method is now read.

### Section summary

On the four axes, the first generation locates the standard in a historically unrealised potential or in non-identity (a), makes contradiction central but changes its meaning from class antagonism to the non-identity of concept and object (b), and is transformative in orientation, though its negativism restricts what it can say positively (c). It breaks with Hegelian progress, treating the progress of reason as turning into regress (d). Two legacies follow. One is a negativist immanent critique of a "wrong" whole. The other is Habermas's charge that such critique cannot account for its own normative basis, whose consequences Section 4 takes up.
