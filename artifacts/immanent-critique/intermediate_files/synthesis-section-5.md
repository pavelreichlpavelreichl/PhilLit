## Section 5: What Makes Critique Immanent? Taxonomy, Normative Authority, and the Conservatism Objection

### 5.1 Taxonomies and their instability

Section 4 showed that Habermas, Honneth, Jaeggi, Walzer and Celikates all claim immanence. Each draws the line between immanent, internal and external critique differently, and the literature offers at least three non-equivalent ways of drawing it. The first locates immanence in the standard. Stahl (2013a) asks of any immanent critique what the social ontology of its norms is, what epistemic access critics have to them, and what critical authority results. Within this frame, "internal" critique applies standards the audience already accepts, and "immanent" critique reconstructs norms implicit in a practice that may outrun its participants' self-understanding. The question is then where such norms can be said to exist.

The second way locates immanence in the form of the practice. Särkelä (2017) distinguishes a Frankfurt tradition, which treats immanence as "a quality of the standard employed" and so needs a prior "model of immanent critique" showing that the standard is in the object, from a tradition running from Hegel's *Phenomenology* to Dewey, in which immanence is "the form of critical practice itself". On the second view the practice transforms any model it applies, so the view is "inherently hostile" to modelling. Becker (2018) finds a related ambiguity inside Hegel's text, between judging an object by its own norms and accounting for one's own standpoint by reference to the object.

The third way lists requirements. Procyshyn (2019) compares immanent critique with other argumentative strategies and extracts three: inherence, contradiction and access. Because each requirement is "multiply interpretable", he concludes that immanent critique "is not a method in any strict sense". On this reading the instability of the taxonomy is a feature of the practice. Solinas (2019) shows that the boundary can also be contested from one side. He argues that Jaeggi's objections to Walzer's internal critique are in many respects inconsistent and indicate a "political deficit" in her neo-Hegelian methodology. If so, the line between Jaeggi's immanent and Walzer's internal critique is less secure than Jaeggi's classification suggests (Section 4.4). On the four axes, these accounts disagree about (a) the source of standards and about (b) the role of contradiction: Procyshyn makes contradiction one requirement among three, whereas Särkelä's practice-form view gives it no fixed role.

### 5.2 The dilemma: conservatism or loss of bite

Fraser (2023) argues that the prospects for immanent criticism, which "promises to be critical of the existing social order without appealing to 'external' normative standards", are "bleak". Such criticism must commit to an implausible social ontology, a flawed metanormative theory, or both. Von Samson (2025) formulates the metanormative side as a dilemma. Recent literature collapses a social-ontological and a metanormative question into a "collapse assumption": a standard acquires normative authority for participants by being suitably embedded in their practice. Critical theorists must then either grant authority to de facto governing norms, which makes critique "structurally conservative", or deny it, in which case it "loses its normative bite". Von Samson's response is to drop the assumption and locate the method's specificity in the clarification of immanent contradictions, so that "individual reasons" become secondary to social theory. Contradiction (b) is thus the answer to the authority problem, a role it lacks for Honneth (Section 4.2).

Other responses relocate immanence differently. Stahl (2017) takes particular moral experience as a resource that can undermine confidence in the concepts framing it, giving experience a negative role. Stahl (2024) identifies a further problem: in oppressive forms of life, the interpretations that partly constitute immanent norms may be distorted by the exclusion of subordinated groups. He proposes either recovering a constitutive form of freedom in all forms of life or consciously adopting a partisan stance for oppressed self-understandings. The second option makes the selection of standards an explicitly political commitment.

Critics of Jaeggi press the dilemma from within the problem-solving model. Marchettoni (2019) argues that if practices are understood as conventions, internal criticism becomes difficult. Jaeggi distinguishes practices from conventions through crises of second-order problems, but according to Marchettoni this leaves forms of life with conventional features unless one is willing to postulate something like an absolute telos. Bodde (2020) argues that the notion of immanent critique Hegel produced and Jaeggi adopts is "intrinsically organic". Where the criterion is long-term stability, capitalism and bureaucracy are therefore not practically vulnerable to immanent critique without supplement, and the article calls for a hybrid external-immanent critique. Evans (2022) goes further and declares the problem of normative foundations insoluble. By analogy with the epistemological "given", a foundation would have to need no justification and yet justify. Evans suggests that recent theory is better understood as appealing to what people actually want than to what they should rationally want.

The hermeneutic strand raises the same dilemma earlier. Raz (1991) asks whether morality is interpretive in a way physics is not, and examines Walzer's claims to that effect. Gregory (2010) argues that Walzer's connected criticism tacitly relies on a Kantian conception of the self, "in violation" of his own commitment to internalism. This is a covert-externalism charge. Agnafors (2012) defends Walzer by reading his method as a version of wide reflective equilibrium, which relocates the question of conservatism to that method (Section 5.3).

### 5.3 Analytic and realist parallels

Analytic literatures face the same structure. Williams (1981) distinguishes internal reasons, which hold only if acting would serve some element of the agent's motivational set, from external ones, which carry no such condition. The immanent critic's requirement that standards be recognisable to the criticised has the same shape. Wide reflective equilibrium, defended by Daniels (1996) as coherence among moral and non-moral beliefs, is a coherentist counterpart in which inconsistency drives revision. The role of contradiction is coherence-restoring rather than dialectical. Dworkin (2011) offers a further analogue in constructive interpretation of practices, tested by holistic moral argument. At the external pole, Cohen (2008) argues that ultimate principles are insensitive to facts about social practice, which denies the immanent critic's premise that practices can be a source of normative standards. Rossi (2015) replies that resisting the dependence of principles on facts forces theorists to choose between principles unbiased by current circumstances and principles that reliably generate political judgments. Rorty (1989; 1990) accepts the label "ethnocentrism" for a view on which standards come from one's own community's contingent vocabularies. This is the conventionalism of the dilemma's first horn, owned openly.

Political realism extends the parallel. Geuss (2008) criticises liberal theories built on abstract conceptions of rights and justice, and Prinz (2016) reads this as a radicalisation of realism that stresses the critical purpose of political theory and its diagnostic engagement with context. Cozzaglio (2020) argues that realist internalist legitimacy theories, which develop normativity "from within the political context under evaluation", still need room for external critique. Without it they risk becoming descriptive accounts of de facto acceptance of power. Clark (2024) addresses the charge of status-quo bias against realist ideology critique and argues that its defenders face a dilemma of their own: implausible epistemological assumptions, or a tempered epistemology that leaves it toothless. Section 6 treats this literature as ideology critique.

Genealogy is offered as a non-foundational alternative. Han-Pile (2016) takes up the charge of crypto-normativity levelled at Foucault by Taylor and Habermas, and concludes that the reply fitting Foucault best is to refuse the demand for normative justification as self-defeating and to treat critique as a practice of the self. Allen (2014) comments on Koopman's reading of Foucault as historical rather than transcendental critique, which Koopman sees as closer to American pragmatism and German critical theory than to some French and Italian thinkers. Santos and Sobottka (2022) treat genealogy as a variant of immanent critique, pairing it with Jaeggi's forms of life. Forst (2011) offers the contrasting external option. He grounds social criticism in the right to justification, a moral status that does not depend on any particular context. This differs from Cozzaglio's and Bodde's hybrids, which begin from the immanent side and add external elements.

### Section summary

No party has dissolved the dilemma. The live options are (i) to relocate immanence, to contradiction (von Samson), the form of critical practice (Särkelä) or experience (Stahl 2017); (ii) to accept a hybrid with external elements (Cozzaglio, Bodde, Forst); and (iii) to reject the demand for foundations (Evans, Han-Pile). Analytic internal-reasons and reflective-equilibrium work shares the structure of the problem, but it has little direct contact with the Frankfurt literature. Section 6 examines how contradiction, ideology and negativity work in practice.
