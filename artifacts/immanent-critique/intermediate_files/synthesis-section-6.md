## Section 6: Contradiction, Ideology, and Immanent Transcendence: From Ideology Critique to "Wrong Life"

### 6.1 Is ideology critique immanent critique?

Sections 4 and 5 asked where immanent standards come from. Ideology critique, which targets distorted belief rather than failed norms, raises the question whether it belongs to the same family. Sørensen (2022) denies it. He argues that for Adorno immanent critique has metaphysical implications beyond Honneth's critique of bourgeois society for inconsistency with its professed ideals. He also holds that Horkheimer and Habermas practised ideology critique rather than immanent critique. Bianchin (2019) defends the opposite view as a reading of Jaeggi. The immanent and progressive character of ideology critique lies in the link between its explanatory and normative tasks. Beliefs are ideological if they support existing practices, come from systematically distorted belief formation, and trace to structural factors shaped by the practice they support. Such beliefs fail to anchor principles of cooperation acceptable under non-domination. The standard is generated by the practice's own functional instability, so contradiction appears as a stability problem.

Analytic work disputes how far the standard can be internal at all. Morris (2016) reconstructs Marx's critique as a theory of knowledge. He argues against purely debunking accounts, which bracket the cognitive properties of beliefs, that reason can be social, historical and interested while retaining its adjudicatory capacity. Haslanger (2012) defines ideology as a stable set of shared dispositions that loop through the material world and make the social appear natural. Her ameliorative, openly political commitments are partly external to the practices she studies. In her exchange with Shelby, whose cognitivism treats racism as false beliefs serving pernicious conditions (as Haslanger 2017 characterises it), she argues that racism is a set of mutually reinforcing practices, attitudes, meanings and material conditions. This locates the object of critique in structures rather than propositions, and it implies that arguments will sometimes be the wrong tool. Stahl (2022) applies a Marxian model to ideal theory itself. He argues that Mills's and Geuss's case for its distorting content fails, and locates its ideological character in form: its concepts are presented as semantically practice-independent, which blocks challenges from subordinate groups. This returns the immanent/external dispute to the objects of critique.

Realist ideology critique pushes in the other direction. Aytac and Rossi (2022) argue that ideology critique should be genealogical and empirical, debunking beliefs by showing they are produced by self-justifying power, itself an epistemic rather than moral flaw. Sleat (2024) first criticises this internally and then argues that critique without morality is misguided. Clark (2024) argues that defenders face a dilemma: implausible epistemological assumptions, or an epistemology so tempered that critique is toothless. Clark proposes a moderate version in which discovering that a belief is likely malignantly epistemically circular triggers further epistemic testing. Rossi (2024) uses the nonmoralised critique to filter the preferences that feed legitimacy, so the ruled's values stay internal but corrected. The contrast with Stanley (2015) is instructive. Stanley argues that inequality produces ideologically flawed belief and so aids propaganda, and Rossi (2017) argues that this argument relies on a moralised conception of democracy that a more realistic one would avoid. Pearson (2025) adds that, under conditions of crisis, debunking apparently ideological beliefs can be epistemically unwarranted. The realist side thus replays the Section 5 dilemma: a move to epistemic standards avoids smuggled morality but is charged with loss of critical bite. Unlike the Frankfurt accounts, these proposals give contradiction no evident role in grounding critique.

### 6.2 Contradiction: real, performative, practical

The work above shows contradiction doing different jobs. Procyshyn (2019) treats it as one of three requirements. Von Samson (2025) makes the clarification of immanent contradictions the specificity of the method, in place of the embedding of norms in practice. The "contradictions" in question differ in kind. Fraser (2017) locates them in capitalism's background conditions (social reproduction, nature, political power), whose contradictions generate crises in a way Marxian conceptions of crisis neglected. The claim is structural, and the standard is derived from a social system's tendencies, not from participants' commitments. Habermas's performative contradiction is a different kind, attributed to the critic. Morris (1996) argues that Adorno's radical critique may embody such a contradiction without being incoherent, because it pursues consistency within its total critique. De Caux (2024) goes further and takes norms to arise from "real normative antinomies" in concepts and social reality, which is offered as an answer to Evans's scepticism (Section 5.2). A critique can therefore be immanent through the contradictions it finds in its object (Fraser, von Samson) or through those it enacts (Morris). The two uses do not obviously coincide.

### 6.3 Immanent transcendence and utopia

Immanent critique also has to say how it can point beyond what exists. Repa (2023) defends Honneth against Jaeggi's classification of his reconstruction as internal criticism lacking transformative negativity. On Repa's reading, the "surplus of validity" does the work of transcendence within historical reality, and it replaces contradiction as the source of transformative potential. Strydom (2022) reads critical theory's Young-Hegelian core as a dialectical theory of social development whose neglected key concept is possibility, with immanent-transcendence among its pivotal concepts. Both are reconstructive and developmental. Allen (2015) asks whether such a model is available once power-theoretic diagnosis shows how deeply subjection shapes agents. The explanatory-diagnostic task seems to undercut the emancipation that the anticipatory-utopian task needs. Allen proposes a negativistic conception of emancipation that turns this paradox into a productive tension. Stahl (2023) gives a reason why critical theory needs a utopian dimension. In non-ideal circumstances, theorists' epistemic, imaginative and conceptual capacities are distorted, and this pulls their ideals toward accommodation with the status quo. Here the Hegelian-Marxian line (Strydom, Repa) and the negativist line (Allen) diverge on axis (d): the first relies on developmental possibility, the second on a progress-free conception of emancipation (taken up in Section 7).

### 6.4 "Wrong life" and negativism

Adorno's wrong-life thesis is the most radical form of the question. Finlayson (2002) argues that Adorno has a normative ethics, but a minimal and negative ethics of resistance, and that the role of the ineffable explains how the good is available without conflict with negativism. Freyenhagen (2011) develops an alternative that does without the ineffable. Freyenhagen (2013) argues that the bad, in the form of suffering and the "wrong life", is more knowable than the good, so that guidance consists in living "less wrongly". The resulting negative Aristotelianism is grounded in experienced suffering and in the unfulfilled possibility of a better society, and it needs neither a positive conception of the good nor a Habermasian foundation. This answers the "normative problem" of Section 3.3 by relocating the standard to experience of the bad rather than to a reconstructed norm.

The replies press from several sides. Celikates (2017) accepts that normative claims can be made without appeal to the right, the good or human nature, but argues that the reading underestimates how far Adorno follows immanent critique and its socio-theoretical underpinnings. O'Connor (2017) argues that if we have morally relevant interests we have an implicit standard of what is good for us, so a strict negativism is detached from those interests. Reeves (2016) supports Freyenhagen's reading but argues that it is intelligible only on Aristotelian metaphysical presuppositions that should be made explicit. Freyenhagen (2017) replies to Allen, Celikates and O'Connor, insisting on a pluralist conception of the bad and on Adorno's holism. Other readings give the thesis different grounds. Whyman (2016) takes it to rest on Hegelian social holism plus an orientation that inverts it, and formulates it through the concept of despair. Skirke (2008) reads it as a claim about moral reality: moral reasons arise case by case though they are blocked overall. Ziegelmann (2025) argues that the "standpoint of redemption" is a methodological self-description that does not conflict with immanent critique. It works through estrangement, presenting the absurd as normal, and can be understood as "inverse utopianism". This links the wrong-life thesis to Allen's question about how to anticipate emancipation without a positive utopia.

### Section summary

The three families locate critical force in different places. Contradiction-centred versions place it in social antagonism or crisis (Fraser, von Samson), ideology-centred ones in distorted belief formation (Bianchin, Haslanger, the realists), and negativist ones in experienced wrongness (Freyenhagen). On the four axes, the standard is functional failure, epistemic circularity or experience of the bad (a). Contradiction is central in Fraser, von Samson and de Caux but absent in the realists (b). Repa and Strydom are reconstructive where Allen, Haslanger and Freyenhagen are transformative (c). Only the developmental accounts presuppose progress (d). Whether such force can be had without a positive standard, as Freyenhagen holds, or whether interests, metaphysics or morality must be added (O'Connor, Reeves, Sleat), remains open. Section 7 asks whether these accounts need a conception of progress.
