## Section 4: Reconstruction, Recognition, Connection, and the Methodological Turn (Habermas to Jaeggi and Stahl)

### 4.1 Habermas and Benhabib: communicative reason

Habermas's answer to the normative problem that Section 3 traced is to relocate the standard. In *The Theory of Communicative Action* (Habermas 1984) the standard is the set of validity claims that speech acts raise, reconstructed through formal pragmatics, and the pathology to be diagnosed is the colonization of the lifeworld by system imperatives. Habermas disclaims the label "immanent critique" for this project. Stahl (2013a) argues that it nonetheless is one. On his reading, Habermas holds that neither a transcendental or metaphysical solution nor a merely hermeneutic reconstruction of historically given norms can clarify the normative foundations of critical theory. The *Theory of Communicative Action* instead locates the normative demands on which critique draws "within the socially instituted practice of communicative understanding". This is what Stahl calls a "practice-based" model, as opposed to a hermeneutic one. He adds that Habermas takes too narrow a view of the social ontology of immanent normativity, so that the critique cannot develop its full potential. On the four axes, the standard is the reconstructed presuppositions of communication (a), contradiction gives way to distortion of communication (b), and the method is reconstructive (c).

Benhabib (1986) studies the foundations of critical theory and uses two moments, the "explanatory-diagnostic" and the "anticipatory-utopian". Her book is cited here only as the source of that vocabulary. Allen (2015) takes up the pair for feminist theory and finds that the diagnostic analysis of gendered power seems to undercut the possibility of emancipation on which the utopian task depends. Schmid (2017) uses the same pair to argue that the Habermasian framework in international relations has uncoupled normative critique from substantive social analysis and weakened both capacities. Kompridis (2006) objects from a different direction. He argues that a critical theory restricted to the normative clarification of procedures forfeits critique's capacity to disclose new possibilities of meaning, and he proposes "world-disclosure", drawing on Heidegger, as a possibility-enhancing alternative. For him the standard is not already available to be reconstructed but is opened up by critique, so that his position differs from Habermas's on the reconstructive/transformative axis.

### 4.2 Honneth: normative reconstruction and its critics

Honneth's *Freedom's Right* (2014) turns the reconstructive strategy toward institutions. It reconstructs the ideal of social freedom as it is embodied in personal relations, the market economy and the democratic public sphere, and it identifies where these ideals have been "misdeveloped". An earlier essay on reconstructive critique with a "genealogical proviso" (Honneth 2009) sets the methodological frame. It is characterised here through Carleheden (2020) and Repa (2023), who treat it as an attempt to supplement reconstruction of embedded norms with attention to history and power. Carleheden (2020) compares Honneth's reconstruction with construction and genealogy and treats it as a case of "normative reasoning". He concludes that the procedure is overly constructivist because it blocks the disclosure of ongoing transformations of ethical life. Zurn (2000) examines the earlier anthropological ground of Honneth's account.

Critics ask whether the method retains critical force. Schaub (2015) argues that normative reconstruction recasts pathologies as misdevelopments of existing norms, so that radical critique and normative revolution are given up, and that *Freedom's Right* has no resources to show why this is not a deficiency. Freyenhagen (2015), writing from an Adornian position, applies an immanent critique to Honneth's account, finds both the early context-transcendent approach and the later immanent one wanting, and charges increasing reformism. McNeill (2015) raises the question of justification. Honneth takes over Hegel's ethical substance without Hegel's idealist commitments, and so needs some alternative demonstration that existing institutions are rational. McNeill finds the grounds he offers insufficient. Buchwalter (2016) makes a related point by comparison with Hegel, whose approach, he argues, is "more robustly reconstructive and more robustly normative", because social rationality is worked out in the course of reconstruction and not read off institutions.

Repa (2023) defends Honneth's method as immanent critique. Against Jaeggi's classification of reconstructive critique as internal criticism "devoid of any transformative negativity", he argues that the "surplus of validity" of norms functions as a transcendence within historical reality. Contradiction, on this reading, gives way to surplus (b), and the method remains reconstructive with a transcending moment (c). Ng (2018) tests the project from outside. She assesses the theory of social freedom against Mills's Marxian ideology critique and concludes that aspects of it could serve ideological functions.

### 4.3 Fraser: dualism and crisis; Walzer: connected criticism

Fraser disputes Honneth's single grammar of recognition. In their exchange (Fraser and Honneth 2003), Fraser argues that redistribution and recognition are irreducible dimensions of justice ("perspectival dualism"), and Honneth argues that a differentiated theory of recognition can ground justice alone. Thompson (2005) assesses the exchange by comparing the accounts of capitalism, the social theories and the moral philosophies behind each position. Fraser (2017) later relocates critique in an expanded conception of capitalism, including its background conditions of social reproduction, nature and political power, whose contradictions generate crises. Contradiction thus regains the central role it loses in Honneth. O'Kane (2021) applies an immanent critique to Fraser's "systematic" account and finds its Marxian premises at odds with its Habermasian notions of capitalism, contradiction, crisis and emancipation.

Walzer's (1987) "connected critic" offers a hermeneutic alternative. The critic interprets the shared meanings of her society and criticises it for failing to live up to them. Allen (1998) finds that Rorty's and Walzer's notion of loyalty conflates recognising one's situatedness in contexts of power with affirming it, and Gregory (2010) argues that Walzer's social criticism tacitly relies on a Kantian conception of the self, contrary to his own commitment to "internalism". Agnafors (2012) reads Walzer in terms of wide reflective equilibrium. Walzer's critic assumes no discovered or invented universals, and contradiction plays a minor role, as a gap between professed meaning and practice. Forst (2011) offers a contrasting ground. He reconstructs dignity as resting on a right to justification, so that the standard is a context-transcending moral status rather than embedded norms, and critical theory becomes a critique of "relations of justification".

### 4.4 The methodological turn since 2000

Since about 2000 theorists have taken immanent critique itself as a topic. Stahl (2013a) gives a formal account built on three questions that any immanent critique must answer: the social ontology of immanent norms, the epistemic access critics have to them, and the critical authority that results. He distinguishes immanent critique from external critique and from merely internal critique, which applies standards already accepted by the audience. (The working paper Stahl 2013b and the monograph Stahl 2013c are cited only for these distinctions, as characterised in Stahl 2013a.) Stahl (2017) adds that particular moral experience can undermine confidence in the concepts that frame it, so that experience plays a negative role. Stahl (2024) asks what follows for the method when subordinated groups are excluded from the interpretations that constitute a practice's norms. Sabia (2010) defends a hermeneutic version, a second-order critique connected to criteria internal to the culture criticised, and denies that it entails relativism or limits critique.

Jaeggi (2018) puts the transformative alternative most explicitly. She rejects liberal "ethical abstinence" and treats forms of life as ensembles of practices that function as problem-solving instances. Their failures, including crises of second-order problems, supply standards that are neither external moral norms nor merely accepted internal ones, and transformations that resolve them count as learning processes. The NDPR review characterises the result as a "dialectical pragmatism". Jaeggi (2009) had earlier presented ideology critique as a form of immanent social critique, not a theory of false consciousness. Celikates (2018) moves the locus from the theorist to agents. Critical theory must not diagnose ideology from above and must not defer to participants, and it analyses the conditions that obstruct the reflexive capacities of agents. Celikates and Mattos (2012) put the point as a position between Bourdieu and Boltanski: the practices that the sociology of critique reconstructs are the basis of critical theory, but participants' perspective has no epistemic authority "immune" to theoretically informed questioning.

### Section summary

By the 2010s the field contains reconstructive (Habermas, Honneth), crisis-theoretic (Fraser, Jaeggi), hermeneutic (Walzer, Sabia) and reflexive-pragmatist (Celikates) variants, with disclosure (Kompridis) and justificatory (Forst) alternatives. All claim immanence in some sense, and they disagree about what makes a standard immanent: embedded norms, presuppositions of communication, the failures of a form of life, or the practice of agents. They also divide on contradiction (central for Fraser and Jaeggi, displaced by "surplus" in Honneth) and on whether the method is reconstructive or transformative. Section 5 takes up these disputes, and Section 7 returns to the progress question that Honneth's and Jaeggi's accounts raise.
