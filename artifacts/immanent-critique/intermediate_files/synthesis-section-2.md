## Section 2: Determinate Negation and the Critique of Political Economy: Hegel, the Young Hegelians, Marx

### 2.1 Hegel: criterion-testing and determinate negation

The conceptual inheritance of immanent critique is usually traced to the Introduction of Hegel's *Phenomenology*, where a form of consciousness is tested against the criterion that it itself supplies. Pippin (1989) reads this procedure as the continuation of Kant's critical project. On his account the self-testing of forms of consciousness, together with the categorial argument of the *Logic*, vindicates the conditions of thought without appeal to external metaphysical claims, and Hegel is presented as a critical philosopher whose disagreements with Kant remain within that project. Westphal (2015) also stresses continuity with Kant, but through a pragmatic-realist reading. He argues that Hegel's 1807 text reconstructs Kant's system of principles, which makes the critique reconstructive of the position criticised and not an attack from outside. Westphal (2009) collects further readings of the Introduction's treatment of the criterion. Sedgwick (1993) takes up the question of whether Hegel's criticisms of Kant are internal to the critical project, in a commentary on Pippin. Pinkard (1994) and Maker (1994) are the usual references for two other interpretive options, a social-historical reading and a non-foundationalist one. They are named here only to mark that the source of Hegel's standards is itself disputed.

Becker (2018) shows that the dispute begins with the definition. He distinguishes two senses of immanent critique that both appear in the *Phenomenology*: judging an object by its internal norms, and accounting for one's own standpoint by reference to the object. His reading of the "internal criteria" and "self-examination" passages is meant to clarify how the two relate. The distinction anticipates a later split between views on which the standard lies in the object and views on which the critical practice is what is immanent (taken up in Section 5). Rosen (1982) states the sceptical question that attaches to either sense. Hegel holds that the true is the whole, so a result is fully adequate only at the completion of the system. Rosen therefore asks whether the point of completion has been reached legitimately, and whether criticism that borrows its standards from the position criticised can produce a positive result without external assumptions.

On the four axes, Hegel's standards come from the object (a), contradiction does the work of moving between forms (b), and the critique is reconstructive in that it displays the development of what is tested (c). Progress (d) is built into the procedure, and the later sections ask whether it can be separated from it.

### 2.2 Young Hegelians and the early Marx

Breckman (1998) argues that the religious concerns of the Hegelians of the 1830s and the sociopolitical concerns of the 1840s were inextricably connected, and he places Feuerbach and Marx within that context. The early Marx therefore inherits the criterion-testing procedure through a political reading of Hegel. Buchwalter (1991) describes how Marx used it. Marx criticises the *Philosophy of Right* for assigning rationality dogmatically to existing reality. Unlike Haym, however, he does not blame the identification of reason and reality as such. He keeps the principle that reason and reality are homogeneous and objects to its dogmatic formulation. He calls the resulting approach immanent critique and contrasts it with the utopian criticism of Young Hegelians, Kantians and natural-law theorists, which measures reality by alien principles. Immanent critique exposes the conflict between reality and its own avowed norms. Brudney (1998) follows Marx from Feuerbach and Bauer to *The German Ideology* and finds an attempt to leave philosophy that still sets out a notion of the good life. Immanence in the early Marx may therefore sit alongside an evaluative standard that is not simply the one the object avows.

### 2.3 *Capital*'s method and the Marx-and-justice debate

The divergence becomes sharper for the critique of political economy. Postone (1993) reads Marx's mature theory as a critique of the historically specific social forms of capitalism, such as value and abstract labour, grounded in capitalism's own categories. He rejects the critique from the standpoint of labour that he ascribes to traditional Marxism. The standard is historical and specific to the form of society, and contradiction lies between those forms and their development. Ninos (2024) locates the model for this procedure in Hegel's *Logic*. He argues that Hegel's treatment of analytic and synthetic finite cognition gives the framework by which Marx marks the stages of political economy, so that Marx's immanent critique of Ricardo is modelled on Hegel's immanent critique of finite cognition. Moseley and Smith (2014) collect the positions on how far *Capital* depends on the *Logic*, from systematic dialectic and the "new dialectic" (Arthur 2002) to the neue Marx-Lektüre (Heinrich 2012). Caligaris and Starosta (2014) ask in the same debate which "rational kernel" Marx kept and which "mystical shell" he discarded. The categorial reading takes the standard to be logical and internal to the categories of capital. Others emphasise the materialist inversion of Hegel, which makes the status of the logical standard a question in its own right.

The other axis of disagreement concerns whether the standard is covertly moral. Geras (2020) surveys the controversy over Marx and justice, in which Wood (1980) and Husami (1980) took the opposed positions. He reports the case for thinking that Marx does not condemn capitalism as unjust and the case for thinking that he does. Geras himself argues that Marx condemns capitalism, for its oppressions and unfreedoms and also for its injustices, even though he is impatient with the language of norms. If so, Marx's critique rests partly on a moral standard that capitalism's own norms of justice do not supply. Whether such a critique remains immanent in Stahl's sense is a question the literature has not settled, and it returns in Section 6 in connection with Cohen.

The section's results are these. The Hegelian-Marxian line gives critique a standard internal to its object, and it makes contradiction the means by which that object is shown to be wanting. Already here, however, readers disagree on whether the standard is logical-categorial (the *Logic*, systematic dialectic), social-historical (Postone) or covertly moral (Geras). This is a division within the line of inheritance, and Finlayson's (2014) challenge to the Continuity Thesis is compatible with it.
