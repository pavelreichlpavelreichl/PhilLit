## Section 7: Progress, Coloniality, Gender, and Immanent Critique Beyond Philosophy

### 7.1 Does immanent critique need progress?

Section 6 ended with a divergence on axis (d): developmental accounts of immanent transcendence versus progress-free accounts of emancipation. The debate over that divergence was set off by Amy Allen's *The End of Progress* (2016), which this review reaches through the symposium of Leeb et al. (2018). There the contributors press on Eurocentrism, normativity and capitalism, and Allen replies. The project, as the symposium describes it, is to decolonize the normative foundations of Frankfurt School critical theory by detaching them from a developmental philosophy of history.

The dependency thesis is stated most plainly for Honneth. Kim (2022) argues that an affirmative stance toward historical progress is what lets Honneth derive the validity of the normative presuppositions of the existing social order. On this reading, immanent critique can rely on norms the social order already embodies, without transcendent or transhistorical principles, because history has vindicated them. Solinas (2025) takes up Allen's challenge and argues that the neo-Hegelian method of normative reconstruction, in Honneth and in some respects Habermas, carries a post-metaphysical *Geschichtsteleologie*. In Solinas's account this teleology makes it hard to theorise coloniality and obstructs an organic connection between critic and subaltern subjects. Agatensi (2025) approaches the same target genealogically. Agatensi traces Honneth's teleological vision from *The Critique of Power* to *Freedom's Right* and locates a "short-circuit" in the combination of an immanent reconstructivist method with a neo-Enlightenment view of modernity and a disclaimed Adornian negativity. On axis (d), then, the progress objection falls hardest on the reconstructive variants of Section 4.2.

Problem-solving models are the main respondents. Melo (2025) reconstructs Jaeggi's claim that progress is never linear or necessary but contingent, multidimensional and embedded in forms of life, arising through crises and problem-solving where gains and losses are articulated together. Wiersma (2026) argues that this formal theory sidesteps Allen's problems, because Jaeggi need not claim the normative superiority of one form of life over another. Wiersma then poses a dilemma: to say what counts as successfully solving a second-order problem, Jaeggi must commit either to objectivism or to a progressive reading of history. Ünal (2025) similarly holds that Jaeggi does not fully explain how learning processes show normative and functional progress, and proposes a richer conception of agency. Chen (2025) goes further, arguing that Jaeggi's analogy between social-moral and scientific progress fails. Appealing to Kuhnian incommensurability, Chen proposes a psychologist alternative. The direct exchange between Allen, Jaeggi and von Redecker (2016) is named here for attribution only.

Two further moves leave the opposition between progress and its rejection. Volpe (2025) reads Allen's book as continuing the self-criticism that critical theory has always practised on its own concepts, so that the decolonial critique is immanent to the tradition. Volpe nonetheless worries that "negativistic meta-normative contextualism" and the dismissal of backward-looking progress preserve little of the inheritance, and recommends discourse ethics as the framework for dialogue with the colonised. Allen (2020) and Heinze and Popp (2024) extend the argument: the developmental reading of history is paired with a developmental model of the subject, and psychoanalysis is offered as a more realistic anthropology. Cozzaglio and Favara (2022) offer a realist alternative, defining political progress through inclusivity, responsiveness and self-reflection by nonmoral, contextualist standards.

### 7.2 Decolonial and feminist challenges

Allen's challenge concerns the philosophy of history behind the method. Radhakrishnan (2025) challenges the practice of the method. Drawing on Quijano, Dussel, Lugones and Oyěwùmí, Radhakrishnan argues that immanent critics risk inattention to constitutive exclusions, impose asymmetrical burdens on certain critics, and replay a mechanism of domination familiar from colonialism. The remedy is attention to the power relations that govern the practice of critique itself and more pluralism about methods. This extends Stahl's (2024) point about oppressive forms of life (Section 5.2): the standards "within" a practice may already be shaped by exclusion. Bhambra's (2007) account of "connected histories" supplies the background to such objections, since modernity narratives that abstract Europe from colonialism provide the premises of reconstruction. Bhambra (2014) also distinguishes postcolonial from decolonial traditions, which matters because the objections above come from different ones. Vázquez-Arroyo (2017; 2018) belongs to this reception, but only titles are available here.

Constructive responses use the method's own resources. Santos and Sobottka (2022) treat genealogy as a variant of immanent critique rather than a rival, pairing Jaeggi's forms of life with Rosa's diagnosis of modernity and Saar's tridimensional genealogy as a path for decolonial studies. Melo's (2025) Brazilian case, with race as a decisive category, treats anti-racist struggles as experiential learning that confronts enduring contradictions. On feminist work, Njoya (2019) reads the *Oresteia* through feminist and Frankfurt School theory and argues that these theorists keep Hegel's progressive thesis while exposing its underside: law advances justice and intensifies domination together, because of the universality of the legal form. Progress is treated as ambivalent, not abandoned. Azmanova (2012) offers an immanent critique of neoliberal capitalism, taking shared conceptions of justice and structural sources of injustice together and arguing that women's victimisation is a symptom of dynamics that also harm supposed winners.

A terminological caution is needed. Stahl's (2020) "colonization" is Habermas's colonization of the lifeworld by system imperatives, not colonialism, and it has no bearing on the decolonial literature.

### 7.3 Uses beyond philosophy

Several disciplines theorise the method before applying it. Isaksen (2018) presents immanent critique as central to Bhaskar's critical realism and as a way to structure a literature review without foundations or a neutral standpoint, by noting theory-theory, theory-practice and theory-data inconsistencies. Curtis (2014) contrasts critical realism's "transcendental" orientation with Foucault's immanent one in organization studies, taking critical discourse analysis as the foil, so immanence here has a Foucauldian rather than a Hegelian-Marxian sense. Dhillon (2021) uses Adornian contradiction to argue that the neoliberal university, whose demand for measurable knowledge is shaped by colonial epistemic boundaries, cannot deliver decolonization. The critic can still open decolonial practices "within, and against" it. Moilanen (2025) asks whether a didactic immanent critique of capitalism can avoid indoctrination, and answers that it can through self-reflectivity, controversy and dialogic teaching.

International relations shows both modes. Baker-Beall (2022) applies a Critical Theory-inspired immanent critique to the policy term "foreign terrorist fighter". Petrova (2018) describes NGO campaigns against landmines and cluster munitions as immanent critique of military practice on the basis of humanitarian law, which restrained US policy while leaving the laws of war intact. This is the reformist pole of the method. Methodological debate in the field recapitulates Sections 3 and 4. Brincat (2016) compares Horkheimer's traditional/critical distinction with Cox's problem-solving/critical divide. Schmid (2017) argues that the Habermasian framework uncouples normative critique from social analysis, weakening its explanatory-diagnostic and anticipatory-utopian tasks, and recommends recovering totalising critique. Ralph (2025) replies that proposals to return to first-generation negativity underplay why it failed, and looks to Dewey and Honneth. Saramago (2021) translates the Allen debate: critique needs a standpoint of orientation, yet grand narratives reproduce Eurocentrism, so a post-Eurocentric historical-sociological narrative is explored. In technology, Feenberg (2023) revives Marcuse's critique, which rests on an experiential ontology, and Reeves and Sinnicks (2023) extend Adorno to workplace monitoring and algorithmic culture. Neither defines immanent critique as a method.

Other uses show terminological drift. Bozalek and Romano (2025) use "immanent" in the Deleuzian and new-materialist sense of a relational ontology, together with Barad's diffraction. Byrne (2024) argues from citation practices and interviews that critique is "immanent to practice" in international law, an empirical claim about internality to a practice, with no norm-based critique of existing arrangements. Trunov and Lesnykh (2025) name the method in the title of their study of platform ideology without defining it. Wallo et al. (2021) distinguish critique for improvement from critique for emancipation in management education, and the connection to immanent critique is not made explicit in their abstract. These papers show how far the label has travelled, and they do not add to the method.

### Section summary

The progress objection hits reconstructive versions hardest (Kim, Solinas, Agatensi). Problem-solving models respond with formal or retrospective conceptions of progress, and these responses are contested on the grounds of objectivism or hidden progressivism (Wiersma), insufficient explanation (Ünal) and a failed scientific analogy (Chen). Decolonial and feminist work asks whose context counts (a): Radhakrishnan and Stahl (2024) press on exclusion in the very practices that supply standards, while Santos and Sobottka, Njoya and Azmanova rework the method rather than leave it. On axis (c), the applications split between the transformative aims of Dhillon and the reformism of Petrova. Contradiction (b) organises the Adornian uses (Dhillon, Feenberg) and is absent from the Foucauldian one (Curtis). Outside philosophy, uses divide between theorised adaptations (Isaksen, Curtis, Dhillon) and loose labels. The Conclusion gathers these results.
