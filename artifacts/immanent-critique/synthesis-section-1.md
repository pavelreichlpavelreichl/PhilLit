## Introduction

Immanent critique is usually described as criticism that takes its standards from the object criticised rather than from a vantage point outside it. Antonio (1981) goes further and treats it as the core of Critical Theory: a society is assessed by its own standards, and the gap between those standards and social reality supplies the critical leverage. The description is easy to state, but it conceals a choice about what "from the object" means. Stahl (2013) separates three options. External critique brings in norms from moral constructivism or moral realism. Merely internal critique applies the standards that the audience already accepts. Immanent critique, on Stahl's account, draws on normative potentials that exist in the objective social reality but are not yet recognised by its participants, so that it rejects both alternatives. On this view any theory of immanent critique owes an account of the social ontology of such standards, of the critic's epistemic access to them, and of the critical authority they can carry. Stahl notes that the term has "rarely been clearly defined".

This review has two parts. Part I traces the genealogy of the method from Hegel and the Young Hegelians through Marx (Section 2), the first generation of the Frankfurt School (Section 3), and the post-1970 grounding strategies of Habermas, Honneth, Fraser, Walzer, Jaeggi, Celikates and Stahl (Section 4). Part II is organised by problem rather than by author. It takes up the taxonomy of immanent, internal and external critique and the charge of conservatism (Section 5), the roles of contradiction, ideology critique and immanent transcendence (Section 6), and the dependence of the method on progress and on a shared context, together with its uptake outside philosophy (Section 7). Section 8 gathers the results.

Both the definition and the genealogy are disputed, and the review keeps each dispute in view. The genealogy is the first. The familiar narrative, which Finlayson (2014) calls the "Continuity Thesis", holds that Hegel invented immanent criticism, Marx developed it, and the Frankfurt School, Adorno in particular, refined it. Finlayson separates four claims within it: that Hegel originated the practice, that Hegel's dialectic is immanent criticism, that Adorno practises it and endorses the term as a description of his own work, and that Adorno's method is fundamentally Hegelian. He argues that the thesis should be rejected. Part I therefore tells a chronological story, but it does so without assuming continuity. At each step it asks what is inherited and what is changed. Becker (2018) already finds two non-equivalent senses of the term within Hegel's *Phenomenology*: judging an object by its own norms, and accounting for one's own standpoint by reference to the object. This suggests that the family of positions called "immanent critique" may have been heterogeneous from the start.

The definition is the second dispute, and recent analytic work presses it harder. Fraser (2023) argues that the prospects of immanent criticism are "bleak", because it must commit to an implausible social ontology, to a flawed metanormative theory, or to both. Von Samson (2025) reconstructs the resulting dilemma. If embedded norms carry normative authority for participants, critique becomes structurally conservative. If they do not, critique loses its normative bite. She replies that the method's specificity lies in clarifying immanent contradictions, not in appealing to embedded norms. Procyshyn (2019) takes a deflationary route. He extracts three requirements, inherence, contradiction and access, and concludes that immanent critique "is not a method in any strict sense", because these requirements can be interpreted in more than one way. Särkelä (2017) offers a different diagnosis. One tradition locates immanence in the standard, and another locates it in the form of the critical practice itself.

Four axes organise the discussion in each section. (a) The source of standards: embedded norms, reconstructed presuppositions, the form of a practice, contradiction, or experienced suffering. (b) The role of contradiction, whether logical, social, performative or practical. (c) Whether the critique is reconstructive, recovering norms already in place, or transformative, directed at changing the practice that bears them. (d) The relation to progress, and whether the method requires a developmental philosophy of history. The Conclusion returns to the four axes to show where the figures discussed fall on each.
