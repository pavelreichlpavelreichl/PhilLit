## Introduction

Theodor W. Adorno and Max Horkheimer write within, and against, the German tradition of speculative philosophy of history inaugurated by Kant's idea of a universal history with a cosmopolitan intent, elaborated by Hegel's philosophy of Spirit, and transformed by Marx's account of historical materialism. That tradition supplies the categories — progress, universal history, totality, a world-historical subject — that organize its narratives of human development. Horkheimer and Adorno's *Dialectic of Enlightenment* ([1944] 2020) inherits these categories but redeploys them genealogically against their own optimistic use: reason's historical trajectory from mythic ritual to Enlightenment and beyond is read as a movement of domination that reverts to the mythic violence it claims to have overcome (Horkheimer and Adorno 2020). This review asks what kind of intellectual project results. Is the outcome a substantive, if negative, philosophy of history that retains the genre's ambitions while inverting its content? Is it instead a meta-critique that dismantles the presuppositions of philosophy of history as such — its subject, its teleology, its claim to totality? Or does it amount to what O'Connor (2008) and Sherman (2016) characterize, in different registers, as a "melancholy science" that holds the question of history's meaning in suspension rather than resolving it in either direction? This guiding tension threads through every section below.

Four cross-cutting problems recur across the literature surveyed here. First is the entwinement of myth and enlightenment — the thesis that mythic domination and enlightened rationality are not successive stages but reciprocally implicated moments of a single historical logic. Second is the dissolution of the antithesis between nature and history, first programmatically stated in Adorno's 1932 lecture on the idea of natural history (Adorno [1932] 1984) and subsequently traced through his mature work. Third is the pairing of progress and catastrophe within a "totality without a subject": Adorno's negative philosophy of history retains totalizing categories — universal history, the whole — while denying history any constituting collective subject, a construction whose internal coherence is contested. Fourth is the normative status of a critique that appears to totalize even as it attacks totalization, a problem posed most forcefully by Habermas's (1987) charge that the *Dialectic*'s critique of reason is performatively self-undermining.

The review preserves internal differentiation that is easily flattened under the label "Frankfurt School": Adorno's and Horkheimer's positions diverge in emphasis and trajectory even within jointly authored work; Walter Benjamin functions as both formative source and critical foil for their historiography; both figures rework a shared Hegelian-Marxist baseline; and the reception, from Habermas (1987) onward, has itself become a site of dispute, including recent decolonial reassessments of the category of progress (Allen 2016).

The review proceeds in seven sections: the *Dialectic of Enlightenment*'s entwinement thesis (1); Adorno's natural-history concept (2); his negative categories of progress, universal history, and totality (3); the Hegelian-Marxist legacy (4); Horkheimer's independent trajectory (5); Benjamin as source and foil (6); and the reception debate (7). Consistent with the priority on comprehensive and balanced coverage, the review deliberately over-samples critical and dissenting literature, so that objections to the Frankfurt position receive treatment proportionate to its defenses rather than being treated as an afterthought.

## Section 1: The *Dialectic of Enlightenment* as Philosophy of History: Myth, Reason, and Self-Destruction

Horkheimer and Adorno's *Dialectic of Enlightenment* opens with a claim that reorganizes the entire subsequent debate over their philosophy of history: "myth is already enlightenment, and enlightenment reverts to mythology" (Horkheimer and Adorno 2020). This entwinement thesis denies that reason's emergence from myth constitutes a clean historical break; instead, the domination of external nature that enlightenment achieves through calculative, instrumental reason is bound up with a corresponding domination — ultimately a mutilation — of the inner nature of the subject who exercises that reason. The Odyssey excursus, read as the "basic text" of Western subjectivity, and the later chapters on the culture industry and antisemitism extend this genealogy from prehistoric ritual into industrial capitalism and fascism, presenting enlightenment's trajectory as one of self-destruction (Horkheimer and Adorno 2020). While this entwinement thesis is largely uncontested as the book's core claim, scholars diverge sharply over its *modality* — whether the narrative it tells is fatalistic, aporetic by design, disclosive, therapeutic, or merely preparatory — and this section traces that divergence across three interpretive layers.

### 1.1 The entwinement thesis and the domination of nature

The bare formula "myth is already enlightenment" invites a two-term reading in which mimesis (magical, imitative comportment toward nature) is simply displaced by instrumental rationality. Hulatt (2016) challenges this simplification, arguing that the book's genealogy is better modeled as a tripartite conflict among mimesis, rationality, and self-preservation understood as an independent ordering principle, rather than as an internally driven development from mimesis to instrumental reason alone. On Hulatt's account, self-preservation functions as a controlling force that intensifies the conflict between mimesis and rationality, a tension only partially relaxed in bourgeois autonomous art's later incorporation of mimetic comportment. This revision matters because it resists collapsing the entwinement thesis into a single deterministic mechanism, leaving room for mimesis to retain unresolved critical residue rather than being fully absorbed into domination.

A parallel complication concerns the status of "nature" itself. Nelson (2011) argues that nature functions doubly in Adorno and the Frankfurt School: as an ideology-critical category (unmasking naturalizing legitimations of social domination) and as an ethical category (registering what is lost or repressed through the domination of outer and inner nature). Nelson contends that these two functions are not always clearly distinguished in *Dialectic of Enlightenment* or in its reception, a conflation that bears on how strongly the domination-of-nature thesis should be read — as historical-sociological explanation, as moral indictment, or as both simultaneously. Stone (2014) situates this same dialectical reversal — domination over nature reverting into domination by nature — within Adorno's broader, ambivalent relation to Hegel, arguing that Adorno retains Hegelian dialectic's demand for reconciliation of contradictions while breaking with Hegel's *identity thinking*; for Adorno, reconciled differences co-exist as differences rather than being unified into a totality. This Hegelian reframing already anticipates the modality question taken up in 1.3, since it recasts the entwinement thesis as internally consistent dialectical logic rather than pure fatalism.

### 1.2 The *Odyssey* excursus as genealogy of the subject

The Odyssey excursus supplies the book's central case study, reading Odysseus as a prototype of bourgeois subjectivity: self-preservation through renunciation and calculated sacrifice constitutes both his mastery over external nature and his self-domination. Comay (2000) offers a close reading of the Sirens episode as the emblematic instance of this logic: Odysseus's cunning self-binding, undertaken to escape bondage to the Sirens' song, unleashes what she calls an "endless ritual of sado-masochistic bondage games" turned against the self — reason becoming unreason at its own limit, and self-domination culminating in self-mutilation. This reading exemplifies the excursus's double movement, whereby the conquest of an external Other (nature, the feminine, pleasure) is purchased through an internal violence the subject inflicts upon itself.

Allen (2024) complicates the standard characterization of Odysseus as merely an instrumental-reason prototype, arguing that readings focused solely on calculation overlook the role of *cunning* (*metis*) in his characterization. Cunning, on Allen's account, is itself a form of mimetic intelligence bearing its own internal dialectic, distinct from bare instrumental calculation, with consequences for how Adorno's later account of non-identity thinking should be understood. This revisionist emphasis converges with Hulatt's (2016) tripartite model in resisting a flattened, purely instrumental reading of the excursus's protagonist.

Bermingham (2018) extends the genealogy-of-the-subject reading through a feminist immanent critique, arguing that Helen of Argos and Troy — conspicuously absent from both the excursus itself and prior feminist commentary on it — is nonetheless central to its thesis. Bermingham contends that Helen exhibits, as much as Odysseus does, the incipient features of an individuated and alienated bourgeois subject, and that her exclusion from Horkheimer and Adorno's analysis exposes an undertheorization of women's experience of subjectivity within the excursus's framework. This reading supplements rather than rejects the entwinement thesis, identifying a structural gap in its historical narrative of subject-formation rather than repudiating the narrative's basic mechanism.

### 1.3 What kind of "philosophy of history"?

Beyond the content of the entwinement thesis, commentators disagree over what genre of historical argument the book advances. Foster (2001) classifies it as genealogy critique in a broadly Nietzschean vein: rather than offering linear causal history or a normatively grounded argument, the book traces the contingent, power-laden emergence of instrumental rationality and the modern subject, exposing costs concealed within reason's self-understanding as progress. Honneth (2000) proposes an alternative classification, reading the text as a "disclosing critique" that deploys rhetorical means — narration, myth, chiasmus, exaggeration — "in order to evoke a new way of seeing the social world," a mode of argument that is world-disclosing rather than propositionally grounded and that sidesteps the demand for normative foundations. Noppen (2012) offers an intermediate reading, treating the book as propaedeutic and diagnostic — preparing, but not yet elaborating, a "positive concept of enlightenment" — such that it should be assessed as an unfinished project rather than as either self-refuting or covertly complete.

Evans (2020) proposes a distinct, genre-based account, reading the book in part as a parody of anti-enlightenment thought in the tradition of Spengler and Klages, with its rhetorical excess and totalizing pronouncements deliberately styled to mimic and thereby undercut cultural-pessimist rhetoric from within. Kent (2024), building on Hulatt's tripartite model, argues against a purely pessimistic reading on different grounds: myth functions not only as what enlightenment has always already been, but also as an incomplete, largely untapped mediator of human experience through language, offering "momentary reflections" of relinquished possibilities of life and thus a renewed chance of enlightenment. Rabinbach (2000) extends the question of the narrative's scope to the "Elements of Anti-Semitism" chapter, arguing that Horkheimer and Adorno treat antisemitism not as contingent political pathology but as an outgrowth of the same historical dialectic of self-preservation and domination traced throughout the book — a move that tests how far the genealogy's explanatory reach extends, from prehistoric myth to twentieth-century genocide. Setiya (2023), writing from outside Critical Theory, engages this same question of scope from an analytic ethical-naturalist standpoint: he defends a form of ethical naturalism in which ethical knowledge is grounded in a socially and historically determined human nature, without lapsing into relativism, and credits Adorno and the Frankfurt School with correctly identifying that once society becomes "total," critique can only proceed immanently.

These competing genre-classifications converge on a single unresolved pivot, first named by Habermas (1987). Habermas argues that *Dialectic of Enlightenment* levels a totalizing critique of reason that performatively contradicts itself: the book "denounces the Enlightenment's becoming totalitarian with its own tools," yet, having conflated instrumental rationality with rationality as such, it is left without normative grounds for its own critique and can only "practice determinate negation on an ad hoc basis." This charge — that the entwinement thesis, taken to its totalizing conclusion, undermines the very standpoint from which it is issued — reframes every genre-classification surveyed above as an implicit answer to Habermas: genealogy critique, disclosive critique, propaedeutic reading, parody, and anti-fatalist recovery of myth each represent a distinct strategy for showing that the book's historical narrative escapes self-refutation. This review returns to adjudicate the performative-contradiction charge directly in Section 7; here it is introduced only as the pivot around which the competing readings organize themselves.

**Connection.** The same text that yields a settled entwinement thesis yields a persistently contested account of its modality: read one way, the narrative of myth reverting to enlightenment and enlightenment reverting to myth is a totalizing dead end that forecloses the very critical resources it deploys; read another way, its aporias, ambiguities, and rhetorical strategies are deliberate features of a critical resource that remains open rather than fatalistic. The entwinement thesis is established as the book's core; whether that thesis is fatalist or emancipatory, argumentative or performative, remains the review's live and recurring question.

## Section 2: Natural History (*Naturgeschichte*): Dissolving the Nature/History Antithesis

If the *Dialectic of Enlightenment* narrates myth and enlightenment as dialectically entangled rather than successive, the conceptual apparatus that makes such entanglement thinkable is supplied a decade earlier, in Adorno's 1932 Frankfurt lecture "The Idea of Natural History." This section reconstructs that lecture's programme and sources, then asks whether "natural history" names a stable philosophical doctrine, a shifting family of motifs, or a method — before tracing its afterlife into Adorno's aesthetics, his engagement with Marx, and contemporary ecological and postcritical reappropriations.

### 2.1 The 1932 programme and its sources

Adorno's lecture argues that the received antithesis between nature — construed as static, ahistorical, mythically recurrent — and history — construed as the domain of meaningful human change — must be dialectically dissolved into a single category, "natural history": nature is shown to be historically produced and sedimented, while history recurs with a nature-like, mythic compulsion (Adorno 1984). The lecture is explicitly polemical on two fronts. Against Heidegger's fundamental ontology, Adorno charges that a supposedly historicized "being" smuggles back an ahistorical "meaning"; against historicism, he rejects any naive faith in meaningful progression (Adorno 1984). Escuela Cruz (2014) reconstructs this polemic as an "ontological reorientation of the philosophy of history" and identifies its two named philosophical antecedents: Lukács's concept of "second nature" from *Theory of the Novel* — the alienated, convention-bound social world confronting the modern subject as meaningless and as opaque as physical nature — and Benjamin's concept of allegory. Stahl's (2013) overview of Lukács's development situates second nature as the point where Lukács's later theory of reification first crystallizes, providing the Weberian-rationalization backdrop against which Adorno's radicalization operates. Buck-Morss (1977) supplies the biographical-intellectual context for this synthesis, documenting how Adorno's pre-exile writings of the late 1920s and early 1930s were shaped above all by Kracauer, Lukács, and Benjamin, and showing how Adorno's Benjaminian method exposes a timeless "second nature" beneath the apparently ahistorical dialectic of subjectivity in his contemporaneous Kierkegaard study — evidence that the 1932 lecture belongs to a broader, biographically traceable formation rather than standing as an isolated occasional piece.

### 2.2 Stable doctrine or shifting motif?: unity vs. therapy

Scholars diverge over what kind of philosophical claim natural history makes. Pensky (2004) distinguishes three strands operating under the concept across Adorno's career — a reflection on the normative and methodological bases of critical social science, a conception of critical memory oriented toward historical suffering, and the "mindfulness of nature in the subject" thesis of *Dialectic of Enlightenment* — and argues these strands are unified by the category of transience: a critical theory attentive to what is transient in history. This is a unificatory reading, treating natural history as a coherent, multiply-articulated concept. Whyman (2016) argues against readings of this kind, contending that the literature has failed to specify what natural history actually is or does, and proposing instead that it is best understood as a "therapeutic" concept whose function is to dissolve philosophical anxieties about the nature/history relation that would otherwise obstruct critical-theoretical understanding, rather than to advance a substantive metaphysical or historiographical thesis. Helmling (2003) approaches the same tension from the side of method rather than doctrine, examining Adorno's practice of "constellation" against Benjamin's "dialectical image." Adorno repeatedly characterizes Benjamin's thought as itself "natural history," drawn to the petrified and obsolete; Helmling traces Adorno's ambivalence toward the resulting stasis — the "Medusa-gaze" that petrifies its object — into the "dialectics at a standstill" structuring the *Dialectic of Enlightenment*'s account of progress reverting to regress. On this reading, natural history is constitutively bound to the question of how critical theory formally presents its objects, not reducible to either a unified doctrine or a purely dissolving therapy.

### 2.3 Persistence and afterlife: aesthetics, Marx, ecology

Natural history's categories persist well beyond 1932. Pensky (2021) argues that the lecture's core notion of transience supplies the interpretive resources for *Aesthetic Theory*'s otherwise obscure claim that artworks possess a "truth content" accessible only through philosophical interpretation, functioning there as ideology critique. Daniels (2020) similarly reads Adorno's account of natural beauty through the Adorno-Benjamin shared category of transience, while Gandesha (2004) extends the post-Heideggerian problematic comparatively to Arendt, arguing that both thinkers turn to Kant's Third Critique to locate a path beyond the "chiasmic" structure of nature and history left unresolved by Heidegger's *Destruktion*. Breitenstein (2021) locates the concept precisely within the second model of *Negative Dialectics*, where Adorno follows Marx's critique of political economy in criticizing the appearance of natural necessity (*Naturwüchsigkeit*) in social relations; she argues that Adorno's own dismissal of Marx's "language mannerisms" rests on a one-sided reading that underestimates the emancipatory potential of Marx's aesthetic mediation of social practice as natural history. Stone (2006) connects natural history to the disenchantment thesis of *Dialectic of Enlightenment*, showing how *Negative Dialectics* and *Aesthetic Theory* generate a critical re-enchantment in which natural beings appear meaningful as bearers of suffering. This ecological register is developed at length by Cook (2011), who compares Adorno's account of nature to social ecology, ecofeminism, and deep ecology, and by Wankhammer (2024), who mobilizes natural history as a corrective to postcritique's exhausted reflex of denaturalizing critique, applying it to environmental-justice politics; Crawford (2018) similarly defends the concept's continued applicability against claims of its obsolescence for contemporary art, and McCann (2024) extends the allegory/symbol axis comparatively to Ernst Jünger's "planetary history." Against this Adorno-centered trajectory, Friedlander (2024) argues that natural history is not merely a category Adorno adapted from Benjamin but an organizing problem running through Benjamin's own corpus — from the early writings on language and nature through Goethean "empiricism" to the Arcades Project's primal history — offering a Benjamin-centered counterweight to accounts that treat the concept as Adorno's intellectual property. This literature flags, without fully developing, the concept's application to the Anthropocene, a gap taken up in Section 3.

Across these disputes, the literature converges on natural history's centrality to Adorno's project and diverges on its status — doctrine, therapy, or method — and on how much credit belongs to Adorno versus Benjamin. This dissolution of the nature/history antithesis is the theoretical precondition for reading myth and enlightenment as dialectically entangled (Section 1) rather than as successive stages, and it anticipates the "world spirit without a subject" and negative-universal-history constructions examined in Section 3.

## Section 3: Adorno's Negative Philosophy of History: Progress, Catastrophe, Universal History, Totality without a Subject

Adorno's mature writings develop the categories inherited from the *Dialectic of Enlightenment* and from natural history into a more explicit, if still deliberately unstable, vocabulary: progress, universal history, and totality. Each term is retained rather than discarded, but retained under negation — a strategy that sharpens rather than resolves the review's guiding question of whether critical theory yields a substantive philosophy of history or a critique of the genre itself.

### 3.1 Progress reconstrued: neither affirmed nor denied

Adorno's 1962 essay "Progress" supplies the primary text for this refusal of affirmation and denial alike. Frelek (2025) reconstructs the essay's argument as holding history and catastrophe together without collapsing one into the other, situating Adorno's dialectical concept of progress within the theological genealogy of progress-thinking and its bearing on the dialectic of freedom and unfreedom; Frelek further extends this "negative hope" reading to the contemporary climate crisis, treating Adorno's non-affirmative, non-nihilistic stance as still actionable rather than merely diagnostic. Hohendahl (2013) situates the same essay as an implicit dialogue with Augustine, Kant, and Benjamin, arguing that Adorno's refusal of both linear-teleological and cyclical models of history draws on this longer theological-philosophical lineage — Augustinian providence, Kantian moral progress, Benjaminian messianic interruption — to fashion a concept of progress that negates each model's closure while retaining something of its ethical force. Çıdam (2020) identifies a "two-track" structure internal to Adorno's progress-talk: it functions simultaneously as the "new categorical imperative" (the post-Auschwitz injunction that Auschwitz not repeat) and as a politics of remembrance, so that Adorno's remarks on progress are not a single unified thesis about historical development but a heterogeneous cluster of ethical and memorial functions. This heterogeneity is corroborated from outside the Adorno literature by Weisbrod (2020), who reconstructs a "missed connection" between Adorno and Karl Löwith, co-panelists at the 1962 congress on progress: Löwith, Weisbrod argues, had already diagnosed the three problems Adorno's dialectical account attempts to solve, and each thinker is sympathetic to an aspect of the other's position — Löwith to the dialectical structure of Adorno's progress, Adorno to Löwith's insistence on progress's redemptive aspect. The decisive disagreement Weisbrod locates is over the status of suffering in history: for Löwith an ontological condition (secularized eschatology), for Adorno a political condition to be struggled against and overcome. This comparison clarifies what is distinctively non-theological, or at least non-fatalistic, in Adorno's retention of a redemptive moment within progress.

### 3.2 Universal history after Auschwitz

Adorno's negative retention of universal history follows the same logic. Burdman (2020) compares Adorno's and Lyotard's reconceptions of historical writing after Auschwitz, treating both as responses to a founding, unrepresentable wrong; Burdman's comparison clarifies that Adorno, unlike Lyotard's differend-based rejection of grand narrative, negatively retains a universal-historical framework rather than abandoning it outright — history remains tracked as a continuity, but one whose unity is constituted by catastrophe rather than by progress. Sherman (2016) names this stance a "qualified rejection of universal history," situating it as one theme among several — alongside the critique of idealist conceptuality, the critique of enlightenment reason's entanglement with capitalist society, and the concept of the constellation — that together constitute negative dialectics' response to a history that has failed to actualize its own prognostications. Johnson (2019) extends this negative universal history into the Anthropocene, arguing that Adorno's framework can accommodate humankind's compulsion to think itself as a unified geological agent while nonetheless resisting the totalizing ambitions of Enlightenment universal history; a "negative universal history" of the Anthropocene, on this reading, tracks planetary-scale forces in their uneven, local manifestations rather than positing a single historical subject or telos. Luna Jiménez (2020) supplies the developmental frame for these claims, tracing the nature/history antithesis from the 1932 "Idea of Natural History" through its reformulation in *Dialectic of Enlightenment* and *Negative Dialectics*, and arguing that this dialectical treatment underwrites Adorno's later capacity to speak of a "universal history" that is negative in structure yet still oriented toward the human/nature relationship — a continuity that bears directly on the ecological extension developed by Bell and Frelek.

### 3.3 Totality without a subject; the Hegelian reworking

The sharpest test of coherence concerns totality. Stone (2014) argues that Adorno's apparently mixed responses to Hegel form a coherent whole: Adorno follows Hegelian dialectical logic in tracing how enlightenment reverts to myth and domination over nature reverts into domination by nature, while rejecting Hegel's dialectic as the culmination of identity thinking. The crucial reworking, on Stone's account, concerns reconciliation itself — where Hegel's reconciliation unites differences into a totalized whole, Adorno's reconciled differences co-exist as differences, without subsumption. Stone deploys this reshaping to defend Adorno's consistency against Habermas's performative-contradiction charge (a debate taken up further in Section 7), suggesting that a negative totality is coherent precisely because it abandons the unifying, subject-constituted whole rather than merely disguising one. Baumann (2011) approaches the same terrain through the abstract/concrete universal distinction: Adorno, on this account, adopts Hegel's view that the abstract universal is the form of false knowledge and society while the concrete universal names a positive aim, but rejects Hegel's own construal of the concrete universal in favor of an alternative. Baumann leaves open whether this alternative overcomes the problems Adorno identifies in Hegel or remains structurally bound to them — a question that, left unresolved, keeps the coherence of "negative totality" an open interpretive matter rather than a settled result. O'Connor (2011) offers a broadly sympathetic overview of the same transformation, reading Adorno's negative dialectic as operating without a guaranteeing subject or absolute spirit, and situating this as an internal transformation of Hegel's method rather than its wholesale rejection. Allen (2025) approaches the question from a different angle, reading Adorno's engagement with Kant's Critique of Judgment as opening the possibility of thinking history "without the whole" — without unity or purpose — an aesthetically grounded alternative to the totalizing philosophies of history in Hegel, Marx, and (implicitly) Lyotard. Bell (2017) closes the loop back to natural history, using Adorno's collapse of the nature/history antithesis as a lens for ecological catastrophe and thereby linking the totality-without-a-subject problem to the progress/catastrophe pairing examined in 3.1.

Taken together, these accounts converge on describing Adorno's operation — negation and retention at once — with increasing precision, while diverging on whether the resulting "negative totality without a subject" is a stable philosophical achievement or an unresolved tension inherited from Hegel. This is the review's meta-question in its sharpest form: whether Adorno offers a substantive negative doctrine of history, a meta-critique that dismantles the genre from within, or a therapeutic suspension of the question altogether. The comparison with Hegel and Marx developed in Section 4 supplies the genealogical baseline needed to assess these readings, and the reception debate in Section 7 returns to adjudicate whether Habermas's performative-contradiction charge, which Stone's reading already anticipates, ultimately stands.

## Section 4: The Hegelian-Marxist Legacy: Continuities and Breaks

Any assessment of Adorno's and Horkheimer's philosophy of history requires a baseline: what, precisely, do they inherit from Hegel and Marx, and at what point does inheritance give way to departure? The literature converges on two inherited commitments — determinate negation and immanent critique — while disputing both their original meaning in Hegel and the degree to which critical theory's refusal of reconciliation constitutes a genuine break or an internal transformation of the same problematic.

### 4.1 The transformation of Hegel's dialectic

Marcuse's reading of Hegel as a theorist of the "power of negative thinking" — a dialectic that denounces the given as merely apparent and points beyond it — established the founding Frankfurt School template for treating Hegelian negativity as inherently critical rather than affirmative (Marcuse 1941). This "positive" appropriation, transmitted to Marx and available for critical theory's own use, functions as the baseline against which Adorno's more skeptical treatment of Hegel is measured. Stone (2014) argues that Adorno's apparently mixed responses to Hegel — endorsing the dialectical process by which enlightenment reverts to myth while rejecting Hegel's dialectic as the ultimate form of "identity thinking" — cohere once reconciliation itself is redefined: where Hegel's reconciliation unites differences into a whole, Adorno's leaves differences co-existing as differences. Finlayson (2014) presses harder on the inherited narrative, isolating what he calls the "Continuity Thesis" — the assumption that Hegel invented immanent criticism, Marx developed it, and the Frankfurt School (especially Adorno) merely refined the same method — and arguing that Adorno's practice diverges from Hegel's in ways the thesis obscures. His companion survey (Finlayson 2017) extends this differentiation across generations, finding the young Horkheimer a qualified Hegelian-Marxist who treats Hegel's idealist metaphysics as superseded by sociology and materialist historiography, Adorno a more committed Hegelian who nonetheless rejects Hegel's philosophy of objective spirit, and Marcuse a more charitable reader emphasizing reason's critical moment. Rose (2009) supplies an independent philosophical test: she argues that Weberian and neo-Kantian sociology — including strands of Western Marxism — loses Hegel's speculative insight that social forms can be grasped only by thinking identity and non-identity together, raising the question of whether critical theory's negative dialectic radicalizes this speculative logic or retreats from it into dualism. Marasco (2015) reframes the entire transformation around Hegel's own trope of the "highway of despair" from the Phenomenology's introduction, arguing that critical theory's most consequential move is to inhabit Hegelian negativity rather than resolve it — accepting negativity without the promise of Absolute reconciliation that structures Hegel's system, against later normative-reconstructive critical theory that seeks a way out of despair.

### 4.2 Marxism, totality, and the vanishing subject

If the Hegel question concerns method, the Marx question concerns the fate of totality and its bearer. Jay (1984) traces totality's "adventures" as the concept migrates from Lukács's identification of totality with the standpoint of the proletarian subject-object of history, through Korsch and the early Frankfurt School, to Habermas's eventual abandonment of strong totality claims for differentiated communicative rationality — treating Adorno's dictum that "the whole is the untrue" as one moment in this adventure rather than a simple rejection of the concept. This genealogical account is grounded institutionally by Jay (1973), who documents how the Institute for Social Research developed a program of "interdisciplinary materialism" deliberately distinct from Soviet economism and Second International determinism, while remaining indebted to Lukács's and Korsch's Hegelian-Marxist revisions of the 1920s; Wiggershaus (1994) supplies further archival grounding for tracing when the Institute's Marxist self-understanding, including expectations about proletarian revolutionary agency, gave way to the more skeptical diagnosis of Dialectic of Enlightenment. Feenberg (2014) synthesizes this trajectory as a single "philosophy of praxis" running from Marx through Lukács's reification theory to the Frankfurt School, arguing that Adorno's and Marcuse's engagement with nature and rationality is best read as an internal transformation of Lukács's subject-object problematic rather than a rupture with it — though Adorno, taking commodity fetishism as his starting point and eschewing appeals to emancipation or revolution, emerges in Feenberg's account as something of an outlier within the tradition he transforms. The origin of the "identical subject-object" thesis itself is reconstructed by Arato and Breines (1979), who trace Lukács's Hegelian-Marxist synthesis from Neo-Kantian and Romantic anti-capitalism to History and Class Consciousness, and by Löwy (1979), who situates the same trajectory within a broader "Romantic anti-capitalist" critique of reified modernity. Both accounts frame why Horkheimer and Adorno could not simply adopt Lukács's proletariat-as-subject thesis once that subject failed to materialize historically.

### 4.3 What survives of Marxian political economy?

The question of what remains once the revolutionary subject recedes is answered differently depending on whether attention turns to value theory or to reification. Postone (1993) reinterprets Marx's mature critique of political economy as a theory of abstract, quasi-automatic domination exercised by the value-form over social life, arguing that traditional Marxism mistakenly locates capitalism's contradiction in the standpoint of labor rather than treating labor itself as a historically specific category to be overcome — a reconstruction that supplies resources for understanding capital's subjectless domination compatible with Adorno's and Horkheimer's skepticism about a guaranteed revolutionary agent. Lichtenstein (2022) extends this line directly into Adorno scholarship, defending Adorno's theory of domination as structural and de-individualized against Honneth's charge of theoretical underdevelopment, and arguing that Marx's value theory — locating value's constitution in the social totality rather than production or exchange alone — supplies the explanatory account of abstraction that Adorno's own texts leave undeveloped. Schmidt (1971), writing from within the Frankfurt School itself, reconstructs Marx's historical materialism as resting on a dialectical concept of nature — worked-on and historically mediated, yet never reducible to social construction — that underlies the School's non-reductive materialism and its critique of the domination of nature. On the reification side, Stahl (2018) offers a balanced reception history of Lukács's influence, noting that his theory of reification inspired the first generation's diagnosis of capitalist modernity even as his political conclusions prompted critical distancing, while Kavoulakos (2017) traces reification's specific conceptual afterlife across generations, from interdisciplinary materialism through Honneth's recognition-theoretic reformulation. Two recent studies test whether negative totality can be detached from the proletarian subject altogether: Gindner (2023) argues that the theoretical work in History and Class Consciousness is done by totality rather than class or reification, and that totality can be actualized in a strictly negative sense — as capitalist coercion to be overcome — without an identifiable revolutionary agent, a strategy converging with, though not directly engaging, Adorno's own negative dialectic. Bueno (2024) reads Lukács and Adorno comparatively, finding that both arrive, by opposite paths, at an elimination of the subject-object distinction that threatens political impasse, but reconstructs both arguments as capable of preserving emancipatory subjectivity via the "non-identical" rather than negating it.

Taken together, these three subsections indicate that the break with Marxist teleology need not entail abandoning the philosophy of history so much as reconstituting it negatively: totality, dialectic, and even value-form domination persist as diagnostic categories once their positive, subject-guaranteeing functions are relinquished. The literature is broadly consistent in treating critical theory as an internal transformation, not a rupture, of the Hegel-Lukács-Marx lineage — Feenberg's continuity thesis, Jay's "adventures" of totality, and Gindner's and Bueno's detachment of totality from the proletariat all converge on this picture, even where Finlayson and Rose complicate how continuous the underlying method really is. What decisively separates Adorno and Horkheimer from their predecessors is not the abandonment of Hegelian negativity or Marxian categories of domination, but the fate of the revolutionary subject: once the proletariat can no longer be relied upon as history's identical subject-object, totality survives only as a negative, subjectless diagnosis of unfreedom — the condition that Sections 3 and 5 will show shaping Adorno's and Horkheimer's divergent responses.

## Section 5: Horkheimer's Trajectory: Materialism, the Critique of Reason, and the Theological Turn

Treating Horkheimer as an independent theorist of history — rather than as Adorno's co-author or the Institute's administrator — surfaces a developmental arc running from 1930s interdisciplinary materialism, through the wartime critique of instrumental reason, to a late pessimism inflected by negative theology. Whether this arc constitutes a single coherent trajectory or a series of ruptures, and how far its final stages are Horkheimer's own rather than artifacts of the Adorno collaboration, organizes the literature surveyed here.

### 5.1 The 1930s materialist programme

"Traditional and Critical Theory" functions as the domain's pivot text: it contrasts theory modeled on natural science with a Marxian mode that takes society as its own object and situates itself within history for emancipatory ends, while already shifting the critique of science from an external complaint about disciplinary fragmentation toward an internal one about formalism (Horkheimer 1982). The essays collected in *Between Philosophy and Social Science* — including "Materialism and Morality" and "On the Problem of Truth" — supply the primary-source basis for this program, conceiving truth as historically mediated yet answerable to an objective reality irreducible to thought, with philosophy's task the reduction of unnecessary suffering (Horkheimer 1993). Berendzen (2008) gives this materialism philosophical content by reconstructing it, via comparison with Bas van Fraassen's "empirical stance," as a considered postmetaphysical orientation toward inquiry rather than either a substantive metaphysical thesis or a mere refusal to theorize. The program's polemical edge against positivism has drawn historical scrutiny: O'Neill and Uebel (2004), recovering Otto Neurath's unpublished reply to Horkheimer's 1937 "Latest Attack on Metaphysics," argue that Horkheimer's caricature of logical positivism as reifying social reality was largely unfounded and reconstruct the debate about whether critique of science must proceed "from above" or "from within" that the two might otherwise have had. Handelman (2019) traces the same prewar confrontation with the Vienna Circle to show how it hardened into a broader historical narrative equating mathematized reason with the "downfall of Enlightenment," a narrative later canonized in the postwar texts. Against readings that treat the 1930s program as merely preparatory to *Dialectic of Enlightenment*, Abromeit (2011) reconstructs it as internally coherent and largely independent, showing that Horkheimer and Adorno did not begin working together closely until the late 1930s and that the Horkheimer-Fromm model of critical theory differs in crucial ways from what follows.

### 5.2 The wartime critique of reason

*Eclipse of Reason* traces the historical transformation of "objective reason" — reason capable of grasping ends and values — into "subjective reason," reduced to calculating means to unexamined ends, a formalization that Horkheimer argues culminates in a self-undermining rationality abetting domination and, at the limit, a "new barbarism" (Horkheimer 1947). Because Horkheimer explicitly withholds any positive program of action, the text has attracted the same normative-deficit objection leveled at *Dialectic of Enlightenment*. Freyenhagen (2023) answers this Habermasian charge by identifying a "linguistic turn avant la lettre" in Horkheimer's and Adorno's shared picture of language, arguing that the inseparability of human life-form and language, akin to the later Wittgenstein's view, makes language an inextinguishable reservoir of objective reason and thus a standard from which instrumental reason can still be criticized. Shea (2021), building on Berendzen's account of 1930s materialism as postmetaphysical, reaches the opposite assessment: the wartime and postwar critique of instrumental reason, he argues, paradoxically reintroduces the metaphysical commitments — a normative conception of nature, "objective reason" itself — that the earlier materialism had explicitly foresworn, generating unresolved tensions rather than a defensible development.

### 5.3 Late pessimism and the theological turn: continuity or rupture?

Horkheimer's postwar notebooks, collected in *Dawn and Decline*, register an increasingly pessimistic diagnosis of the "administered world" alongside recurring reflections on compassion and the limits of what critical theory can positively affirm (Horkheimer 1978). This late period crystallizes around the category of the "longing for the wholly other" — the desire, uncoupled from institutional religion, that history's injustice not be the final word — which Siebert (2005) reconstructs as a negative-theological resource for critical resistance rather than a lapse into dogma, and which Nagl-Docekal (2020), revisiting Horkheimer's 1970 *Spiegel* interview, traces to a Kantian conception of religion grounded in the needs of practical reason. Several accounts read the turn as continuous with Horkheimer's earlier commitments: Gunderson (2012) argues that pessimism about progress was always grounded in an abiding compassion for suffering running from the earliest to the latest writings, Walentowicz (2006) proposes an explicit three-stage periodization (prewar, wartime, postwar) unified by sustained self-critique and an unrelenting desire for a better world, and Michaelis (2022) qualifies rather than rejects this continuity narrative by specifying precisely what Horkheimer selectively appropriated from Schopenhauer's metaphysics of will and compassion. Crawford (2025) contests this consensus at the level of historiography: he argues that the "religious-metaphysical" late Horkheimer is not simply an immanent development but a Cold War-inflected construction of American reception history, one that relocated the Frankfurt School's foundations from Marxism and contemporary social problems to philosophy and religion. Stirk (1992) situates the same late period's political conservatism — opposition to the 1960s student movements and qualified support for American intervention in Vietnam — within, rather than as a betrayal of, Horkheimer's mature critical theory.

Because "the Frankfurt School" often collapses Horkheimer into Adorno, isolating Horkheimer's independent trajectory sharpens what in *Dialectic of Enlightenment* is genuinely co-authored versus individually held — a question relevant wherever the book's pessimism or its theological undertones are attributed to "critical theory" as such. The literature documents the developmental stages with some precision, but two questions remain open and comparatively under-theorized: whether continuity (Gunderson, Walentowicz, Michaelis) or historicized rupture (Crawford) better explains the late theological turn, and how far Horkheimer's own trajectory diverges from Adorno's negative philosophy of history examined in Section 3.

## Section 6: Benjamin's Messianic Historiography: Source and Foil

Walter Benjamin occupies a distinctive position in this literature: neither a predecessor whose categories Adorno and Horkheimer straightforwardly inherit nor an outside critic, but a co-formative interlocutor whose historiography is at once absorbed into and resisted by the Frankfurt position. The literature organizes around three questions — what Benjamin's theses actually claim against progressive historicism; how far his constellational method can be reconciled with Adorno's demand for dialectical mediation; and whether the messianic-theological register Benjamin retains is adopted, transformed, or ultimately expelled by his Frankfurt readers.

### 6.1 The theses and the critique of progress

Löwy's (2005) line-by-line commentary on "On the Concept of History" reads the theses as a "fire alarm" sounded simultaneously against fascism and against the complacent evolutionism of Second International Marxism and Social Democracy. On Löwy's account, Benjamin's "weak messianic power" names not a transcendent redeemer but a collective, this-worldly capacity for interruption — a reading that fuses Jewish messianism and revolutionary materialism as mutually constitutive rather than as a theological veneer over materialist content. Pensky (2010) situates the ninth thesis's angel of history, its face turned toward the past as it is driven backward into the future by the storm "we call progress," within the Kantian tradition of philosophy of history, arguing that Benjamin's anti-progressivism transforms rather than simply ruptures the Kantian problematic of historical knowing that also informs Frankfurt School thought. Khatib (2017) develops the companion concept of the "tradition of the oppressed," reconstructing it as a discontinuous medium that cannot be represented as a single coherent world-history but only as a discontinuum of struggles and "empty spots" — a formulation that breaks with both historicism's homogeneous, empty time and orthodox historical materialism's view of tradition as a merely retarding force.

This anti-progressivist reading nonetheless leaves open how far Benjamin's messianism is materialist rhetoric or genuinely theological substance. Tiedemann (1989 [1983]) poses this dilemma directly in his "historical materialism or political messianism?" framing, arguing — from within the circle of Benjamin's literary executors — for the priority of the materialist reading while conceding the irreducibility of the theses' theological imagery. Wolin (1994) argues the opposite case, tracing a redemptive, quasi-theological impulse across Benjamin's ostensibly more materialist later phases and contending that this impulse cannot be excised without loss of critical force. The Tiedemann-Wolin dispute thus frames the interpretive stakes for everything that follows: whether Adorno and Horkheimer's own philosophy of history is best understood as a secularization of Benjaminian messianism or as its disavowal.

### 6.2 Constellation, dialectical image, and the mediation dispute

The Arcades Project supplies the primary textual site for this dispute. Buck-Morss (1989) reconstructs Benjamin's method as a "dialectics of seeing" — a materialist historiography built from the montage of concrete historical images rather than narrative argument, in which nineteenth-century Paris becomes legible as the prehistory of the present — and documents Adorno's objections to the 1935 Exposé, articulated in the so-called "Hornberg letter," that crystallized the demand for explicit conceptual and economic mediation. Recoder (2019) examines Adorno's editorial and critical interventions into the Arcades materials as expressions of what Adorno himself called his "Arcades orthodoxy": a methodological insistence on rigorous dialectical mediation that Benjamin's own practice persistently exceeded or resisted, and that Adorno extended into a broader metacritique of Benjamin's treatment of aesthetic autonomy. Feldman (2011) offers the most precise reconstruction of the resulting charge, showing that Adorno's recurring accusation that Benjamin is "not dialectical enough" is internally chiastic: Adorno faults Benjamin's aesthetics for surrendering the artwork to ideology while faulting his historiography for refusing historical narrative altogether — precisely the site where Benjamin locates ideology's operation. Feldman's analysis resists assigning victory to either side, instead showing the disagreement's internal structure as one of divergent, only partly compatible conceptions of autonomy and redemption. Comay (1999) sharpens the methodological stakes further, arguing that Benjamin's historiography deliberately courts a "perverse" relation to history — reproducing, rather than simply diagnosing, the fetishistic freezing of time it seeks to interrupt — which complicates any simple opposition between Benjamin's supposedly "immediate" dialectical images and Adorno's demand for mediation. Buck-Morss's (1977) earlier study of the origin of negative dialectics situates this dispute institutionally, arguing that Adorno's mature method cannot be understood apart from a decade of collaboration and disagreement with Benjamin conducted partly under the material conditions of Institute funding and editorial control.

### 6.3 Messianism retained or expelled?

Beardsworth (2005) compares Benjamin's dialectical image with Horkheimer and Adorno's fragmentary method in *Dialectic of Enlightenment* as parallel rather than derivative responses to a shared problem: how to make authoritative critical statements about history once the concept of history is itself understood as entangled with domination. On this reading, the fragmentary form of the *Dialectic* is a structural analogue to, and departure from, Benjamin's dialectical image, rather than a simple extension of it. Brittain (2010) contests Agamben's claim that Adorno's negative dialectics is "absolutely non-messianic," arguing instead — via the original Adorno-Benjamin debate over theology, dialectics, and politics — that Adorno's relation to the messianic is one of cautious, dialectically mediated retention rather than outright rejection. Horkheimer's position appears more resistant: Bohn (2019) documents Horkheimer's and Tiedemann's dismissal of Benjamin's concept of *Eingedenken* (remembrance) as merely theological or aesthetic rather than politically significant, and argues, against this secularizing critique, that the ethical force of *Eingedenken* depends precisely on embracing its theological dimension. Moir (2016) asks what remains usable in Benjamin's philosophy of history once separated from strict theological or strict materialist orthodoxy, contending — via Bloch and Osborne — that any interpretation of Benjamin's historical significance requires a "weakly teleological" concept of history akin to the one his own theses sought to dismantle.

Taken together, this literature positions Benjamin as source and foil: his critique of progress and his constellational method supply resources Adorno and Horkheimer draw on, while the mediation dispute and the divergent fates of *Eingedenken* in Horkheimer's and Adorno's hands show the Frankfurt School unable either fully to adopt or fully to abandon Benjamin's theological-redemptive register. The secularization question this raises directly informs Horkheimer's own theological turn and its contested continuity with his earlier materialism, and it bears on the coherence of Adorno's negative universal history, which likewise retains categories — redemption, interruption, the non-identical particular — that it cannot straightforwardly ground apart from the Benjaminian inheritance it partially disavows.

## Section 7: Reception, Critique, and Contemporary Debates

The reconstructions offered in Sections 1 through 6 leave open a question that the literature treats as decisive for the entire research programme: does Adorno and Horkheimer's negative philosophy of history survive contact with its critics, or does it collapse under its own totalizing weight? The reception literature divides along two axes that only partially overlap. The first, older axis is Habermas's charge that the *Dialectic of Enlightenment* performs a self-undermining "totalization" of ideology-critique — a debate that structured Frankfurt School self-understanding for three generations. The second, more recent axis is the charge, mounted from decolonial and postcolonial theory, that critical theory's categories of progress and universal history remain Eurocentric even after Habermas's own corrective. Read together, these two bodies of criticism converge on a single unresolved object: the fate of "universal history" as a category critical theory can neither fully endorse nor fully discard.

### 7.1 The Habermas debate and the *Historical Materialism* exchange

Habermas's charge, most fully stated in *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity*, is that Adorno and Horkheimer's confidence in the emancipatory potential of reason was shaken so thoroughly by Nazism and Stalinism that their ideology-critique came to embrace reason itself, rendering critique "independent even in relation to its own foundations" and thus suspect of "not producing truths" (Habermas 1987). On this reading, once critique indicts reason as such, it forfeits the normative standard by which it distinguishes itself from what it criticizes — the "philosophy of consciousness" trapped in a performative contradiction it cannot resolve from within its own resources. Honneth's early essay on the dispute, written shortly after the charge crystallized, frames the stakes as a contest between Habermas's communication-theoretic model of intersubjective understanding and Adorno's aesthetically mediated conception of reconciliation with non-identical, mimetic nature — already registering, from within the second generation, the "methodological bareness" Habermas found once Adorno's imagery was stripped away (Honneth 1979).

This charge generated a sustained three-paper exchange in *Historical Materialism*. Cook (2000) contests Habermas's reading, arguing that Adorno's theory of ideology retains a coherent, if unorthodox, standard of truth that does not require the discourse-theoretic foundations Habermas demands. Finlayson (2003) offers a qualified defense of Habermas in response, holding that the diagnosis of self-undermining totalization retains force against Adorno's theory of ideology even once the more polemical exaggerations of Habermas's presentation are corrected. Vaki (2005) closes the cycle by reasserting that Adorno's practice of immanent critique constitutes a self-sufficient model of critical theory that does not require translation into a communicative-rationality framework to secure its normative standing. The exchange therefore does not resolve the underlying question so much as clarify exactly where it turns: whether immanent critique can certify its own normative authority without appeal to an intersubjective, discourse-theoretic ground external to the object it criticizes.

Merawi (2018) reframes the debate around what Habermas's proposed solution costs rather than whether it succeeds. Habermas, on this account, correctly identifies Horkheimer and Adorno's equation of the rational with the instrumental in the *Dialectic*, and his communicative-rationality model does supply an emancipatory alternative — but that alternative pays insufficient attention to aesthetic truth as a distinct, non-discursive register of critique that Adorno's work had preserved. This is a significant qualification: even readers who grant Habermas's diagnostic point can register a loss in his prescriptive remedy. The anti-Habermasian rejoinders already introduced elsewhere in this review sharpen that qualification into direct rebuttal. Freyenhagen (2024), using the polemical device of asking whether Habermas's own essay on the *Dialectic* would pass as coursework on the book, argues that Habermas's "Entwinement of Myth and Enlightenment" is philosophically and biographically unreflective, and proposes reading the *Dialectic* as a self-therapeutic exercise in destabilizing a complacent self-conception of modernity as moral progress rather than as the theoretical dead end Habermas presents. Stone (2014), independently, shows that Adorno can consistently criticize the Enlightenment practice of critique because his relation to Hegelian dialectic is more structured than Habermas allows: Adorno accepts the dialectical movement by which enlightenment reverts to myth while rejecting only Hegel's claim that dialectic can conceptually exhaust its object — a distinction Habermas's charge of inconsistency does not register. Taken together, the *Historical Materialism* cycle and these rejoinders show the performative-contradiction charge functioning less as a settled verdict than as the fixed reference point around which the entire subsequent reception organizes itself.

### 7.2 Rescue, replacement, or abandonment: the generational transmission

If Habermas's charge is granted even partial force, later Frankfurt theorists face a choice between rescuing Adorno's philosophy of history, replacing it, or abandoning it outright — and the literature registers all three responses without settling on one. Wellmer (2007) represents the clearest rescue strategy. He argues that Adorno's method of "rescuing" (*rettende*) critical constructions of the historical present — reading truth, freedom, justice, and democracy as ideas that transcend whatever is empirically given while remaining immanently operative within society — can still renew the original project of critical theory, provided it is decoupled from Adorno's totalizing pessimism about the "administered world." This is explicitly a middle path: neither full Habermasian replacement of Adorno's categories nor unmodified Adornian orthodoxy, but a selective extraction of method from mood.

Honneth's later essay collection *Pathologies of Reason* stages the rescue question from the vantage of the third generation. Its chapter "Saving the Sacred with a Philosophy of History" asks what, if anything, critical theory can still coherently mean by "philosophy of history" once teleological and totalizing versions are ruled out, while a companion chapter assesses Wellmer's own attempt to secularize Adornian "dissonance" — the irreducible remainder resisting full communicative-rational reconciliation — within a broadly Habermasian framework (Honneth 2009). Honneth's position is thus doubly mediated: a third-generation attempt to preserve selective elements of a critical philosophy of history while simultaneously adjudicating between Wellmer's and Habermas's competing strategies for accommodating, or dissolving, what Adorno leaves unreconciled.

Bernstein (2017) offers a more skeptical, non-partisan stock-taking of the whole trajectory. Reviewing Martin Jay's account of reason's "eclipse," Bernstein argues that the first generation never succeeded in articulating the substantive, Hegelian conception of reason (*Vernunft*) it hankered after, and grew increasingly pessimistic as reason appeared to self-destruct from within; Habermas's communicative-rationality alternative avoids the misleading promises of Hegelian *Vernunft* but leaves a series of deep, still-unresolved problems about what, if anything, remains distinctive about critical theory. Bernstein's conclusion — that neither the first generation's substantive reason nor Habermas's proceduralist alternative has fully resolved the problem of grounding critical theory's normative claims — refuses to award victory to either side of the rescue-versus-replacement dispute.

This internal indeterminacy is compounded by an external objection. Albert (1985), writing from German critical rationalism in the aftermath of the *Positivismusstreit*, rejects Frankfurt School dialectics as an alternative to logical empiricism and hermeneutics alike, arguing that it fails to escape foundationalism without collapsing into an unacceptable relativism about its own normative basis — a charge that presses from outside the Frankfurt tradition entirely and thus cannot be answered by internal generational adjustment. Bottomore (2002) surveys this broader reception landscape, cataloguing recurrent charges against the Frankfurt School as a whole — its neglect of political economy, its ambivalent relation to 1960s radical movements, its contested standing relative to positivism and structuralism — that frame the more specialized disputes of this section within a wider pattern of external and internal criticism. The generational transmission from Adorno through Wellmer and Honneth to Bernstein's assessment therefore does not converge on a settled successor doctrine; it registers, at each stage, what is given up in exchange for normative security.

### 7.3 Eurocentrism, the universal-history residue, and contemporary reassessment

A distinct and more recent line of criticism targets not the coherence of immanent critique but the historical-developmental assumptions embedded in critical theory's normative foundations as such. Allen (2016) argues that while decolonial and postcolonial theorists have debunked historical progress as a Eurocentric, imperialist fallacy, Habermas, Honneth, and Forst have each continued to rely on notions of progress, development, or modernity to ground their normative claims. Allen proposes "fracturing" critical theory from within: dispensing with a progressive reading of history as an accomplished fact while retaining progress as a political imperative — a stance she credits explicitly to Adorno, positioning him, against his own successors, as the resource for a decolonized critical theory. This argument inverts the terms of the Habermas debate: where Habermas treated Adorno's totalizing pessimism as a normative deficit to be corrected by communicative rationality, Allen treats Adorno's refusal to certify progress as historically accomplished as precisely what protects critical theory from a deeper, unexamined Eurocentric commitment that survives in Habermas's and Honneth's own frameworks.

The *Political Theory* symposium on Allen's book presses this claim from several directions without rejecting its basic thrust. Leeb, Nichols, and Winter question, respectively, the relative absence of capitalism from Allen's account, the specificity of settler-colonial as against other colonial histories, and the normative resources her contextualist alternative can and cannot supply (Leeb et al. 2018). Olson (2019) goes further, agreeing with Allen's diagnosis of Eurocentrism and progress-thinking but arguing that recent Frankfurt School work displaces critique in two ways Allen's own remedy does not fully address: a retreat into paradigmatic, meta-level analysis rather than concrete engagement, and reliance on democratic procedure for normativity without registering tensions in actual political cultures; he calls instead for postcolonial theory's interpretive, non-dogmatic critical strategies. Baum (2015), writing a year before Allen's book, had already argued that critical theory needed to confront the constitutive role of colonialism and racial domination in the formation of the capitalist societies it takes as its object, rather than treating colonialism as a peripheral concern — marking an early crystallization of the decolonizing-critical-theory project that Allen's book would extend. McArthur (2021) broadens the charge into a distinct disciplinary register, arguing that even in its early radical figurations critical theory shares Western philosophy's Eurocentric traits, and proposing a "decolonial conversation" as the basis for genuine engagement between critical theory and Indigenous philosophies.

Bhambra (2021) pushes the critique further than Allen, Baum, or the symposium contributors by arguing that "decolonizing" a tradition that has never explicitly acknowledged colonial histories requires recognizing the "colonial modern" — the constitutive role of colonization and slavery in modernity itself — which implicates critical theory's very categories of critique and their associated normative claims, not merely its substantive neglect of colonial topics; she concludes that epistemic acknowledgment must be accompanied by material reparations and what she terms "epistemological justice." Biskamp (2025) closes a related loop by extending postcolonial critique specifically to Habermas's own communicative-rationality alternative, applying Chakrabarty's "provincializing" strategy to argue that Habermas's post-metaphysical account of reason carries unexamined Eurocentric assumptions about historical development. This matters directly for the Habermas debate reconstructed in 7.1: if Habermas's proposed solution to Adorno's performative contradiction itself inherits rather than resolves a Eurocentric universal-history residue, then the normative deficit Habermas diagnosed in Adorno has not been eliminated but relocated.

Two further contributions register how far this reassessment has moved institutionally and reflexively. Torres (2025) documents that the Eurocentrism critique is no longer confined to academic symposia, tracing its ascent to institutional prominence at the Frankfurt Institute for Social Research itself following Stephan Lessenich's 2021 appointment as director. And in a 2025 roundtable, Allen, McNay, Bufkin, and Dyson jointly ask whether the post-Habermasian turn toward metanormative foundations has come at the cost of concrete, sociologically grounded analysis of power, explicitly linking the marginalization of gender hierarchies, the Marxist legacy, and Eurocentrism within a single stock-taking of critical theory's trajectory since Habermas (Allen et al. 2025). The critical lens is not reserved for Habermas and Adorno alone: Deranty (2022) turns it on Honneth's own historicized recognition theory, raising doubts about the cultural contexts, national framings, and intellectual-historiographical method Honneth uses to narrate a history of recognition — evidence that the reception debate's methodological scrutiny now extends to the third generation's own attempts at historical reconstruction.

The two axes surveyed in this section adjudicate the meta-question posed throughout this review at the level of reception rather than exegesis. The Habermasian normative-deficit charge and its *Historical Materialism* aftermath treat Adorno and Horkheimer's negative philosophy of history as a self-undermining dead end requiring either discourse-theoretic replacement or careful, decoupled rescue. The decolonial and postcolonial literature, by contrast, treats the same refusal to certify progress as historically accomplished — the feature Habermas found normatively deficient — as a resource against a Eurocentric residue that survives in the very communicative-rationality framework proposed to replace it. Both bodies of criticism converge, from opposite evaluative directions, on the same unresolved object: the status of "universal history" and progress as categories critical theory can neither straightforwardly affirm nor cleanly abandon.

## Conclusion

This review opened with a guiding meta-question: does early Frankfurt School critical theory offer a philosophy of history, or a critique of the genre as such? Across seven domains — the entwinement thesis of the *Dialectic*, natural history, Adorno's negative categories of progress and universal history, the Hegelian-Marxist legacy, Horkheimer's independent trajectory, Benjamin as source and foil, and the reception literature — the literature converges, without fully resolving, on a qualified answer. Adorno and Horkheimer are most defensibly read not as proponents of a rival, substantive philosophy of history standing alongside Kant's, Hegel's, or Marx's, but as practitioners of an immanent, negative critique of the genre's guiding categories — progress, universal history, totality, the world-historical subject — that nonetheless proves unable, or unwilling, to relinquish those categories outright. Progress is neither affirmed nor denied but held together with catastrophe (Section 3); universal history is retained "negatively," as O'Connor's (2008) framing of Adorno's project already anticipates, rather than abandoned as Lyotard's post-metanarrative stance would recommend; totality survives as "the whole is the false" even as its constituting subject disappears. The categories persist, in other words, under erasure: disavowed as achieved fact, retained as critical and normative pressure.

That formulation, however, names a settlement point around which several tensions remain genuinely unresolved rather than a stable consensus. First, the coherence question raised in Section 3 persists: whether a negative totality without a subject is a workable philosophical construction or merely a placeholder for an argument critical theory has not completed. Second, the modality question from Section 1 recurs in the reception literature of Section 7: whether the totalizing sweep of the entwinement thesis is self-undermining, as Habermas charged, or deliberately aporetic — a therapeutic destabilization of complacent narratives of moral progress, as Freyenhagen (2024) and others contend. Third, Section 5 registered how readily the "Adorno-Horkheimer" position collapses into a single undifferentiated voice, obscuring real divergences in trajectory, tone, and theological commitment between the two authors. Fourth, the secularization question from Section 6 remains open: whether the negative philosophy of history is Benjaminian messianism carried forward under a materialist disguise or a position that has expelled the redemptive register it cannot fully justify. Fifth, the decolonial critique surveyed in Section 7 exposes a universal-history residue that survives even sympathetic reconstructions, pressing the question of whether Adorno's refusal to certify progress as accomplished fact is sufficient to escape, rather than merely qualify, a Eurocentric inheritance.

These tensions mark not failures of the literature but its active frontiers. Three gaps deserve particular note. The relationship between *Naturgeschichte* and Anthropocene-era ecological crisis remains underdeveloped relative to its apparent promise, with Johnson's (2019) extension of negative universal history to climate catastrophe representing an early rather than settled contribution. The negative philosophy of history has had limited engagement with analytic debates on moral progress, leaving a body of parallel argument largely untapped. And postcolonial and feminist critiques, while increasingly prominent, remain under-integrated with mainstream Adorno scholarship rather than treated as internal to it.

The review closes, finally, by noting a reversal of fortune visible across the literature surveyed. For decades, Habermas's normative-deficit charge cast Adorno and Horkheimer's negative philosophy of history as a dead end to be superseded by communicative rationality. Under conditions of ecological catastrophe, renewed authoritarianism, and decolonial reckoning, that same negative, anti-progressivist stance is increasingly reassessed as a resource rather than a liability — in Allen's (2016) decolonial retrieval of Adorno against his own successors, in Freyenhagen's (2024) defense of the *Dialectic* as therapeutic rather than fatalistic, in Whyman's (2016) deflationary but still generative reading of natural history, and in Johnson's (2019) ecological extension of negative universal history. Whether this reassessment ultimately resolves the tensions identified above, or simply relocates them under new historical pressure, remains the question this literature leaves for further inquiry.
