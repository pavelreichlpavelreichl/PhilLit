## Section 5: Horkheimer's Trajectory — Materialism, the Critique of Reason, and the Theological Turn

Treating Horkheimer as an independent theorist of history — rather than as Adorno's co-author or the Institute's administrator — surfaces a developmental arc running from 1930s interdisciplinary materialism, through the wartime critique of instrumental reason, to a late pessimism inflected by negative theology. Whether this arc constitutes a single coherent trajectory or a series of ruptures, and how far its final stages are Horkheimer's own rather than artifacts of the Adorno collaboration, organizes the literature surveyed here.

### 5.1: The 1930s materialist programme

"Traditional and Critical Theory" functions as the domain's pivot text: it contrasts theory modeled on natural science with a Marxian mode that takes society as its own object and situates itself within history for emancipatory ends, while already shifting the critique of science from an external complaint about disciplinary fragmentation toward an internal one about formalism (Horkheimer 1982). The essays collected in *Between Philosophy and Social Science* — including "Materialism and Morality" and "On the Problem of Truth" — supply the primary-source basis for this program, conceiving truth as historically mediated yet answerable to an objective reality irreducible to thought, with philosophy's task the reduction of unnecessary suffering (Horkheimer 1993). Berendzen (2008) gives this materialism philosophical content by reconstructing it, via comparison with Bas van Fraassen's "empirical stance," as a considered postmetaphysical orientation toward inquiry rather than either a substantive metaphysical thesis or a mere refusal to theorize. The program's polemical edge against positivism has drawn historical scrutiny: O'Neill and Uebel (2004), recovering Otto Neurath's unpublished reply to Horkheimer's 1937 "Latest Attack on Metaphysics," argue that Horkheimer's caricature of logical positivism as reifying social reality was largely unfounded and reconstruct the debate about whether critique of science must proceed "from above" or "from within" that the two might otherwise have had. Handelman (2019) traces the same prewar confrontation with the Vienna Circle to show how it hardened into a broader historical narrative equating mathematized reason with the "downfall of Enlightenment," a narrative later canonized in the postwar texts. Against readings that treat the 1930s program as merely preparatory to *Dialectic of Enlightenment*, Abromeit (2011) reconstructs it as internally coherent and largely independent, showing that Horkheimer and Adorno did not begin working together closely until the late 1930s and that the Horkheimer-Fromm model of critical theory differs in crucial ways from what follows.

### 5.2: The wartime critique of reason

*Eclipse of Reason* traces the historical transformation of "objective reason" — reason capable of grasping ends and values — into "subjective reason," reduced to calculating means to unexamined ends, a formalization that Horkheimer argues culminates in a self-undermining rationality abetting domination and, at the limit, a "new barbarism" (Horkheimer 1947). Because Horkheimer explicitly withholds any positive program of action, the text has attracted the same normative-deficit objection leveled at *Dialectic of Enlightenment*. Freyenhagen (2023) answers this Habermasian charge by identifying a "linguistic turn avant la lettre" in Horkheimer's and Adorno's shared picture of language, arguing that the inseparability of human life-form and language, akin to the later Wittgenstein's view, makes language an inextinguishable reservoir of objective reason and thus a standard from which instrumental reason can still be criticized. Shea (2021), building on Berendzen's account of 1930s materialism as postmetaphysical, reaches the opposite assessment: the wartime and postwar critique of instrumental reason, he argues, paradoxically reintroduces the metaphysical commitments — a normative conception of nature, "objective reason" itself — that the earlier materialism had explicitly foresworn, generating unresolved tensions rather than a defensible development.

### 5.3: Late pessimism and the theological turn — continuity or rupture?

Horkheimer's postwar notebooks, collected in *Dawn and Decline*, register an increasingly pessimistic diagnosis of the "administered world" alongside recurring reflections on compassion and the limits of what critical theory can positively affirm (Horkheimer 1978). This late period crystallizes around the category of the "longing for the wholly other" — the desire, uncoupled from institutional religion, that history's injustice not be the final word — which Siebert (2005) reconstructs as a negative-theological resource for critical resistance rather than a lapse into dogma, and which Nagl-Docekal (2020), revisiting Horkheimer's 1970 *Spiegel* interview, traces to a Kantian conception of religion grounded in the needs of practical reason. Several accounts read the turn as continuous with Horkheimer's earlier commitments: Gunderson (2012) argues that pessimism about progress was always grounded in an abiding compassion for suffering running from the earliest to the latest writings, Walentowicz (2006) proposes an explicit three-stage periodization (prewar, wartime, postwar) unified by sustained self-critique and an unrelenting desire for a better world, and Michaelis (2022) qualifies rather than rejects this continuity narrative by specifying precisely what Horkheimer selectively appropriated from Schopenhauer's metaphysics of will and compassion. Crawford (2025) contests this consensus at the level of historiography: he argues that the "religious-metaphysical" late Horkheimer is not simply an immanent development but a Cold War-inflected construction of American reception history, one that relocated the Frankfurt School's foundations from Marxism and contemporary social problems to philosophy and religion. Stirk (1992) situates the same late period's political conservatism — opposition to the 1960s student movements and qualified support for American intervention in Vietnam — within, rather than as a betrayal of, Horkheimer's mature critical theory.

Because "the Frankfurt School" often collapses Horkheimer into Adorno, isolating Horkheimer's independent trajectory sharpens what in *Dialectic of Enlightenment* is genuinely co-authored versus individually held — a question relevant wherever the book's pessimism or its theological undertones are attributed to "critical theory" as such. The literature documents the developmental stages with some precision, but two questions remain open and comparatively under-theorized: whether continuity (Gunderson, Walentowicz, Michaelis) or historicized rupture (Crawford) better explains the late theological turn, and how far Horkheimer's own trajectory diverges from Adorno's negative philosophy of history examined in Section 3.
