## Section 1: The *Dialectic of Enlightenment* as Philosophy of History — Myth, Reason, and Self-Destruction

Horkheimer and Adorno's *Dialectic of Enlightenment* opens with a claim that reorganizes the entire subsequent debate over their philosophy of history: "myth is already enlightenment, and enlightenment reverts to mythology" (Horkheimer and Adorno 2020). This entwinement thesis denies that reason's emergence from myth constitutes a clean historical break; instead, the domination of external nature that enlightenment achieves through calculative, instrumental reason is bound up with a corresponding domination — ultimately a mutilation — of the inner nature of the subject who exercises that reason. The Odyssey excursus, read as the "basic text" of Western subjectivity, and the later chapters on the culture industry and antisemitism extend this genealogy from prehistoric ritual into industrial capitalism and fascism, presenting enlightenment's trajectory as one of self-destruction (Horkheimer and Adorno 2020). While this entwinement thesis is largely uncontested as the book's core claim, scholars diverge sharply over its *modality* — whether the narrative it tells is fatalistic, aporetic by design, disclosive, therapeutic, or merely preparatory — and this section traces that divergence across three interpretive layers.

### 1.1 The entwinement thesis and the domination of nature

The bare formula "myth is already enlightenment" invites a two-term reading in which mimesis (magical, imitative comportment toward nature) is simply displaced by instrumental rationality. Hulatt (2016) challenges this simplification, arguing that the book's genealogy is better modeled as a tripartite conflict among mimesis, rationality, and self-preservation understood as an independent ordering principle, rather than as an internally driven development from mimesis to instrumental reason alone. On Hulatt's account, self-preservation functions as a controlling force that intensifies the conflict between mimesis and rationality, a tension only partially relaxed in bourgeois autonomous art's later incorporation of mimetic comportment. This revision matters because it resists collapsing the entwinement thesis into a single deterministic mechanism, leaving room for mimesis to retain unresolved critical residue rather than being fully absorbed into domination.

A parallel complication concerns the status of "nature" itself. Nelson (2011) argues that nature functions doubly in Adorno and the Frankfurt School: as an ideology-critical category (unmasking naturalizing legitimations of social domination) and as an ethical category (registering what is lost or repressed through the domination of outer and inner nature). Nelson contends that these two functions are not always clearly distinguished in *Dialectic of Enlightenment* or in its reception, a conflation that bears on how strongly the domination-of-nature thesis should be read — as historical-sociological explanation, as moral indictment, or as both simultaneously. Stone (2014) situates this same dialectical reversal — domination over nature reverting into domination by nature — within Adorno's broader, ambivalent relation to Hegel, arguing that Adorno retains Hegelian dialectic's demand for reconciliation of contradictions while breaking with Hegel's *identity thinking*; for Adorno, reconciled differences co-exist as differences rather than being unified into a totality. This Hegelian reframing already anticipates the modality question taken up in 1.3, since it recasts the entwinement thesis as internally consistent dialectical logic rather than pure fatalism.

### 1.2 The *Odyssey* excursus as genealogy of the subject

The Odyssey excursus supplies the book's central case study, reading Odysseus as a prototype of bourgeois subjectivity: self-preservation through renunciation and calculated sacrifice constitutes both his mastery over external nature and his self-domination. Comay (2000) offers a close reading of the Sirens episode as the emblematic instance of this logic: Odysseus's cunning self-binding, undertaken to escape bondage to the Sirens' song, unleashes what she calls an "endless ritual of sado-masochistic bondage games" turned against the self — reason becoming unreason at its own limit, and self-domination culminating in self-mutilation. This reading exemplifies the excursus's double movement, whereby the conquest of an external Other (nature, the feminine, pleasure) is purchased through an internal violence the subject inflicts upon itself.

Allen (2024) complicates the standard characterization of Odysseus as merely an instrumental-reason prototype, arguing that readings focused solely on calculation overlook the role of *cunning* (*metis*) in his characterization. Cunning, on Allen's account, is itself a form of mimetic intelligence bearing its own internal dialectic, distinct from bare instrumental calculation, with consequences for how Adorno's later account of non-identity thinking should be understood. This revisionist emphasis converges with Hulatt's (2016) tripartite model in resisting a flattened, purely instrumental reading of the excursus's protagonist.

Bermingham (2018) extends the genealogy-of-the-subject reading through a feminist immanent critique, arguing that Helen of Argos and Troy — conspicuously absent from both the excursus itself and prior feminist commentary on it — is nonetheless central to its thesis. Bermingham contends that Helen exhibits, as much as Odysseus does, the incipient features of an individuated and alienated bourgeois subject, and that her exclusion from Horkheimer and Adorno's analysis exposes an undertheorization of women's experience of subjectivity within the excursus's framework. This reading supplements rather than rejects the entwinement thesis, identifying a structural gap in its historical narrative of subject-formation rather than repudiating the narrative's basic mechanism.

### 1.3 What kind of "philosophy of history"?

Beyond the content of the entwinement thesis, commentators disagree over what genre of historical argument the book advances. Foster (2001) classifies it as genealogy critique in a broadly Nietzschean vein: rather than offering linear causal history or a normatively grounded argument, the book traces the contingent, power-laden emergence of instrumental rationality and the modern subject, exposing costs concealed within reason's self-understanding as progress. Honneth (2000) proposes an alternative classification, reading the text as a "disclosing critique" that deploys rhetorical means — narration, myth, chiasmus, exaggeration — "in order to evoke a new way of seeing the social world," a mode of argument that is world-disclosing rather than propositionally grounded and that sidesteps the demand for normative foundations. Noppen (2012) offers an intermediate reading, treating the book as propaedeutic and diagnostic — preparing, but not yet elaborating, a "positive concept of enlightenment" — such that it should be assessed as an unfinished project rather than as either self-refuting or covertly complete.

Evans (2020) proposes a distinct, genre-based account, reading the book in part as a parody of anti-enlightenment thought in the tradition of Spengler and Klages, with its rhetorical excess and totalizing pronouncements deliberately styled to mimic and thereby undercut cultural-pessimist rhetoric from within. Kent (2024), building on Hulatt's tripartite model, argues against a purely pessimistic reading on different grounds: myth functions not only as what enlightenment has always already been, but also as an incomplete, largely untapped mediator of human experience through language, offering "momentary reflections" of relinquished possibilities of life and thus a renewed chance of enlightenment. Rabinbach (2000) extends the question of the narrative's scope to the "Elements of Anti-Semitism" chapter, arguing that Horkheimer and Adorno treat antisemitism not as contingent political pathology but as an outgrowth of the same historical dialectic of self-preservation and domination traced throughout the book — a move that tests how far the genealogy's explanatory reach extends, from prehistoric myth to twentieth-century genocide. Setiya (2023), writing from outside Critical Theory, engages this same question of scope from an analytic ethical-naturalist standpoint: he defends a form of ethical naturalism in which ethical knowledge is grounded in a socially and historically determined human nature, without lapsing into relativism, and credits Adorno and the Frankfurt School with correctly identifying that once society becomes "total," critique can only proceed immanently.

These competing genre-classifications converge on a single unresolved pivot, first named by Habermas (1987). Habermas argues that *Dialectic of Enlightenment* levels a totalizing critique of reason that performatively contradicts itself: the book "denounces the Enlightenment's becoming totalitarian with its own tools," yet, having conflated instrumental rationality with rationality as such, it is left without normative grounds for its own critique and can only "practice determinate negation on an ad hoc basis." This charge — that the entwinement thesis, taken to its totalizing conclusion, undermines the very standpoint from which it is issued — reframes every genre-classification surveyed above as an implicit answer to Habermas: genealogy critique, disclosive critique, propaedeutic reading, parody, and anti-fatalist recovery of myth each represent a distinct strategy for showing that the book's historical narrative escapes self-refutation. This review returns to adjudicate the performative-contradiction charge directly in Section 7; here it is introduced only as the pivot around which the competing readings organize themselves.

**Connection.** The same text that yields a settled entwinement thesis yields a persistently contested account of its modality: read one way, the narrative of myth reverting to enlightenment and enlightenment reverting to myth is a totalizing dead end that forecloses the very critical resources it deploys; read another way, its aporias, ambiguities, and rhetorical strategies are deliberate features of a critical resource that remains open rather than fatalistic. The entwinement thesis is established as the book's core; whether that thesis is fatalist or emancipatory, argumentative or performative, remains the review's live and recurring question.
