## Section 4: The Hegelian-Marxist Legacy — Continuities and Breaks

Any assessment of Adorno's and Horkheimer's philosophy of history requires a baseline: what, precisely, do they inherit from Hegel and Marx, and at what point does inheritance give way to departure? The literature converges on two inherited commitments — determinate negation and immanent critique — while disputing both their original meaning in Hegel and the degree to which critical theory's refusal of reconciliation constitutes a genuine break or an internal transformation of the same problematic.

### 4.1: The transformation of Hegel's dialectic

Marcuse's reading of Hegel as a theorist of the "power of negative thinking" — a dialectic that denounces the given as merely apparent and points beyond it — established the founding Frankfurt School template for treating Hegelian negativity as inherently critical rather than affirmative (Marcuse 1941). This "positive" appropriation, transmitted to Marx and available for critical theory's own use, functions as the baseline against which Adorno's more skeptical treatment of Hegel is measured. Stone (2014) argues that Adorno's apparently mixed responses to Hegel — endorsing the dialectical process by which enlightenment reverts to myth while rejecting Hegel's dialectic as the ultimate form of "identity thinking" — cohere once reconciliation itself is redefined: where Hegel's reconciliation unites differences into a whole, Adorno's leaves differences co-existing as differences. Finlayson (2014) presses harder on the inherited narrative, isolating what he calls the "Continuity Thesis" — the assumption that Hegel invented immanent criticism, Marx developed it, and the Frankfurt School (especially Adorno) merely refined the same method — and arguing that Adorno's practice diverges from Hegel's in ways the thesis obscures. His companion survey (Finlayson 2017) extends this differentiation across generations, finding the young Horkheimer a qualified Hegelian-Marxist who treats Hegel's idealist metaphysics as superseded by sociology and materialist historiography, Adorno a more committed Hegelian who nonetheless rejects Hegel's philosophy of objective spirit, and Marcuse a more charitable reader emphasizing reason's critical moment. Rose (2009) supplies an independent philosophical test: she argues that Weberian and neo-Kantian sociology — including strands of Western Marxism — loses Hegel's speculative insight that social forms can be grasped only by thinking identity and non-identity together, raising the question of whether critical theory's negative dialectic radicalizes this speculative logic or retreats from it into dualism. Marasco (2015) reframes the entire transformation around Hegel's own trope of the "highway of despair" from the Phenomenology's introduction, arguing that critical theory's most consequential move is to inhabit Hegelian negativity rather than resolve it — accepting negativity without the promise of Absolute reconciliation that structures Hegel's system, against later normative-reconstructive critical theory that seeks a way out of despair.

### 4.2: Marxism, totality, and the vanishing subject

If the Hegel question concerns method, the Marx question concerns the fate of totality and its bearer. Jay (1984) traces totality's "adventures" as the concept migrates from Lukács's identification of totality with the standpoint of the proletarian subject-object of history, through Korsch and the early Frankfurt School, to Habermas's eventual abandonment of strong totality claims for differentiated communicative rationality — treating Adorno's dictum that "the whole is the untrue" as one moment in this adventure rather than a simple rejection of the concept. This genealogical account is grounded institutionally by Jay (1973), who documents how the Institute for Social Research developed a program of "interdisciplinary materialism" deliberately distinct from Soviet economism and Second International determinism, while remaining indebted to Lukács's and Korsch's Hegelian-Marxist revisions of the 1920s; Wiggershaus (1994) supplies further archival grounding for tracing when the Institute's Marxist self-understanding, including expectations about proletarian revolutionary agency, gave way to the more skeptical diagnosis of Dialectic of Enlightenment. Feenberg (2014) synthesizes this trajectory as a single "philosophy of praxis" running from Marx through Lukács's reification theory to the Frankfurt School, arguing that Adorno's and Marcuse's engagement with nature and rationality is best read as an internal transformation of Lukács's subject-object problematic rather than a rupture with it — though Adorno, taking commodity fetishism as his starting point and eschewing appeals to emancipation or revolution, emerges in Feenberg's account as something of an outlier within the tradition he transforms. The origin of the "identical subject-object" thesis itself is reconstructed by Arato and Breines (1979), who trace Lukács's Hegelian-Marxist synthesis from Neo-Kantian and Romantic anti-capitalism to History and Class Consciousness, and by Löwy (1979), who situates the same trajectory within a broader "Romantic anti-capitalist" critique of reified modernity. Both accounts frame why Horkheimer and Adorno could not simply adopt Lukács's proletariat-as-subject thesis once that subject failed to materialize historically.

### 4.3: What survives of Marxian political economy?

The question of what remains once the revolutionary subject recedes is answered differently depending on whether attention turns to value theory or to reification. Postone (1993) reinterprets Marx's mature critique of political economy as a theory of abstract, quasi-automatic domination exercised by the value-form over social life, arguing that traditional Marxism mistakenly locates capitalism's contradiction in the standpoint of labor rather than treating labor itself as a historically specific category to be overcome — a reconstruction that supplies resources for understanding capital's subjectless domination compatible with Adorno's and Horkheimer's skepticism about a guaranteed revolutionary agent. Lichtenstein (2022) extends this line directly into Adorno scholarship, defending Adorno's theory of domination as structural and de-individualized against Honneth's charge of theoretical underdevelopment, and arguing that Marx's value theory — locating value's constitution in the social totality rather than production or exchange alone — supplies the explanatory account of abstraction that Adorno's own texts leave undeveloped. Schmidt (1971), writing from within the Frankfurt School itself, reconstructs Marx's historical materialism as resting on a dialectical concept of nature — worked-on and historically mediated, yet never reducible to social construction — that underlies the School's non-reductive materialism and its critique of the domination of nature. On the reification side, Stahl (2018) offers a balanced reception history of Lukács's influence, noting that his theory of reification inspired the first generation's diagnosis of capitalist modernity even as his political conclusions prompted critical distancing, while Kavoulakos (2017) traces reification's specific conceptual afterlife across generations, from interdisciplinary materialism through Honneth's recognition-theoretic reformulation. Two recent studies test whether negative totality can be detached from the proletarian subject altogether: Gindner (2023) argues that the theoretical work in History and Class Consciousness is done by totality rather than class or reification, and that totality can be actualized in a strictly negative sense — as capitalist coercion to be overcome — without an identifiable revolutionary agent, a strategy converging with, though not directly engaging, Adorno's own negative dialectic. Bueno (2024) reads Lukács and Adorno comparatively, finding that both arrive, by opposite paths, at an elimination of the subject-object distinction that threatens political impasse, but reconstructs both arguments as capable of preserving emancipatory subjectivity via the "non-identical" rather than negating it.

Taken together, these three subsections indicate that the break with Marxist teleology need not entail abandoning the philosophy of history so much as reconstituting it negatively: totality, dialectic, and even value-form domination persist as diagnostic categories once their positive, subject-guaranteeing functions are relinquished. The literature is broadly consistent in treating critical theory as an internal transformation, not a rupture, of the Hegel-Lukács-Marx lineage — Feenberg's continuity thesis, Jay's "adventures" of totality, and Gindner's and Bueno's detachment of totality from the proletariat all converge on this picture, even where Finlayson and Rose complicate how continuous the underlying method really is. What decisively separates Adorno and Horkheimer from their predecessors is not the abandonment of Hegelian negativity or Marxian categories of domination, but the fate of the revolutionary subject: once the proletariat can no longer be relied upon as history's identical subject-object, totality survives only as a negative, subjectless diagnosis of unfreedom — the condition that Sections 3 and 5 will show shaping Adorno's and Horkheimer's divergent responses.
