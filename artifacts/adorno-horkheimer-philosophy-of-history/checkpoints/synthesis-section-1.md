## Introduction

Theodor W. Adorno and Max Horkheimer write within, and against, the German tradition of speculative philosophy of history inaugurated by Kant's idea of a universal history with a cosmopolitan intent, elaborated by Hegel's philosophy of Spirit, and transformed by Marx's account of historical materialism. That tradition supplies the categories — progress, universal history, totality, a world-historical subject — that organize its narratives of human development. Horkheimer and Adorno's *Dialectic of Enlightenment* ([1944] 2002) inherits these categories but redeploys them genealogically against their own optimistic use: reason's historical trajectory from mythic ritual to Enlightenment and beyond is read as a movement of domination that reverts to the mythic violence it claims to have overcome (Horkheimer and Adorno 2002). This review asks what kind of intellectual project results. Is the outcome a substantive, if negative, philosophy of history that retains the genre's ambitions while inverting its content? Is it instead a meta-critique that dismantles the presuppositions of philosophy of history as such — its subject, its teleology, its claim to totality? Or does it amount to what O'Connor (2008) and Sherman (2016) characterize, in different registers, as a "melancholy science" that holds the question of history's meaning in suspension rather than resolving it in either direction? This guiding tension threads through every section below.

Four cross-cutting problems recur across the literature surveyed here. First is the entwinement of myth and enlightenment — the thesis that mythic domination and enlightened rationality are not successive stages but reciprocally implicated moments of a single historical logic. Second is the dissolution of the antithesis between nature and history, first programmatically stated in Adorno's 1932 lecture on the idea of natural history (Adorno [1932] 1984) and subsequently traced through his mature work. Third is the pairing of progress and catastrophe within a "totality without a subject": Adorno's negative philosophy of history retains totalizing categories — universal history, the whole — while denying history any constituting collective subject, a construction whose internal coherence is contested. Fourth is the normative status of a critique that appears to totalize even as it attacks totalization, a problem posed most forcefully by Habermas's (1987) charge that the *Dialectic*'s critique of reason is performatively self-undermining.

The review preserves internal differentiation that is easily flattened under the label "Frankfurt School": Adorno's and Horkheimer's positions diverge in emphasis and trajectory even within jointly authored work; Walter Benjamin functions as both formative source and critical foil for their historiography; both figures rework a shared Hegelian-Marxist baseline; and the reception, from Habermas (1987) onward, has itself become a site of dispute, including recent decolonial reassessments of the category of progress (Allen 2016).

The review proceeds in seven sections: the *Dialectic of Enlightenment*'s entwinement thesis (1); Adorno's natural-history concept (2); his negative categories of progress, universal history, and totality (3); the Hegelian-Marxist legacy (4); Horkheimer's independent trajectory (5); Benjamin as source and foil (6); and the reception debate (7). Consistent with the priority on comprehensive and balanced coverage, the review deliberately over-samples critical and dissenting literature, so that objections to the Frankfurt position receive treatment proportionate to its defenses rather than being treated as an afterthought.
