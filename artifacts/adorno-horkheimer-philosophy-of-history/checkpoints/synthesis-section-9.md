## Conclusion

This review opened with a guiding meta-question: does early Frankfurt School critical theory offer a philosophy of history, or a critique of the genre as such? Across seven domains — the entwinement thesis of the *Dialectic*, natural history, Adorno's negative categories of progress and universal history, the Hegelian-Marxist legacy, Horkheimer's independent trajectory, Benjamin as source and foil, and the reception literature — the literature converges, without fully resolving, on a qualified answer. Adorno and Horkheimer are most defensibly read not as proponents of a rival, substantive philosophy of history standing alongside Kant's, Hegel's, or Marx's, but as practitioners of an immanent, negative critique of the genre's guiding categories — progress, universal history, totality, the world-historical subject — that nonetheless proves unable, or unwilling, to relinquish those categories outright. Progress is neither affirmed nor denied but held together with catastrophe (Section 3); universal history is retained "negatively," as O'Connor's (2008) framing of Adorno's project already anticipates, rather than abandoned as Lyotard's post-metanarrative stance would recommend; totality survives as "the whole is the false" even as its constituting subject disappears. The categories persist, in other words, under erasure: disavowed as achieved fact, retained as critical and normative pressure.

That formulation, however, names a settlement point around which several tensions remain genuinely unresolved rather than a stable consensus. First, the coherence question raised in Section 3 persists: whether a negative totality without a subject is a workable philosophical construction or merely a placeholder for an argument critical theory has not completed. Second, the modality question from Section 1 recurs in the reception literature of Section 7: whether the totalizing sweep of the entwinement thesis is self-undermining, as Habermas charged, or deliberately aporetic — a therapeutic destabilization of complacent narratives of moral progress, as Freyenhagen (2024) and others contend. Third, Section 5 registered how readily the "Adorno-Horkheimer" position collapses into a single undifferentiated voice, obscuring real divergences in trajectory, tone, and theological commitment between the two authors. Fourth, the secularization question from Section 6 remains open: whether the negative philosophy of history is Benjaminian messianism carried forward under a materialist disguise or a position that has expelled the redemptive register it cannot fully justify. Fifth, the decolonial critique surveyed in Section 7 exposes a universal-history residue that survives even sympathetic reconstructions, pressing the question of whether Adorno's refusal to certify progress as accomplished fact is sufficient to escape, rather than merely qualify, a Eurocentric inheritance.

These tensions mark not failures of the literature but its active frontiers. Three gaps deserve particular note. The relationship between *Naturgeschichte* and Anthropocene-era ecological crisis remains underdeveloped relative to its apparent promise, with Johnson's (2019) extension of negative universal history to climate catastrophe representing an early rather than settled contribution. The negative philosophy of history has had limited engagement with analytic debates on moral progress, leaving a body of parallel argument largely untapped. And postcolonial and feminist critiques, while increasingly prominent, remain under-integrated with mainstream Adorno scholarship rather than treated as internal to it.

The review closes, finally, by noting a reversal of fortune visible across the literature surveyed. For decades, Habermas's normative-deficit charge cast Adorno and Horkheimer's negative philosophy of history as a dead end to be superseded by communicative rationality. Under conditions of ecological catastrophe, renewed authoritarianism, and decolonial reckoning, that same negative, anti-progressivist stance is increasingly reassessed as a resource rather than a liability — in Allen's (2016) decolonial retrieval of Adorno against his own successors, in Freyenhagen's (2024) defense of the *Dialectic* as therapeutic rather than fatalistic, in Whyman's (2016) deflationary but still generative reading of natural history, and in Johnson's (2019) ecological extension of negative universal history. Whether this reassessment ultimately resolves the tensions identified above, or simply relocates them under new historical pressure, remains the question this literature leaves for further inquiry.
