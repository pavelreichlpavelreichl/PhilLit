## Section 6: Benjamin's Messianic Historiography — Source and Foil

Walter Benjamin occupies a distinctive position in this literature: neither a predecessor whose categories Adorno and Horkheimer straightforwardly inherit nor an outside critic, but a co-formative interlocutor whose historiography is at once absorbed into and resisted by the Frankfurt position. The literature organizes around three questions — what Benjamin's theses actually claim against progressive historicism; how far his constellational method can be reconciled with Adorno's demand for dialectical mediation; and whether the messianic-theological register Benjamin retains is adopted, transformed, or ultimately expelled by his Frankfurt readers.

### Subsection 6.1: The theses and the critique of progress

Löwy's (2005) line-by-line commentary on "On the Concept of History" reads the theses as a "fire alarm" sounded simultaneously against fascism and against the complacent evolutionism of Second International Marxism and Social Democracy. On Löwy's account, Benjamin's "weak messianic power" names not a transcendent redeemer but a collective, this-worldly capacity for interruption — a reading that fuses Jewish messianism and revolutionary materialism as mutually constitutive rather than as a theological veneer over materialist content. Pensky (2010) situates the ninth thesis's angel of history, its face turned toward the past as it is driven backward into the future by the storm "we call progress," within the Kantian tradition of philosophy of history, arguing that Benjamin's anti-progressivism transforms rather than simply ruptures the Kantian problematic of historical knowing that also informs Frankfurt School thought. Khatib (2017) develops the companion concept of the "tradition of the oppressed," reconstructing it as a discontinuous medium that cannot be represented as a single coherent world-history but only as a discontinuum of struggles and "empty spots" — a formulation that breaks with both historicism's homogeneous, empty time and orthodox historical materialism's view of tradition as a merely retarding force.

This anti-progressivist reading nonetheless leaves open how far Benjamin's messianism is materialist rhetoric or genuinely theological substance. Tiedemann (1989 [1983]) poses this dilemma directly in his "historical materialism or political messianism?" framing, arguing — from within the circle of Benjamin's literary executors — for the priority of the materialist reading while conceding the irreducibility of the theses' theological imagery. Wolin (1994) argues the opposite case, tracing a redemptive, quasi-theological impulse across Benjamin's ostensibly more materialist later phases and contending that this impulse cannot be excised without loss of critical force. The Tiedemann-Wolin dispute thus frames the interpretive stakes for everything that follows: whether Adorno and Horkheimer's own philosophy of history is best understood as a secularization of Benjaminian messianism or as its disavowal.

### Subsection 6.2: Constellation, dialectical image, and the mediation dispute

The Arcades Project supplies the primary textual site for this dispute. Buck-Morss (1989) reconstructs Benjamin's method as a "dialectics of seeing" — a materialist historiography built from the montage of concrete historical images rather than narrative argument, in which nineteenth-century Paris becomes legible as the prehistory of the present — and documents Adorno's objections to the 1935 Exposé, articulated in the so-called "Hornberg letter," that crystallized the demand for explicit conceptual and economic mediation. Recoder (2019) examines Adorno's editorial and critical interventions into the Arcades materials as expressions of what Adorno himself called his "Arcades orthodoxy": a methodological insistence on rigorous dialectical mediation that Benjamin's own practice persistently exceeded or resisted, and that Adorno extended into a broader metacritique of Benjamin's treatment of aesthetic autonomy. Feldman (2011) offers the most precise reconstruction of the resulting charge, showing that Adorno's recurring accusation that Benjamin is "not dialectical enough" is internally chiastic: Adorno faults Benjamin's aesthetics for surrendering the artwork to ideology while faulting his historiography for refusing historical narrative altogether — precisely the site where Benjamin locates ideology's operation. Feldman's analysis resists assigning victory to either side, instead showing the disagreement's internal structure as one of divergent, only partly compatible conceptions of autonomy and redemption. Comay (1999) sharpens the methodological stakes further, arguing that Benjamin's historiography deliberately courts a "perverse" relation to history — reproducing, rather than simply diagnosing, the fetishistic freezing of time it seeks to interrupt — which complicates any simple opposition between Benjamin's supposedly "immediate" dialectical images and Adorno's demand for mediation. Buck-Morss's (1977) earlier study of the origin of negative dialectics situates this dispute institutionally, arguing that Adorno's mature method cannot be understood apart from a decade of collaboration and disagreement with Benjamin conducted partly under the material conditions of Institute funding and editorial control.

### Subsection 6.3: Messianism retained or expelled?

Beardsworth (2005) compares Benjamin's dialectical image with Horkheimer and Adorno's fragmentary method in *Dialectic of Enlightenment* as parallel rather than derivative responses to a shared problem: how to make authoritative critical statements about history once the concept of history is itself understood as entangled with domination. On this reading, the fragmentary form of the *Dialectic* is a structural analogue to, and departure from, Benjamin's dialectical image, rather than a simple extension of it. Brittain (2010) contests Agamben's claim that Adorno's negative dialectics is "absolutely non-messianic," arguing instead — via the original Adorno-Benjamin debate over theology, dialectics, and politics — that Adorno's relation to the messianic is one of cautious, dialectically mediated retention rather than outright rejection. Horkheimer's position appears more resistant: Bohn (2019) documents Horkheimer's and Tiedemann's dismissal of Benjamin's concept of *Eingedenken* (remembrance) as merely theological or aesthetic rather than politically significant, and argues, against this secularizing critique, that the ethical force of *Eingedenken* depends precisely on embracing its theological dimension. Moir (2016) asks what remains usable in Benjamin's philosophy of history once separated from strict theological or strict materialist orthodoxy, contending — via Bloch and Osborne — that any interpretation of Benjamin's historical significance requires a "weakly teleological" concept of history akin to the one his own theses sought to dismantle.

Taken together, this literature positions Benjamin as source and foil: his critique of progress and his constellational method supply resources Adorno and Horkheimer draw on, while the mediation dispute and the divergent fates of *Eingedenken* in Horkheimer's and Adorno's hands show the Frankfurt School unable either fully to adopt or fully to abandon Benjamin's theological-redemptive register. The secularization question this raises directly informs Horkheimer's own theological turn and its contested continuity with his earlier materialism, and it bears on the coherence of Adorno's negative universal history, which likewise retains categories — redemption, interruption, the non-identical particular — that it cannot straightforwardly ground apart from the Benjaminian inheritance it partially disavows.
