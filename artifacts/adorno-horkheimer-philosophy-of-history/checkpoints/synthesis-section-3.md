## Section 2: Natural History (*Naturgeschichte*) — Dissolving the Nature/History Antithesis

If the *Dialectic of Enlightenment* narrates myth and enlightenment as dialectically entangled rather than successive, the conceptual apparatus that makes such entanglement thinkable is supplied a decade earlier, in Adorno's 1932 Frankfurt lecture "The Idea of Natural History." This section reconstructs that lecture's programme and sources, then asks whether "natural history" names a stable philosophical doctrine, a shifting family of motifs, or a method — before tracing its afterlife into Adorno's aesthetics, his engagement with Marx, and contemporary ecological and postcritical reappropriations.

### 2.1: The 1932 programme and its sources

Adorno's lecture argues that the received antithesis between nature — construed as static, ahistorical, mythically recurrent — and history — construed as the domain of meaningful human change — must be dialectically dissolved into a single category, "natural history": nature is shown to be historically produced and sedimented, while history recurs with a nature-like, mythic compulsion (Adorno 1984). The lecture is explicitly polemical on two fronts. Against Heidegger's fundamental ontology, Adorno charges that a supposedly historicized "being" smuggles back an ahistorical "meaning"; against historicism, he rejects any naive faith in meaningful progression (Adorno 1984). Escuela Cruz (2014) reconstructs this polemic as an "ontological reorientation of the philosophy of history" and identifies its two named philosophical antecedents: Lukács's concept of "second nature" from *Theory of the Novel* — the alienated, convention-bound social world confronting the modern subject as meaningless and as opaque as physical nature — and Benjamin's concept of allegory. Stahl's (2013) overview of Lukács's development situates second nature as the point where Lukács's later theory of reification first crystallizes, providing the Weberian-rationalization backdrop against which Adorno's radicalization operates. Buck-Morss (1977) supplies the biographical-intellectual context for this synthesis, documenting how Adorno's pre-exile writings of the late 1920s and early 1930s were shaped above all by Kracauer, Lukács, and Benjamin, and showing how Adorno's Benjaminian method exposes a timeless "second nature" beneath the apparently ahistorical dialectic of subjectivity in his contemporaneous Kierkegaard study — evidence that the 1932 lecture belongs to a broader, biographically traceable formation rather than standing as an isolated occasional piece.

### 2.2: Stable doctrine or shifting motif? — unity vs. therapy

Scholars diverge over what kind of philosophical claim natural history makes. Pensky (2004) distinguishes three strands operating under the concept across Adorno's career — a reflection on the normative and methodological bases of critical social science, a conception of critical memory oriented toward historical suffering, and the "mindfulness of nature in the subject" thesis of *Dialectic of Enlightenment* — and argues these strands are unified by the category of transience: a critical theory attentive to what is transient in history. This is a unificatory reading, treating natural history as a coherent, multiply-articulated concept. Whyman (2016) argues against readings of this kind, contending that the literature has failed to specify what natural history actually is or does, and proposing instead that it is best understood as a "therapeutic" concept whose function is to dissolve philosophical anxieties about the nature/history relation that would otherwise obstruct critical-theoretical understanding, rather than to advance a substantive metaphysical or historiographical thesis. Helmling (2003) approaches the same tension from the side of method rather than doctrine, examining Adorno's practice of "constellation" against Benjamin's "dialectical image." Adorno repeatedly characterizes Benjamin's thought as itself "natural history," drawn to the petrified and obsolete; Helmling traces Adorno's ambivalence toward the resulting stasis — the "Medusa-gaze" that petrifies its object — into the "dialectics at a standstill" structuring the *Dialectic of Enlightenment*'s account of progress reverting to regress. On this reading, natural history is constitutively bound to the question of how critical theory formally presents its objects, not reducible to either a unified doctrine or a purely dissolving therapy.

### 2.3: Persistence and afterlife — aesthetics, Marx, ecology

Natural history's categories persist well beyond 1932. Pensky (2021) argues that the lecture's core notion of transience supplies the interpretive resources for *Aesthetic Theory*'s otherwise obscure claim that artworks possess a "truth content" accessible only through philosophical interpretation, functioning there as ideology critique. Daniels (2020) similarly reads Adorno's account of natural beauty through the Adorno-Benjamin shared category of transience, while Gandesha (2004) extends the post-Heideggerian problematic comparatively to Arendt, arguing that both thinkers turn to Kant's Third Critique to locate a path beyond the "chiasmic" structure of nature and history left unresolved by Heidegger's *Destruktion*. Breitenstein (2021) locates the concept precisely within the second model of *Negative Dialectics*, where Adorno follows Marx's critique of political economy in criticizing the appearance of natural necessity (*Naturwüchsigkeit*) in social relations; she argues that Adorno's own dismissal of Marx's "language mannerisms" rests on a one-sided reading that underestimates the emancipatory potential of Marx's aesthetic mediation of social practice as natural history. Stone (2006) connects natural history to the disenchantment thesis of *Dialectic of Enlightenment*, showing how *Negative Dialectics* and *Aesthetic Theory* generate a critical re-enchantment in which natural beings appear meaningful as bearers of suffering. This ecological register is developed at length by Cook (2011), who compares Adorno's account of nature to social ecology, ecofeminism, and deep ecology, and by Wankhammer (2024), who mobilizes natural history as a corrective to postcritique's exhausted reflex of denaturalizing critique, applying it to environmental-justice politics; Crawford (2018) similarly defends the concept's continued applicability against claims of its obsolescence for contemporary art, and McCann (2024) extends the allegory/symbol axis comparatively to Ernst Jünger's "planetary history." Against this Adorno-centered trajectory, Friedlander (2024) argues that natural history is not merely a category Adorno adapted from Benjamin but an organizing problem running through Benjamin's own corpus — from the early writings on language and nature through Goethean "empiricism" to the Arcades Project's primal history — offering a Benjamin-centered counterweight to accounts that treat the concept as Adorno's intellectual property. This literature flags, without fully developing, the concept's application to the Anthropocene, a gap taken up in Section 3.

Across these disputes, the literature converges on natural history's centrality to Adorno's project and diverges on its status — doctrine, therapy, or method — and on how much credit belongs to Adorno versus Benjamin. This dissolution of the nature/history antithesis is the theoretical precondition for reading myth and enlightenment as dialectically entangled (Section 1) rather than as successive stages, and it anticipates the "world spirit without a subject" and negative-universal-history constructions examined in Section 3.
