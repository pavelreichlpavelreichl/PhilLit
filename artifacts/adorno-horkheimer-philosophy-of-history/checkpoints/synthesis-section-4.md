## Section 3: Adorno's Negative Philosophy of History — Progress, Catastrophe, Universal History, Totality without a Subject

Adorno's mature writings develop the categories inherited from the *Dialectic of Enlightenment* and from natural history into a more explicit, if still deliberately unstable, vocabulary: progress, universal history, and totality. Each term is retained rather than discarded, but retained under negation — a strategy that sharpens rather than resolves the review's guiding question of whether critical theory yields a substantive philosophy of history or a critique of the genre itself.

### Subsection 3.1: Progress reconstrued — neither affirmed nor denied

Adorno's 1962 essay "Progress" supplies the primary text for this refusal of affirmation and denial alike. Frelek (2025) reconstructs the essay's argument as holding history and catastrophe together without collapsing one into the other, situating Adorno's dialectical concept of progress within the theological genealogy of progress-thinking and its bearing on the dialectic of freedom and unfreedom; Frelek further extends this "negative hope" reading to the contemporary climate crisis, treating Adorno's non-affirmative, non-nihilistic stance as still actionable rather than merely diagnostic. Hohendahl (2013) situates the same essay as an implicit dialogue with Augustine, Kant, and Benjamin, arguing that Adorno's refusal of both linear-teleological and cyclical models of history draws on this longer theological-philosophical lineage — Augustinian providence, Kantian moral progress, Benjaminian messianic interruption — to fashion a concept of progress that negates each model's closure while retaining something of its ethical force. Çıdam (2020) identifies a "two-track" structure internal to Adorno's progress-talk: it functions simultaneously as the "new categorical imperative" (the post-Auschwitz injunction that Auschwitz not repeat) and as a politics of remembrance, so that Adorno's remarks on progress are not a single unified thesis about historical development but a heterogeneous cluster of ethical and memorial functions. This heterogeneity is corroborated from outside the Adorno literature by Weisbrod (2020), who reconstructs a "missed connection" between Adorno and Karl Löwith, co-panelists at the 1962 congress on progress: Löwith, Weisbrod argues, had already diagnosed the three problems Adorno's dialectical account attempts to solve, and each thinker is sympathetic to an aspect of the other's position — Löwith to the dialectical structure of Adorno's progress, Adorno to Löwith's insistence on progress's redemptive aspect. The decisive disagreement Weisbrod locates is over the status of suffering in history: for Löwith an ontological condition (secularized eschatology), for Adorno a political condition to be struggled against and overcome. This comparison clarifies what is distinctively non-theological, or at least non-fatalistic, in Adorno's retention of a redemptive moment within progress.

### Subsection 3.2: Universal history after Auschwitz

Adorno's negative retention of universal history follows the same logic. Burdman (2020) compares Adorno's and Lyotard's reconceptions of historical writing after Auschwitz, treating both as responses to a founding, unrepresentable wrong; Burdman's comparison clarifies that Adorno, unlike Lyotard's differend-based rejection of grand narrative, negatively retains a universal-historical framework rather than abandoning it outright — history remains tracked as a continuity, but one whose unity is constituted by catastrophe rather than by progress. Sherman (2016) names this stance a "qualified rejection of universal history," situating it as one theme among several — alongside the critique of idealist conceptuality, the critique of enlightenment reason's entanglement with capitalist society, and the concept of the constellation — that together constitute negative dialectics' response to a history that has failed to actualize its own prognostications. Johnson (2019) extends this negative universal history into the Anthropocene, arguing that Adorno's framework can accommodate humankind's compulsion to think itself as a unified geological agent while nonetheless resisting the totalizing ambitions of Enlightenment universal history; a "negative universal history" of the Anthropocene, on this reading, tracks planetary-scale forces in their uneven, local manifestations rather than positing a single historical subject or telos. Luna Jiménez (2020) supplies the developmental frame for these claims, tracing the nature/history antithesis from the 1932 "Idea of Natural History" through its reformulation in *Dialectic of Enlightenment* and *Negative Dialectics*, and arguing that this dialectical treatment underwrites Adorno's later capacity to speak of a "universal history" that is negative in structure yet still oriented toward the human/nature relationship — a continuity that bears directly on the ecological extension developed by Bell and Frelek.

### Subsection 3.3: Totality without a subject; the Hegelian reworking

The sharpest test of coherence concerns totality. Stone (2014) argues that Adorno's apparently mixed responses to Hegel form a coherent whole: Adorno follows Hegelian dialectical logic in tracing how enlightenment reverts to myth and domination over nature reverts into domination by nature, while rejecting Hegel's dialectic as the culmination of identity thinking. The crucial reworking, on Stone's account, concerns reconciliation itself — where Hegel's reconciliation unites differences into a totalized whole, Adorno's reconciled differences co-exist as differences, without subsumption. Stone deploys this reshaping to defend Adorno's consistency against Habermas's performative-contradiction charge (a debate taken up further in Section 7), suggesting that a negative totality is coherent precisely because it abandons the unifying, subject-constituted whole rather than merely disguising one. Baumann (2011) approaches the same terrain through the abstract/concrete universal distinction: Adorno, on this account, adopts Hegel's view that the abstract universal is the form of false knowledge and society while the concrete universal names a positive aim, but rejects Hegel's own construal of the concrete universal in favor of an alternative. Baumann leaves open whether this alternative overcomes the problems Adorno identifies in Hegel or remains structurally bound to them — a question that, left unresolved, keeps the coherence of "negative totality" an open interpretive matter rather than a settled result. O'Connor (2011) offers a broadly sympathetic overview of the same transformation, reading Adorno's negative dialectic as operating without a guaranteeing subject or absolute spirit, and situating this as an internal transformation of Hegel's method rather than its wholesale rejection. Allen (2025) approaches the question from a different angle, reading Adorno's engagement with Kant's Critique of Judgment as opening the possibility of thinking history "without the whole" — without unity or purpose — an aesthetically grounded alternative to the totalizing philosophies of history in Hegel, Marx, and (implicitly) Lyotard. Bell (2017) closes the loop back to natural history, using Adorno's collapse of the nature/history antithesis as a lens for ecological catastrophe and thereby linking the totality-without-a-subject problem to the progress/catastrophe pairing examined in 3.1.

Taken together, these accounts converge on describing Adorno's operation — negation and retention at once — with increasing precision, while diverging on whether the resulting "negative totality without a subject" is a stable philosophical achievement or an unresolved tension inherited from Hegel. This is the review's meta-question in its sharpest form: whether Adorno offers a substantive negative doctrine of history, a meta-critique that dismantles the genre from within, or a therapeutic suspension of the question altogether. The comparison with Hegel and Marx developed in Section 4 supplies the genealogical baseline needed to assess these readings, and the reception debate in Section 7 returns to adjudicate whether Habermas's performative-contradiction charge, which Stone's reading already anticipates, ultimately stands.
