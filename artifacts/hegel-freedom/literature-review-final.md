## Introduction

Hegel's theory of freedom poses a distinctive interpretive challenge. He neither defends the liberal picture of freedom as the individual's independence from social constraint, nor does he straightforwardly endorse a communitarian view in which the individual's ends are simply absorbed into those of the community. This refusal of easy classification is not evasion but the consequence of a systematic philosophical position: that the standard alternatives rest on a false metaphysics of the self as pre-social atom, and that any adequate account of freedom must explain how individuals can be genuinely at home in social arrangements without ceasing to be self-determining. The secondary literature reflects the genuine difficulty of locating Hegel within recognizable categories. From Popper's (1945) charge that Hegel's glorification of the rational state prepared the ground for twentieth-century totalitarianism, through Taylor's (1975) expressivist communitarianism and Wood's (1990) normative-ethical reconstruction, to Pippin's (2008) argument that Hegel's practical philosophy is best read as a social theory of rational agency, the field has been marked by sharp disagreements that are not merely exegetical but philosophically substantive.

Three interpretive fault lines structure the contemporary debate. The first concerns whether Hegel's account of freedom requires commitment to metaphysical idealism — to the claim that Geist is the fundamental ontological category whose self-realization is freedom — or whether it can be reconstructed in social-normative terms, as an account of the conditions under which rational self-determination is achieved, without any appeal to absolute spirit. Pippin's non-metaphysical reading and Beiser's (2005) insistence on the speculative commitments of Hegel's system define the poles of this dispute. The second fault line is political: whether Hegel's social conception of freedom is compatible with robust individual rights, political pluralism, and subjective self-expression, or whether it tends — despite revisionist protestations — toward a holism that subordinates individual freedom to the claims of the rational state. The third concerns the role of recognition (Anerkennung): whether mutual recognition is constitutive of freedom, such that freedom is literally not possible without the recognitive relations institutionalized in family, civil society, and state, or whether recognition is one institutional pathway among others through which a freedom already partially defined at the individual level is given its fullest social expression. Honneth's (2014) neo-Hegelian reconstruction makes recognition the normative core; Neuhouser's (2000) social-freedom thesis centers the institutional conditions; and Nance (2016) has argued that these positions, while often presented as rivals, rest on a distinction — between freedom as non-alienation and freedom as social freedom — that is itself unresolved in Hegel's texts.

These three axes are mutually entangled. One's reading of Hegel's metaphysics tends to shape one's view of his politics: if Geist requires an ontological framework irreducible to the social-normative, then the state's claim to embody rational freedom carries a weight that a deflationary reading cannot sustain. And both the metaphysical and political questions bear on the role of recognition: whether recognition is constitutive or instrumental depends in part on whether freedom's conditions are understood in terms of the self-realization of Spirit or in terms of the social-normative achievement of rational agency. This review traces the connections across all three axes while maintaining the distinctions among them.

The review proceeds in four substantive steps. Section 1 expounds Hegel's positive account with analytical precision, attending to the tripartite structure of the will and the progressive movement from Abstract Right through Morality to Sittlichkeit. Section 2 reconstructs Hegel's critiques of negative liberty, Willkür, and abstract Kantian formalism, showing how these rejections also mark his debts to Kant and Rousseau. Section 3 traces the developmental arc from the Phenomenology of Spirit through the Science of Logic to the Philosophy of Right, examining the continuity debate and the question of whether the mature Hegel represents a conservative stabilization of the earlier dialectic. Section 4 surveys the three scholarly controversies — metaphysical, political, and recognitive — as live and interconnected philosophical debates. Throughout, the review is guided by the claim that Hegel's concept of freedom remains philosophically productive precisely because it forces the question of whether freedom can be adequately theorized at the level of the individual alone.

## Section 1: The Structure of Hegel's Conception of Freedom

Hegel's account of freedom opens with a deceptively simple claim: that the will, when genuinely free, is "with itself in its other" — bei sich selbst im Anderen. The formula signals that freedom is not a condition of isolation from determination but of a particular relation to it. To understand what this means, and why it marks a departure from both liberal voluntarism and Kantian formalism, one must follow Hegel through the tripartite structure of the will that grounds the entire argument of the Philosophy of Right. Universality, particularity, and individuality are not three separate faculties but three moments of a single, self-developing structure: the will in its moment of universality is pure self-relation, the capacity to abstract from any and all determinate content; in its moment of particularity, it is the moment of determinate drive, desire, and inclination — the will as directed toward some specific object; in its moment of individuality, these two are unified in a concrete self-determination that is no longer abstract but actual. As Yeomans (2011) shows, this tripartite structure is not merely political scaffolding but is grounded in the logical structure of reflection in the Science of Logic: the free will is the logical structure of self-positing particularity within a universal, and the moments of the will replicate the dialectic of essence, appearance, and actuality at the level of practical philosophy. The upshot is that a will that remains at the level of universality alone — capable of abstracting from everything — is not yet genuinely free but only formally free. And a will that remains at the level of particularity alone — driven by desire without rational self-legislation — is equally unfree, determined from without rather than from within.

The philosophical payoff of this structure is the concept of "mediated immediacy": the genuinely free will is not the will that has escaped determination but the will that, having passed through determination, finds itself fully at home in it. Wood (1990) reconstructs this as Hegel's central claim against Kantian abstraction — that genuine rational agency is not the purely formal capacity to legislate from nowhere but the actualization of rational self-determination in a determinate social context. Pippin (2008) presses this further, reading the Philosophy of Right as a theory of rational agency in which the "actualization condition" is doing the most work: to be genuinely free is not merely to have the capacity for self-determination but to exercise that capacity in social practices that recognize and sustain it. On Pippin's reading, Hegel's departure from Kant is not a retreat from rational autonomy but its radicalization — autonomy must be concretely embodied in recognized institutions to be more than an aspiration. This yields a conception of freedom that is relational and institutional in a way that distinguishes it sharply from the "inner citadel" version of positive liberty that Isaiah Berlin criticized. Berlin's target was the idea that freedom consists in self-mastery over the passional self, with the rational self as a kind of inner sovereign. Hegel's account is not of this form: the self that is free is not one that has retreated inward to govern its desires but one that has found itself at home in the external social world. Patten (2002) captures this by describing Hegel's concept of rational self-determination as requiring both the subjective condition of rational self-legislation and the objective condition of actualization in social institutions — neither condition is reducible to the other, and neither alone is sufficient.

The structural account of the will provides the framework for understanding the move from Abstract Right through Morality to Sittlichkeit. This progression is not a narrative of increasing restriction but of increasingly adequate modes of freedom. Abstract Right — the sphere of property, contract, and formal personhood — instantiates the moment of universality: persons are recognized as bearers of rights irrespective of their particular social identities. Morality — the sphere of conscience, intention, and moral self-assessment — instantiates the moment of particularity: the subject insists on her right to act only on principles she can reflectively endorse. Both are genuine, indispensable moments of freedom, but each is one-sided. Abstract Right provides no content for choice; Morality provides subjective content but cannot generate determinate obligations without reference to existing social forms. Sittlichkeit — ethical life, the institutions of family, civil society, and state — is not a cancellation of these earlier moments but their "truth": the social context in which abstract right and moral subjectivity are simultaneously preserved and completed. Wood (1990) emphasizes that Hegel's critique of Kantian morality is not that moral self-legislation is wrong but that it is insufficient — morality taken in isolation cannot specify what one ought to do and generates the pathological possibility of moral fanaticism. Hardimon (1994) develops what he calls the "reconciliation thesis": that the modern rational state is the social world in which individuals can genuinely be at home, not alienated from their social roles but finding in those roles the expression of their own freedom.

The most philosophically contentious element of this account is what Neuhouser (2000) calls the constitutive thesis: that the institutions of Sittlichkeit are not merely instrumental protectors of a freedom that pre-exists them but are conditions of possibility for freedom as such. On this reading, family, civil society, and state do not stand in a merely external relation to individual freedom — enabling it, protecting it, providing its occasion — but partly constitute the kind of willing that freedom requires. Neuhouser reconstructs this thesis with considerable philosophical rigor, arguing that the tripartite structure of subjective, objective, and absolute freedom in Hegel maps onto the three spheres of ethical life in a way that makes each sphere the actualization of a distinct but necessary dimension of free willing. This is a strong and controversial claim. It implies that there is no coherent sense in which an individual could be free — in Hegel's full sense — outside the institutions of ethical life, and that the liberal picture of a pre-social individual whose freedom is then constrained or protected by social institutions rests on a philosophical error about the nature of willing itself.

The constitutive thesis, however, generates a tension that Hegel's own resolution leaves partially unstable. The tension is between subjective freedom — the right of the individual to find herself in her social roles, to endorse them as genuinely her own — and the objective rationality of the institutions themselves. Hegel's claim is that these two dimensions are reconciled in a well-ordered ethical life: the individual who participates fully in the institutions of family, civil society, and state finds in them not alien impositions but expressions of her own rational will. But this reconciliation depends on the institutions actually being rational — a condition that the Philosophy of Right makes demanding but not easy to verify. Hardimon (1994) is candid that the reconciliation thesis presupposes the rationality of the existing social world; critics have pressed the worry that the very embeddedness in institutions that constitutes freedom might equally constitute conformity. The structural architecture of the will — the claim that the individual is most free precisely when most at home in her social context — risks collapsing the distinction between freedom and adaptation unless the institutions themselves are subjected to an independent critical standard. Patten (2002) addresses this by insisting that rational self-determination in Hegel is not mere acceptance of given norms but participation in institutions whose rational structure can be reflectively endorsed; but the criteria for such endorsement remain a live philosophical question that the secondary literature has not settled. What is clear is that Hegel's account is not the inner-citadel positive liberty of Berlin's typology, nor is it the thin negative liberty of liberal social contract theory: it is a relational, institutional, and dialectically structured conception whose philosophical distinctiveness — and whose vulnerabilities — emerge precisely from its refusal to locate freedom either wholly inside or wholly outside the self.

## Section 2: The Foils: What Hegel Rejects and the Sources He Transforms

Hegel's positive account of freedom is intelligible only against the range of positions he systematically argues are deficient. Three targets are central: negative liberty, as the canonical liberal idea of freedom as non-interference; Willkür, or arbitrary free will, the mere capacity to choose among given options; and abstract Kantian moral autonomy, the purely formal self-legislation of the moral will. These rejections are not merely polemical maneuvers. They trace the conceptual space that Hegel is attempting to occupy, and they are structurally connected: each inadequate conception fails in the same fundamental way, by abstracting the will from the concrete social and rational conditions in which genuine self-determination is possible. Equally important, the rejections do not amount to wholesale discarding of the traditions from which these concepts emerge. Hegel's relationship to Kant and Rousseau is one of critical transformation: he appropriates their deepest insights while insisting that those insights cannot be sustained in the form in which their originators left them.

Hegel's objection to negative liberty — freedom as the absence of external interference — operates at two levels simultaneously. At the political level, he argues that this conception misunderstands the relation between the individual and social institutions: to treat all constraint as a limitation on a freedom that exists independently of social relations is to misread the genesis and content of individual will. At the metaphysical level, and this is the more fundamental charge, negative liberty presupposes what Hardimon (1994) calls an "atomistic" model of personhood: the subject of negative liberty is conceived as constituted independently of the social world, which then appears to that subject as an external set of potential interferences. Stillman (1974) identifies precisely this move in Hegel's critique of liberal theories of rights: the liberal account presupposes a subject who has rights prior to and independent of social life, but Hegel holds that persons are constituted through social relations and cannot be bearers of determinate freedom claims in abstraction from them. Neuhouser (2000) articulates the upshot: negative liberty does not fail because freedom is unimportant but because an atomistic and non-social conception cannot explain how freedom is actualized — it leaves the will empty of determinate content and dependent on conditions it cannot account for. Isaiah Berlin's taxonomy of negative and positive liberty (1969) sharpens the stakes of this debate, since Berlin's influential critique of positive-liberty traditions targets precisely the move that Hegel makes — from freedom as non-interference to freedom as rational self-realization — as a path toward authoritarianism. For Hegel, however, Berlin's dichotomy rests on the very atomism that needs to be contested.

The critique of Willkür is one of the most philosophically precise moves in Hegel's philosophy of freedom. Willkür is not ignorance or weakness of will; it is the will's capacity to abstract from any particular determination and choose among given impulses or options. For Hegel, this capacity represents a formally necessary moment of the will — the moment of pure indifference to determinate content — but it is a deficient form of freedom rather than freedom itself. A will that can choose anything has, in a philosophically significant sense, chosen nothing: it remains dependent on contingent impulse for whatever content it takes up, and its "freedom" consists only in the negative act of endorsing or rejecting what is given from without. Wood (1990) provides the standard reconstruction of this argument: Willkür treats contingent preference as self-determination, but genuine self-determination requires that the will find its content through its own rational activity, not through external givenness. The upshot is that arbitrary free will, far from being the highest expression of freedom, is freedom's negation at the formal level — what Hegel calls, with characteristic sharpness, freedom-that-is-not-yet-free.

Hegel's critique of Kantian moral autonomy follows the same structural logic, though it is directed at a philosophically more sophisticated target. Kant's moral will is autonomous in the strong sense: it legislates the moral law to itself and acts from that law alone, abstracting from all contingent inclination and empirical content. For Hegel, this is precisely the problem. Finlayson (1998) reconstructs the main charges Hegel presses against Kantian formalism: the categorical imperative, precisely because it abstracts from all determinate content, cannot generate specific moral duties — it produces the "empty formalism" in which any maxim can be reformulated to pass the universalizability test. The objection is not that Kant is simply wrong about the importance of rational self-legislation, but that morality taken in abstraction from the social context in which norms are embedded and effective is one-sided. Pippin (2008) argues on this basis that Hegel's critique of Kantian Moralität is best read as internal to the Kantian project rather than a rejection of it: the inadequacy of abstract moral autonomy lies in its failure to explain how rational norms acquire genuine authority over agents — a failure that Hegel's account of Sittlichkeit is designed to remedy.

This last point opens onto what is philosophically most interesting about Hegel's relationship to his predecessors. He does not simply discard Kant and Rousseau; the appropriation and the critique are two sides of the same move. From Kant, Hegel inherits the core insight that freedom is rational self-determination and that genuine agency cannot be mere responsiveness to external causal or motivational pressure. What Hegel rejects is the formalism through which Kant pursues this insight — the attempt to ground freedom in a pure practical reason severed from historical, institutional, and social embodiment. From Rousseau, Hegel appropriates the idea that genuine freedom, in its specifically political form, consists in willing the general will rather than mere private preference: the identity of individual will and collective will is not an external imposition but the condition of the individual's own freedom (Neuhouser 2000; Taylor 1975). Taylor's account of Hegel's intellectual debts remains an essential orientation here: he traces how Hegel fuses the Rousseauian insight into civic freedom with the Kantian demand for rational self-legislation, producing an account in which self-determination is neither the private moral achievement of isolated reason nor the sentimental fusion of individuals into a collective. Neuhouser (2008) illuminates the Rousseauian side more precisely: Rousseau's analysis of amour propre and the social preconditions of recognition establishes a framework of recognition-dependent freedom that Hegel inherits and institutionalizes. But Hegel also rejects Rousseau's residual sentimentalism: the general will, left at the level of sentiment or volonté générale in the abstract, cannot deliver the determinate institutional content that freedom requires — it requires, as Ritter (1982) argues, reconciliation with the concrete ethical forms of modernity. Church (2010) develops this further, showing that Hegel's account of civil society constitutes a specific response to the Rousseauian problem of reconciling particular desire with general interest by embedding freedom in a system of needs where desire itself becomes rational.

The 1802/03 Natural Law essay is the clearest early statement of this double gesture of rejection and transformation. Clarke (2011) reconstructs Hegel's critique of Fichte's formalist deduction of right from pure practical reason in the essay, arguing that this anti-Fichtean move — which is simultaneously anti-Kantian in its formalist register — is what opens the space for Hegel's own Sittlichkeit framework. Molloy (2024) extends the analysis, arguing that the essay targets both empiricist natural law (Grotius, Hobbes), which abstracts from the living ethical community by grounding right in individual psychology, and formalist natural law (Kant, Fichte), which abstracts from the same community by grounding right in pure practical reason. Both traditions fail, on Hegel's account, for the same reason: they seek to derive ethical life from something more primitive, when it is in fact the ground from which any adequate account of freedom must begin. The early essay thus already exhibits the philosophical structure that will characterize Hegel's mature position: the critique of inadequate conceptions and the appropriation of their genuine insights are not separable operations but constitute a single philosophical act.

## Section 3: Freedom in Development: From Phenomenology to Philosophy of Right

The relation between Hegel's early and mature thought on freedom is not merely a biographical question but a philosophical one: whether the concept of freedom that motivates the *Phenomenology of Spirit* (1807) is the same concept systematized in the *Philosophy of Right* (1820), or whether the later work represents a significant reorientation — and perhaps a conservative stabilization — of the earlier dialectic. The secondary literature has not reached consensus on this question, and how one reads the developmental arc shapes how one reads Hegel's political conclusions.

The *Phenomenology*'s most discussed treatment of freedom opens with the master-slave dialectic. When two self-consciousnesses first confront one another, each attempts to secure recognition — and thereby its own freedom — by negating the other as a mere object. The outcome of this struggle, Hegel argues, is inherently unstable: the victor achieves domination but not genuine recognition, since the acknowledgment of a being whose independence one has suppressed is worthless as recognition. Freedom cannot be won through domination. This argument contains, in compressed form, the germ of the social-constitutive thesis that will be elaborated institutionally in the *Philosophy of Right*: genuine self-consciousness, and with it genuine freedom, requires the kind of mutual recognition that domination structurally forecloses. Williams (1997) reads recognition as the connective thread running across Hegel's entire development, arguing that the master-slave dialectic and the political philosophy of ethical life share a common normative foundation in the demand for mutuality. Harris (1983) shows, through painstaking attention to the Jena manuscripts, that this recognitive structure was already taking shape in the *System of Ethical Life* and the two Realphilosophie drafts — the crucial transitional years during which Hegel was working out how natural life, conflict, and ethical community were related before he settled on the *Phenomenology*'s structure.

The sections on stoicism, scepticism, and the unhappy consciousness elaborate a complementary lesson. Each represents a different strategy for achieving freedom by withdrawal from the social world. The stoic retreats into the inner citadel of rational indifference; the sceptic deconstructs all determinate content so as to be bound by nothing; the unhappy consciousness seeks an absolute object beyond the finite world. In each case, the *Phenomenology* shows that the retreat reproduces alienation in a new form rather than overcoming it. Freedom sought through inner self-sufficiency remains abstract precisely because it leaves the determinate social world — the world in which a self actually lives and acts — untransformed and alien. This is the phenomenological demonstration of a claim that the *Philosophy of Right* will assert systematically: that abstract right and pure moral autonomy are not false but one-sided, requiring actualization in the concrete institutions of ethical life to be genuinely free rather than merely formally so.

The role of the *Science of Logic* in mediating this development is contested. Yeomans (2011) provides the most sustained account of the logical underpinnings of Hegel's practical philosophy, arguing that the free will is grounded in the logical structure of reflection as developed in the doctrine of essence: the self-positing particularity within a universal that constitutes freedom is not an ad hoc practical postulate but follows from the Logic's analysis of self-determination. Pippin (1989) reads the Logic's categories as normative-conceptual conditions for genuine rational self-determination rather than as claims about mind-independent reality; his later engagement with the *Science of Logic* (Pippin 2018) refines this into a "metaphysics of conceptuality" — an account of what it means for thought to be genuinely self-determining — that grounds practical freedom without reverting to substance-metaphysics. Beiser (2005), by contrast, argues that this deflationary reading strips away what is philosophically distinctive in Hegel's idealism: freedom as the truth of Geist requires the full speculative framework, including Hegel's holistic account of nature and absolute spirit, not merely an account of social-normative self-legislation. On Beiser's reading, the Logic's contribution to the practical philosophy is ontological in a sense that Pippin's neo-Kantian reconstruction cannot capture.

This metaphysical disagreement feeds directly into the continuity debate. Avineri (1972) defends a reading of continuous development: Hegel's political philosophy represents not a conservative retreat but a sophisticated attempt to articulate the institutions through which the freedom dramatized in the *Phenomenology* becomes actual in the modern state. On this reading, the *Philosophy of Right* extends rather than betrays the *Phenomenology*'s dialectical ambitions. A counter-tendency in the literature — shaped in part by the reception of the young Marx — holds that the mature Hegel systematized and stabilized what had been genuinely open and radical in the earlier work, trading the *Phenomenology*'s dynamic account of spirit's self-education for a normative justification of existing modern institutions. Westphal (1993) offers a careful middle position: the *Philosophy of Right* draws its structural logic directly from the *Encyclopaedia Logic*'s account of the Concept, and this relationship shows that the text is neither a mere political tract nor a simple application of the *Phenomenology*, but a systematic derivation of the forms of freedom from a logical analysis of the will.

What this means concretely is that the *Philosophy of Right*'s institutional account is better understood as a reconfiguration of the *Phenomenology*'s shapes of consciousness rather than their direct continuation. The phenomenological figures — stoic withdrawal, unhappy self-alienation, the frustrated struggle for recognition — do not simply reappear in institutional form; they are transformed into moments within a normative theory of how freedom is actualized. Abstract right, morality, and ethical life are not phenomenological shapes but logical-normative stages, and the transition between them follows the structure of the Concept rather than the dialectical dynamics of spirit's self-experience. Blumenfeld (2022) illuminates this transition by examining the early *System of Ethical Life*'s treatment of crime, property, and punishment, showing how the Jena-period Hegel was already integrating conflict and negativity into his framework as essential rather than merely disruptive elements — a move that anticipates the mature theory while operating under different systematic constraints. Wood (1990) argues that the *Philosophy of Right*'s normative account of social freedom is defensible on its own terms, not reducible to a conservative stabilization of earlier radicalism; Avineri (1972) similarly stresses that the social and institutional concerns of the mature political philosophy were continuous with the critical engagement with modernity visible throughout Hegel's career. Whether this continuity is deep enough to sustain the claim that the same concept of freedom is at work across both texts, or whether the systematic closure of the *Philosophy of Right* represents a genuine narrowing of the dialectical openness the *Phenomenology* had achieved, remains one of the central unresolved questions in Hegel scholarship.

## Section 4: Three Scholarly Controversies

The secondary literature on Hegel's conception of freedom is organized around three controversies that are not merely interpretive disputes about what Hegel "really meant" but substantive philosophical disagreements about the conditions under which any adequate theory of freedom must be developed. These debates are also interconnected: one's position in the metaphysical controversy shapes one's reading of the political implications of Hegel's social theory, and both bear directly on how one assesses the neo-Hegelian recognition literature. Taking them in sequence reveals the cumulative philosophical stakes.

### 4.1 The Metaphysical Controversy: Deflation or Idealism?

The most technically demanding dispute concerns what Hegel's theory of freedom presupposes at the level of ontology. Pippin's *Hegel's Idealism* (1989) inaugurated what is now standardly called the non-metaphysical or neo-Kantian reading: Hegel's idealism is not a pre-critical thesis about cosmic mind but a radicalization of Kant's transcendental project, in which the categories of the Logic articulate conditions under which thought can be genuinely self-determining rather than merely empirically driven. On this account, Hegel's claim that freedom is the "truth of spirit" amounts to the claim that genuine agency is a social-normative achievement — the product of mutual recognition and institutional embodiment — rather than a metaphysical property of individual subjects considered in isolation. Pippin's *Hegel's Practical Philosophy* (2008) develops this reading into a full account of rational agency: the free will is not an independent faculty but a structure of self-governance that can only be actualized through relations of reciprocal recognition in shared ethical life. Pippin's later *Hegel's Realm of Shadows* (2018) returns to the Logic to argue that it is a metaphysics of conceptuality — an account of what it means for thought to be self-determining — without reverting to substance-metaphysics or anything that would embarrass a contemporary philosopher of action.

Beiser's response is frontal and uncompromising. In his 1995 polemic against Pinkard's closely related position, Beiser argues that the non-metaphysical reading strips away the speculative core of Hegel's philosophy — above all, Hegel's commitment to the real self-development of Geist through nature and history — and reduces him to an implausibly thin epistemological idealism (Beiser 1995). The deflationary reading, on Beiser's view, turns Hegel into a sophisticated Kantian and loses the ontological distinctiveness that makes his account of freedom philosophically interesting: for Hegel, freedom is not merely a social-normative achievement but the expression of Geist's self-realization in the world, a claim that cannot be parsed in purely social or normative terms without remainder. Pinkard replied that the non-metaphysical reading does not deny Hegel's speculative ambitions but rather reconstructs his claims about Geist as claims about the historical-social conditions of rational self-understanding rather than as commitment to a substance ontology or mind-independent idealism (Pinkard 1996). The exchange clarified the positions without resolving them: both sides agree that Hegel's account of freedom is not naively pre-critical; they disagree about how much ontological work the notion of spirit is required to do.

Stern and Kreines offer mediating positions that resist the dichotomy itself. Stern's *Hegelian Metaphysics* (2009) defends a revisionary reading according to which Hegel is committed to ontological claims about the concept-laden structure of reality — claims that go beyond anything Kant's transcendental idealism would sanction — but does so without reverting to dogmatic pre-critical metaphysics. Hegel's categories, on this reading, are not merely conditions of possible experience but make genuine claims about what the world is like. Kreines (2006) argues that both the non-metaphysical and the robustly idealist camps remain trapped within a Kantian conceptual framework that Hegel himself rejected, and that Hegel's metaphysics is better understood as a form of post-Kantian naturalism concerning the inherent purposiveness of natural kinds and the logical structure of things as such. On Kreines's diagnosis, the texts genuinely underdetermine the debate.

The stakes are significant for any account of Hegel's positive conception of freedom. If Pippin's reading is correct, then Hegel's theory of freedom is in principle compatible with post-Kantian analytic philosophy of action: one can accept his social-constitutive thesis without committing to anything metaphysically revisionary. If Beiser and Stern are right, then the full account of freedom as Beisichsein im Anderen — being-with-oneself-in-otherness — requires an ontology of spirit that most analytic philosophers would find difficult to absorb, and the neo-Hegelian appropriations of Hegel by Honneth, Neuhouser, and Pippin himself are already departures from rather than reconstructions of Hegel's own position.

### 4.2 The Political Debate: Authoritarianism, Liberalism, and Republicanism

The political controversy over Hegel's social theory has a longer reception history, its twentieth-century trajectory inaugurated by Popper's charge in *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945) that Hegel is an intellectual ancestor of totalitarianism whose glorification of the state and subordination of individual conscience to national spirit prepared the philosophical ground for fascism and Marxist tyranny. Popper's reading was historically consequential — it shaped anglophone perception of Hegel for a generation — but scholars have largely found it philosophically crude: it conflates Hegel's organic account of rational institutions with a surrender of individual judgment to state authority and ignores the extensive textual basis for the priority Hegel assigns to subjective freedom. Franco's survey of the debate (1997) maps three broad interpretive paradigms that have organized the response: Hegel as anti-liberal, Hegel as proto-liberal, and Hegel as transcending the liberal-communitarian dichotomy.

Taylor's *Hegel* (1975) is the pivotal revisionist text. Reconstructing Hegel through the lens of expressivism, Taylor argues that Hegel's account of social embeddedness is not a subordination of the individual to the state but a precondition for genuine identity formation: the self cannot be what it is apart from the communal practices and institutions in which it is constituted. This reading became foundational for communitarian appropriations of Hegel in debates with Rawlsian liberalism. The force of Taylor's interpretation is that it reframes the apparent tension between individual freedom and social membership as a philosophical mistake on the part of liberal atomism rather than a cost that Hegel imposes: beings who are constitutively social cannot meaningfully ask whether they would be better off without their social ties. Critics, however, have argued that Taylor's reading domesticates Hegel's radicalism and softens the tension between objective freedom — the rationality of actual institutions — and subjective freedom — the individual's capacity to find herself, or fail to find herself, within those institutions.

Smith and Patten approach the question from within a broadly liberal analytical framework. Smith's *Hegel's Critique of Liberalism* (1989) argues that Hegel's target is abstract liberalism — the conception of rights as properties of pre-social individuals — rather than liberal rights as such: rights in Hegel are achievements of historical ethical life, contextually embedded in institutions that make them real rather than merely formal. Patten's *Hegel's Idea of Freedom* (2002) provides what it describes as the first systematic analytical treatment of Hegel's freedom concept, arguing that it is best understood as autonomy in the Kantian sense rather than mere positive liberty, and that Hegel's three-moment structure of abstract right, morality, and Sittlichkeit constitutes a liberal theory in its fundamentals, albeit one grounded in social membership rather than atomist contract. The upshot of both readings is that Hegel's state realizes individuality rather than suppressing it, and that the authoritarianism charge depends on a misreading of what Hegel means by the universality of the state's purposes.

A more recent strand of scholarship has pursued the affinities between Hegel's account and neo-republican political philosophy. Allen (2006) argues that while Hegel's theory of freedom cannot be fully assimilated to Pettit's non-domination framework — the expressivist dimension of Hegel's account goes beyond what non-domination captures — the two models share important structural features: in both, unfreedom is constituted by subordination to the arbitrary will of another, and institutional arrangements are evaluated by whether they eliminate such subordination. Westphal (2017) makes a stronger claim, arguing that Hegel's analysis of mutual recognition directly entails a republican right to non-domination as a constitutive requirement of rational justification in all non-formal domains. These neo-republican readings share the revisionist consensus in rejecting the authoritarianism charge but diverge from standard liberal readings by emphasizing relational rather than individualist foundations for freedom — closer to Philip Pettit than to Isaiah Berlin.

### 4.3 Recognition and Social Freedom: Honneth, Neuhouser, and Their Critics

The third controversy concerns how recognition (Anerkennung) and social freedom are related in Hegel's texts and in neo-Hegelian reconstruction. The debates here are both exegetical and normative, and they connect the first two controversies: how much metaphysics one thinks is required for Hegel's recognition theory shapes whether one finds Honneth's reconstruction faithful, and how one assesses the political implications of Hegel's institutional theory shapes whether one finds it progressive or conformist.

Honneth's *The Struggle for Recognition* (1995) grounds a neo-Hegelian recognition framework in a social-psychological account of intersubjective development, arguing that freedom requires mutual recognition across three spheres — love, legal respect, and solidarity — and that misrecognition in any sphere constitutes a moral injury to the person's practical relation-to-self. The claim that recognition is constitutive of, rather than merely enabling for, full self-realization is the key premise. Honneth's later *Freedom's Right* (2014) pursues a more explicitly Hegelian project: a "normative reconstruction" of modern institutional life — personal relationships, the market, and democratic will-formation — that treats social freedom as the master concept of justice. On this account, institutions are just to the extent that they enable individuals to pursue their ends through, rather than against, one another. Neuhouser's *Foundations of Hegel's Social Theory* (2000) offers a reading that tracks Hegel's own texts more closely, arguing that Hegel's social theory organizes three distinct freedom concepts — subjective, objective, and social — and that Hegel's central achievement is showing how subjective individual freedom can be reconciled with social membership through appropriately structured institutions. Social freedom, for Neuhouser, is realized when individuals can identify their own ends with the ends embodied in institutional practices, not merely alongside them.

The critical literature has focused three lines of objection. De Boer (2013) provides the most exegetically thorough critique, arguing that Honneth's normative reconstruction misreads the Philosophy of Right by reducing its dialectical structure to a catalogue of symmetrical recognition spheres and jettisoning the speculative philosophical framework that provides Hegel's account its internal justificatory force. On de Boer's reading, Honneth's reconstruction presupposes the very form of reading — recognition as mutual and symmetrical — that Hegel's actual texts do not warrant; the result is a Hegel stripped of the metaphysical commitments that make the recognition theory coherent. This connects directly to the first controversy: if de Boer and Stern are right that Hegel's recognition theory requires its speculative-idealist context, then Honneth's post-metaphysical translation is not simply a reconstruction but a transformation into a substantially different position.

Jütten (2015) focuses the critique on Honneth's treatment of the market sphere, arguing that the labor market in particular cannot realize the conditions of mutual recognition and cooperative solidarity that social freedom requires. Honneth claims that the promise of social freedom is implicit in market institutions and explains why modern subjects have experienced capitalism as legitimate; Jütten contends that this normative potential is not adequately grounded in Honneth's reconstruction and that the labor market structurally resists the form of mutual identification that social freedom presupposes. The implication is that social freedom, taken as a normative standard, may be systematically unrealizable within capitalist civil society as Hegel himself analyzed it. Dum and Guay (2017) identify a further structural deficit in both Hegel's and Honneth's accounts: both theorize how individuals come to identify with institutional norms but neither adequately theorizes education as the institution that mediates between subjective formation and full institutional participation. The mediation that would explain how social freedom is reproduced across generations is missing from both accounts.

The Fraser-Honneth exchange adds a further dimension. Fraser's challenge — that recognition-based theories of justice cannot accommodate claims grounded in material inequality without distorting them into demands for social esteem — questions the sufficiency of the recognition framework itself rather than only its Hegelian credentials. Where Honneth defends recognition monism, arguing that distributional injustices are best understood as expressions of failed recognition, Fraser's perspectival dualism insists on the irreducibility of redistribution as a normative category (Fraser and Honneth 2003). The debate defines the limits of what a Hegelian social-freedom theory, however carefully reconstructed, can claim to deliver as a theory of justice.

What emerges from these three controversies taken together is that the philosophical disagreements are not incidental but constitutive of what it means to take Hegel's conception of freedom seriously. Whether the non-metaphysical reading preserves what is philosophically distinctive in Hegel's account, whether his institutional theory secures space for dissent and nonconformity, and whether recognition and social freedom are genuinely unified or merely coordinated in neo-Hegelian reconstruction — these remain live and interconnected questions that any philosophical appropriation of Hegel must address.

## Conclusion

Three points of convergence emerge from the foregoing review, each supported by the scholarly record even where the debates remain unresolved. First, Hegel's positive account of freedom is definitively not a negative-liberty view in Berlin's sense. The social-constitutive thesis — developed with precision by Neuhouser and underwritten philosophically by Pippin's account of rational agency — is well established: institutions are not merely protective scaffolding for a pre-given individual will but the conditions under which a genuinely free will becomes possible at all. Wood's careful reconstruction of Hegel's ethical thought, and Patten's systematic exposition of the idea of freedom, confirm that Abstract Right and subjective Morality are partial achievements that require Sittlichkeit not as a constraint on freedom but as its actualization. Second, the developmental arc from the Phenomenology of Spirit through the Philosophy of Right reveals a coherent program. The master-slave dialectic's demonstration that freedom cannot be won unilaterally, the subsequent phenomenological elimination of inner retreats as adequate responses to unfreedom, and the institutional realization of mutual recognition in the mature system are not disconnected episodes. They constitute, as Beiser and Taylor have shown from different angles, a sustained philosophical argument about what genuine self-determination requires. That the program is contested — that critics influenced by the early Marx question whether the mature Hegel stabilizes what the Phenomenology had left productively unstable — does not dissolve the coherence but sharpens the question about whether institutional reconciliation is philosophically achieved or merely asserted. Third, the three scholarly controversies surveyed here are genuinely productive rather than merely sectarian. The metaphysical debate forces precision about what the philosophy of freedom is ultimately trying to explain. The liberalism debate forces precision about the political implications of any social account of selfhood. The recognition debate forces precision about how the normative core of freedom relates to its institutional expression.

Three enduring open questions mark the frontier of the literature. The first concerns the non-metaphysical readings pioneered by Pippin: whether they can retain what is philosophically distinctive in Hegel's account or whether, as Beiser argues, the deflationary move produces a sophisticated post-Kantian theory at the cost of Hegel's actual commitments. This is not merely an exegetical dispute; it bears on what resources Hegel's concept of freedom offers to contemporary practical philosophy and whether those resources depend on a revisionary ontology of spirit or can be recovered within broadly naturalistic or social-normative frameworks. The second open question is the conformity worry. Hegel's claim that subjective freedom is preserved within Sittlichkeit — that individuals can find themselves in their institutional roles — has not been conclusively vindicated against the charge that the account offers insufficient protection to dissent, nonconformity, and the individual who cannot or will not identify with the social arrangements into which she is born. Taylor's communitarian reading partially deflects the worry; Popper's blunt version of it has been set aside; but the more refined internal challenge, visible in de Boer's and Jütten's responses to Honneth's reconstruction, persists. Whether Hegel's account can generate internal critical resources for challenging existing institutional arrangements — rather than rationalizing them — remains genuinely open. The third question concerns the unity of recognition and social freedom. Honneth's reconstruction in Freedom's Right represents the most ambitious contemporary attempt to integrate these strands into a single normative theory, and its critical reception by de Boer, Jütten, and others defines the current frontier. Whether recognition and social freedom are genuinely unified in Hegel's own system, or whether neo-Hegelian reconstructions coordinate them externally by selectively drawing on different moments of the texts, is a question that cannot be settled by appeal to Hegel alone; it requires an independent account of what such unity would require.

What makes Hegel's concept of freedom philosophically durable is precisely that it refuses the apparent simplicity of the alternatives. Against accounts that treat freedom as a relation between a pre-social individual and external obstacles, Hegel insists that the very capacity for self-determination is socially constituted. Against accounts that dissolve the individual into collective life, he insists that Sittlichkeit must be recognizable by the individuals who inhabit it as the expression of their own rational will. Neither the atomism of liberal contract theory nor the holism its critics sometimes attribute to him captures the position he is actually defending. The debates this position generates — about metaphysics, politics, and recognition — are not signs of a theory in difficulty. They are evidence that the philosophical stakes of theorizing freedom are higher than any single approach has yet managed to exhaust.
