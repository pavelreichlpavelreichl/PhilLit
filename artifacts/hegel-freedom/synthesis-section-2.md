## Section 1: The Structure of Hegel's Conception of Freedom

Hegel's account of freedom opens with a deceptively simple claim: that the will, when genuinely free, is "with itself in its other" — bei sich selbst im Anderen. The formula signals that freedom is not a condition of isolation from determination but of a particular relation to it. To understand what this means, and why it marks a departure from both liberal voluntarism and Kantian formalism, one must follow Hegel through the tripartite structure of the will that grounds the entire argument of the Philosophy of Right. Universality, particularity, and individuality are not three separate faculties but three moments of a single, self-developing structure: the will in its moment of universality is pure self-relation, the capacity to abstract from any and all determinate content; in its moment of particularity, it is the moment of determinate drive, desire, and inclination — the will as directed toward some specific object; in its moment of individuality, these two are unified in a concrete self-determination that is no longer abstract but actual. As Yeomans (2011) shows, this tripartite structure is not merely political scaffolding but is grounded in the logical structure of reflection in the Science of Logic: the free will is the logical structure of self-positing particularity within a universal, and the moments of the will replicate the dialectic of essence, appearance, and actuality at the level of practical philosophy. The upshot is that a will that remains at the level of universality alone — capable of abstracting from everything — is not yet genuinely free but only formally free. And a will that remains at the level of particularity alone — driven by desire without rational self-legislation — is equally unfree, determined from without rather than from within.

The philosophical payoff of this structure is the concept of "mediated immediacy": the genuinely free will is not the will that has escaped determination but the will that, having passed through determination, finds itself fully at home in it. Wood (1990) reconstructs this as Hegel's central claim against Kantian abstraction — that genuine rational agency is not the purely formal capacity to legislate from nowhere but the actualization of rational self-determination in a determinate social context. Pippin (2008) presses this further, reading the Philosophy of Right as a theory of rational agency in which the "actualization condition" is doing the most work: to be genuinely free is not merely to have the capacity for self-determination but to exercise that capacity in social practices that recognize and sustain it. On Pippin's reading, Hegel's departure from Kant is not a retreat from rational autonomy but its radicalization — autonomy must be concretely embodied in recognized institutions to be more than an aspiration. This yields a conception of freedom that is relational and institutional in a way that distinguishes it sharply from the "inner citadel" version of positive liberty that Isaiah Berlin criticized. Berlin's target was the idea that freedom consists in self-mastery over the passional self, with the rational self as a kind of inner sovereign. Hegel's account is not of this form: the self that is free is not one that has retreated inward to govern its desires but one that has found itself at home in the external social world. Patten (2002) captures this by describing Hegel's concept of rational self-determination as requiring both the subjective condition of rational self-legislation and the objective condition of actualization in social institutions — neither condition is reducible to the other, and neither alone is sufficient.

The structural account of the will provides the framework for understanding the move from Abstract Right through Morality to Sittlichkeit. This progression is not a narrative of increasing restriction but of increasingly adequate modes of freedom. Abstract Right — the sphere of property, contract, and formal personhood — instantiates the moment of universality: persons are recognized as bearers of rights irrespective of their particular social identities. Morality — the sphere of conscience, intention, and moral self-assessment — instantiates the moment of particularity: the subject insists on her right to act only on principles she can reflectively endorse. Both are genuine, indispensable moments of freedom, but each is one-sided. Abstract Right provides no content for choice; Morality provides subjective content but cannot generate determinate obligations without reference to existing social forms. Sittlichkeit — ethical life, the institutions of family, civil society, and state — is not a cancellation of these earlier moments but their "truth": the social context in which abstract right and moral subjectivity are simultaneously preserved and completed. Wood (1990) emphasizes that Hegel's critique of Kantian morality is not that moral self-legislation is wrong but that it is insufficient — morality taken in isolation cannot specify what one ought to do and generates the pathological possibility of moral fanaticism. Hardimon (1994) develops what he calls the "reconciliation thesis": that the modern rational state is the social world in which individuals can genuinely be at home, not alienated from their social roles but finding in those roles the expression of their own freedom.

The most philosophically contentious element of this account is what Neuhouser (2000) calls the constitutive thesis: that the institutions of Sittlichkeit are not merely instrumental protectors of a freedom that pre-exists them but are conditions of possibility for freedom as such. On this reading, family, civil society, and state do not stand in a merely external relation to individual freedom — enabling it, protecting it, providing its occasion — but partly constitute the kind of willing that freedom requires. Neuhouser reconstructs this thesis with considerable philosophical rigor, arguing that the tripartite structure of subjective, objective, and absolute freedom in Hegel maps onto the three spheres of ethical life in a way that makes each sphere the actualization of a distinct but necessary dimension of free willing. This is a strong and controversial claim. It implies that there is no coherent sense in which an individual could be free — in Hegel's full sense — outside the institutions of ethical life, and that the liberal picture of a pre-social individual whose freedom is then constrained or protected by social institutions rests on a philosophical error about the nature of willing itself.

The constitutive thesis, however, generates a tension that Hegel's own resolution leaves partially unstable. The tension is between subjective freedom — the right of the individual to find herself in her social roles, to endorse them as genuinely her own — and the objective rationality of the institutions themselves. Hegel's claim is that these two dimensions are reconciled in a well-ordered ethical life: the individual who participates fully in the institutions of family, civil society, and state finds in them not alien impositions but expressions of her own rational will. But this reconciliation depends on the institutions actually being rational — a condition that the Philosophy of Right makes demanding but not easy to verify. Hardimon (1994) is candid that the reconciliation thesis presupposes the rationality of the existing social world; critics have pressed the worry that the very embeddedness in institutions that constitutes freedom might equally constitute conformity. The structural architecture of the will — the claim that the individual is most free precisely when most at home in her social context — risks collapsing the distinction between freedom and adaptation unless the institutions themselves are subjected to an independent critical standard. Patten (2002) addresses this by insisting that rational self-determination in Hegel is not mere acceptance of given norms but participation in institutions whose rational structure can be reflectively endorsed; but the criteria for such endorsement remain a live philosophical question that the secondary literature has not settled. What is clear is that Hegel's account is not the inner-citadel positive liberty of Berlin's typology, nor is it the thin negative liberty of liberal social contract theory: it is a relational, institutional, and dialectically structured conception whose philosophical distinctiveness — and whose vulnerabilities — emerge precisely from its refusal to locate freedom either wholly inside or wholly outside the self.
