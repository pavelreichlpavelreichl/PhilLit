## Section 2: The Foils — What Hegel Rejects and the Sources He Transforms

Hegel's positive account of freedom is intelligible only against the range of positions he systematically argues are deficient. Three targets are central: negative liberty, as the canonical liberal idea of freedom as non-interference; Willkür, or arbitrary free will, the mere capacity to choose among given options; and abstract Kantian moral autonomy, the purely formal self-legislation of the moral will. These rejections are not merely polemical maneuvers. They trace the conceptual space that Hegel is attempting to occupy, and they are structurally connected: each inadequate conception fails in the same fundamental way, by abstracting the will from the concrete social and rational conditions in which genuine self-determination is possible. Equally important, the rejections do not amount to wholesale discarding of the traditions from which these concepts emerge. Hegel's relationship to Kant and Rousseau is one of critical transformation: he appropriates their deepest insights while insisting that those insights cannot be sustained in the form in which their originators left them.

Hegel's objection to negative liberty — freedom as the absence of external interference — operates at two levels simultaneously. At the political level, he argues that this conception misunderstands the relation between the individual and social institutions: to treat all constraint as a limitation on a freedom that exists independently of social relations is to misread the genesis and content of individual will. At the metaphysical level, and this is the more fundamental charge, negative liberty presupposes what Hardimon (1994) calls an "atomistic" model of personhood: the subject of negative liberty is conceived as constituted independently of the social world, which then appears to that subject as an external set of potential interferences. Stillman (1974) identifies precisely this move in Hegel's critique of liberal theories of rights: the liberal account presupposes a subject who has rights prior to and independent of social life, but Hegel holds that persons are constituted through social relations and cannot be bearers of determinate freedom claims in abstraction from them. Neuhouser (2000) articulates the upshot: negative liberty does not fail because freedom is unimportant but because an atomistic and non-social conception cannot explain how freedom is actualized — it leaves the will empty of determinate content and dependent on conditions it cannot account for. Isaiah Berlin's taxonomy of negative and positive liberty (1969) sharpens the stakes of this debate, since Berlin's influential critique of positive-liberty traditions targets precisely the move that Hegel makes — from freedom as non-interference to freedom as rational self-realization — as a path toward authoritarianism. For Hegel, however, Berlin's dichotomy rests on the very atomism that needs to be contested.

The critique of Willkür is one of the most philosophically precise moves in Hegel's philosophy of freedom. Willkür is not ignorance or weakness of will; it is the will's capacity to abstract from any particular determination and choose among given impulses or options. For Hegel, this capacity represents a formally necessary moment of the will — the moment of pure indifference to determinate content — but it is a deficient form of freedom rather than freedom itself. A will that can choose anything has, in a philosophically significant sense, chosen nothing: it remains dependent on contingent impulse for whatever content it takes up, and its "freedom" consists only in the negative act of endorsing or rejecting what is given from without. Wood (1990) provides the standard reconstruction of this argument: Willkür treats contingent preference as self-determination, but genuine self-determination requires that the will find its content through its own rational activity, not through external givenness. The upshot is that arbitrary free will, far from being the highest expression of freedom, is freedom's negation at the formal level — what Hegel calls, with characteristic sharpness, freedom-that-is-not-yet-free.

Hegel's critique of Kantian moral autonomy follows the same structural logic, though it is directed at a philosophically more sophisticated target. Kant's moral will is autonomous in the strong sense: it legislates the moral law to itself and acts from that law alone, abstracting from all contingent inclination and empirical content. For Hegel, this is precisely the problem. Finlayson (1998) reconstructs the main charges Hegel presses against Kantian formalism: the categorical imperative, precisely because it abstracts from all determinate content, cannot generate specific moral duties — it produces the "empty formalism" in which any maxim can be reformulated to pass the universalizability test. The objection is not that Kant is simply wrong about the importance of rational self-legislation, but that morality taken in abstraction from the social context in which norms are embedded and effective is one-sided. Pippin (2008) argues on this basis that Hegel's critique of Kantian Moralität is best read as internal to the Kantian project rather than a rejection of it: the inadequacy of abstract moral autonomy lies in its failure to explain how rational norms acquire genuine authority over agents — a failure that Hegel's account of Sittlichkeit is designed to remedy.

This last point opens onto what is philosophically most interesting about Hegel's relationship to his predecessors. He does not simply discard Kant and Rousseau; the appropriation and the critique are two sides of the same move. From Kant, Hegel inherits the core insight that freedom is rational self-determination and that genuine agency cannot be mere responsiveness to external causal or motivational pressure. What Hegel rejects is the formalism through which Kant pursues this insight — the attempt to ground freedom in a pure practical reason severed from historical, institutional, and social embodiment. From Rousseau, Hegel appropriates the idea that genuine freedom, in its specifically political form, consists in willing the general will rather than mere private preference: the identity of individual will and collective will is not an external imposition but the condition of the individual's own freedom (Neuhouser 2000; Taylor 1975). Taylor's account of Hegel's intellectual debts remains an essential orientation here: he traces how Hegel fuses the Rousseauian insight into civic freedom with the Kantian demand for rational self-legislation, producing an account in which self-determination is neither the private moral achievement of isolated reason nor the sentimental fusion of individuals into a collective. Neuhouser (2008) illuminates the Rousseauian side more precisely: Rousseau's analysis of amour propre and the social preconditions of recognition establishes a framework of recognition-dependent freedom that Hegel inherits and institutionalizes. But Hegel also rejects Rousseau's residual sentimentalism: the general will, left at the level of sentiment or volonté générale in the abstract, cannot deliver the determinate institutional content that freedom requires — it requires, as Ritter (1982) argues, reconciliation with the concrete ethical forms of modernity. Church (2010) develops this further, showing that Hegel's account of civil society constitutes a specific response to the Rousseauian problem of reconciling particular desire with general interest by embedding freedom in a system of needs where desire itself becomes rational.

The 1802/03 Natural Law essay is the clearest early statement of this double gesture of rejection and transformation. Clarke (2011) reconstructs Hegel's critique of Fichte's formalist deduction of right from pure practical reason in the essay, arguing that this anti-Fichtean move — which is simultaneously anti-Kantian in its formalist register — is what opens the space for Hegel's own Sittlichkeit framework. Molloy (2024) extends the analysis, arguing that the essay targets both empiricist natural law (Grotius, Hobbes), which abstracts from the living ethical community by grounding right in individual psychology, and formalist natural law (Kant, Fichte), which abstracts from the same community by grounding right in pure practical reason. Both traditions fail, on Hegel's account, for the same reason: they seek to derive ethical life from something more primitive, when it is in fact the ground from which any adequate account of freedom must begin. The early essay thus already exhibits the philosophical structure that will characterize Hegel's mature position: the critique of inadequate conceptions and the appropriation of their genuine insights are not separable operations but constitute a single philosophical act.
