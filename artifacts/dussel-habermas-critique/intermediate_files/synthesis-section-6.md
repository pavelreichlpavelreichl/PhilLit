## Conclusion

The secondary literature on Dussel's critique of Habermas organizes itself, with notable consistency, around a three-position spectrum. Toward the complementarity end stand Mendieta and Schelkshorn: Mendieta (1995) argues that discourse ethics and liberation ethics each supply what the other lacks, making them jointly necessary components of a complete moral theory, while Schelkshorn (2018) preserves a similar complementarity reading by grounding the two ethics in different geopolitical situations rather than treating one as the corrective of the other. Toward the displacement end stand Zúñiga (2021), who argues that the material principle of life cannot be derived from the formal-pragmatic presuppositions of argumentation and thus represents an independent foundation that discourse ethics cannot absorb, alongside Maldonado-Torres and Grosfoguel, whose decolonial readings treat communicative universalism as a particular locus of enunciation rather than a genuinely post-conventional standard. Between these poles, Sørensen (2011) and Radhakrishnan (2025) occupy a structurally distinct position: for them the question is not supplementation or displacement but unmasking — discourse ethics and the broader methodology of immanent critique function ideologically, naturalizing the exclusions that prevent the poor and colonized from entering communicative communities on equal terms.

A recurring structural feature runs through all positions: Apel, not Habermas, is the documented interlocutor. The foundational written exchange is between Apel and Dussel (Apel and Mendieta 1996), and secondary literature consistently reads the Dussel–Habermas relationship *through* this Apel mediation. The strongest disputes in the secondary literature — over the genealogy of modernity, the structure of valid interlocutorship in universal pragmatics, and the adequacy of immanent critique for colonial critique — lack a direct Habermasian reply. Defenses of Habermas specifically against the Eurocentrism charge are sparse; the available responses to feminist (Meehan 2000) and European-internal (Papastephanou 2011) versions of the charge do not translate easily to Dussel's material-liberation standpoint. Ahmad's (2025) recent "ethnic universalism" critique, developed independently of the liberation-philosophy tradition, suggests the absence of a direct Habermasian self-defense is a genuine gap in the field rather than a resolved question.

Four open questions emerge from this survey. First, the grounding status of the material principle of life remains contested: Zúñiga (2021) makes the most rigorous case for its irreducibility, but this claim has not received a sustained discourse-ethical counter-argument. Second, it remains unclear whether transmodernity and analectics constitute an operable normative theory or a standpoint claim — the literature provides the diagnosis but not a worked-out alternative that could be tested against concrete normative problems. Third, Radhakrishnan (2025) and Allen (2021) raise in sharpest form whether Frankfurt School immanent critique can be decolonized from within or must be replaced; the question cuts across the supplement/correction/displacement spectrum and has not been resolved. Fourth, direct defenses of Habermas against the liberation-ethics charge of Eurocentrism are scarce: Apel's Part A/Part B distinction is the standing response, but its adequacy is precisely what the irreducibility readings contest.

A future contribution might position itself most productively at the intersection of the second and third gaps — testing whether analectics can be rendered as normative theory, and engaging the Irvine (2011) and Quintana-Paz (2014) objections about internal incoherence, while confronting Radhakrishnan's challenge that any methodology anchored in immanent norms of modernity forecloses rather than enables the critique it promises.
