## Section 2: The Material/Formal Distinction — Supplement, Alternative, or Ideology Critique?

The philosophical crux of the Dussel–Habermas encounter is not primarily a dispute about scope or application but about the grounding of normativity itself. Where Habermasian and Apelian discourse ethics locate the ultimate criterion of moral validity in the formal-pragmatic presuppositions of argumentation — the symmetry conditions of the ideal communication community — Dussel insists that this formal criterion is both dependent upon and insufficient for a material principle grounded in the bodily reproduction of life and the standpoint of the excluded victim. How the secondary literature characterizes the relationship between these two grounding claims determines, in large measure, which picture of the debate one accepts: productive complementarity, immanent correction, irreducible difference, or ideological unmasking.

### 2.1 The Complementarity and Contextual-Differentiation Readings

The most widely represented position in the English-language secondary literature holds that discourse ethics and the ethics of liberation, rather than being simply opposed, address different theoretical registers of a complete moral theory. Mendieta (1995) advances this thesis explicitly: discourse ethics supplies formal criteria for the universal validity of norms, while liberation ethics supplies the material criterion — the dignity of concrete life and the obligation toward the Other who calls from outside the existing communication community. On this reading, each tradition suffers from the absence of what the other provides, and their encounter is philosophically productive rather than mutually undermining.

Schelkshorn (2018) develops a more contextually differentiated version of this position. He argues that discourse ethics and the ethics of liberation represent two distinct forms of universalistic moral theory: both are committed to universalism but ground it from different starting points responding to different historical and geopolitical situations. Discourse ethics inherits the European Enlightenment tradition of rational adjudication between conflicting interests and extends it to conditions of fallibilism and cultural pluralism; liberation ethics arises from and remains oriented toward the perspective of communities in the Global South for whom the conditions of symmetric argumentation have been systematically denied. For Schelkshorn, the two ethics are not merely formally complementary — they represent different contextual embedings of universalist aspiration. This is a more demanding claim than Mendieta's: it is not that the two theories jointly exhaust moral theory, but that they are responses to structurally different predicaments that nonetheless converge on universalist commitments.

Mills (2018) treats Dussel's critical appropriation of discourse ethics as the dominant mode of their relationship: liberation ethics takes the formal validity principle on board as a necessary but insufficient moment, incorporating it into the architectonic as the second of three structural levels — after the material principle of life-reproduction and before the feasibility principle. On this expository reconstruction, the formal-pragmatic criterion is not rejected but sublated into a richer structure.

Cobos (2015) pushes the complementarity reading to a more assertive conclusion. Examining how Latin American political thought responds to the discourse ethics tradition, Cobos argues that Apel's framework can maintain internal consistency only if subsumed under the program of liberation philosophy. This is a stronger claim than Schelkshorn's contextual differentiation: not that the two ethics respond to different situations and can coexist, but that discourse ethics requires the material and geopolitical grounding that liberation philosophy provides in order to avoid reproducing, despite itself, the very exclusions it claims to oppose. Cobos's reading thus occupies a middle position between complementarity and the irreducibility thesis developed below.

### 2.2 The Irreducibility Thesis

Against the complementarity readings, a distinct strand of the scholarship argues that the material principle of life cannot be derived from, reduced to, or even straightforwardly supplemented by the formal-pragmatic presuppositions of argumentation. Zúñiga (2021) advances this claim most directly in the Allen–Mendieta volume. He reconstructs Dussel's critique by showing that the grounding of moral norms in the formal structure of argumentative discourse leaves open the question of what is materially at stake in argument: which claims matter, and for whose lives. The material criterion — the obligation to produce and reproduce the conditions under which living subjects can survive and flourish — is not derivable from any structural feature of communicative competence. It is a genuinely independent transcendental claim that discourse ethics cannot generate from within its own resources.

This irreducibility thesis carries direct implications for how Dussel's three-moment architectonic is to be read. In his own formulation (Dussel 1997a), the formal-discursive moment is the second of three necessary levels, preceded by the material moment that grounds it and followed by the feasibility principle that orients it toward strategic action. Zúñiga's reading makes explicit what this architectonic implies: the formal is not self-sufficient and cannot supply its own material telos. The grounding relation runs from life to discourse, not the reverse — making discourse ethics, taken alone, not merely incomplete but structurally dependent on a prior criterion it cannot articulate.

Sáenz (2017) approaches the same conclusion from a different angle. Examining the role of biological and social life-reproduction in Dussel's ethics, he argues that Dussel's life-centered materialism constitutes a genuinely different ethical orientation from Habermasian proceduralism — not a supplement or a correction applied to an otherwise intact framework, but a reconception of the conditions under which ethics is possible at all. The distinction is between a theory that asks "which norms can be justified through symmetric argumentation?" and one that asks "what must be the case for living subjects to be in a position to argue at all?" These are different questions, and the second, on Sáenz's reading, is more fundamental.

Together, Zúñiga and Sáenz mark the "displacement" pole of the interpretive spectrum: the material principle does not extend or complete discourse ethics but registers a priority claim that discourse ethics is structurally unable to accommodate.

### 2.3 Dussel's Critique as Ideology Critique

A third, sharper reading recasts the entire dispute not as a question about the relation between two ethical theories but as a question about the ideological function of one of them. Sørensen (2011) argues that Dussel's critique of Habermasian discourse ethics is most accurately understood as a critique of ideology in the Frankfurt School sense: the ideal speech situation does not merely fail to include the poor and colonized as a contingent matter of application — it functions ideologically by naturalizing their exclusion. By grounding moral validity in the formal symmetry conditions of actual argumentation among existing participants, discourse ethics implicitly ratifies the inequalities that prevent the excluded from entering communicative communities on equal terms. The ideal is not a regulative counterfactual that exposes real asymmetries as deficits to be remedied; it is a normative standard that, in practice, takes the prevailing communication community as its reference point and thereby occludes the voices of those systematically excluded from it.

On Sørensen's reading, Dussel's counter-move — grounding ethics in the materiality of victims, in the embodied negativity of those the existing system renders invisible — is not a supplement to discourse ethics but an unmasking of its ideological character. Liberation ethics, from this standpoint, does not stand alongside discourse ethics in a complementary relationship; it exposes a structural feature of discourse ethics that complementarity readings obscure.

Dallmayr (2004) situates this critique within a longer genealogy of thinking from the "underside" of Eurocentric reason. Drawing a comparative line between Adorno's negative dialectics and Dussel's philosophy of exteriority, Dallmayr argues that both thinkers share a commitment to thinking what the dominant rationality excludes: Adorno's "non-identity" and Dussel's "exteriority of the Other" name structurally analogous moves against the closure of Western reason. Applied to the Habermas encounter, this framing suggests that the communicative ideal — for all its emancipatory intent — reproduces modernity's exclusionary logic at the level of formal normativity. Dussel's challenge is thus located not only within debates about moral theory but within the broader critical-theoretical tradition's unresolved tension between its universalist aspirations and the historical conditions of its emergence.

The ideology-critique reading sharpens the stakes of the entire debate. Where the complementarity reading frames the Dussel–Habermas encounter as a cooperative refinement of moral theory — each side contributing what the other lacks — Sørensen's and Dallmayr's readings frame it as a structural confrontation: whether the normative core of discourse ethics can be made to account for the excluded, or whether its very structure is part of what needs to be overcome. This distinction maps directly onto the supplement/correction/displacement spectrum that organizes the broader secondary literature, placing the ideology-critique reading firmly at the displacement pole while also connecting it to the question, developed in Section 3, of whether Habermas's genealogy of modernity can accommodate the colonial genealogy Dussel insists upon.

The material/formal distinction thus ramifies into three distinct scholarly positions: a complementarity or contextual-differentiation reading (Mendieta, Schelkshorn, Mills, Cobos), an irreducibility thesis (Zúñiga, Sáenz), and an ideology-critique reading (Sørensen, Dallmayr). What remains unresolved across these positions is the grounding question: whether the material principle of life is an independent foundation that discourse ethics presupposes without acknowledging, or whether it represents a genuinely external standpoint from which the formalist tradition can be exposed and surpassed.
