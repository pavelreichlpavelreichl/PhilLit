## Section 1: Reconstructing the Exchange — Convergence or Irreducible Difference?

The intellectual encounter between Enrique Dussel and the Frankfurt tradition unfolded in a specific institutional and textual form: a series of exchanges published in *Philosophy & Social Criticism* and *Philosophy Today* in the mid-1990s, culminating in Dussel's systematic statement in the *Ethics of Liberation* (1998). The interlocutor who responded in writing was not Habermas but Karl-Otto Apel. This asymmetry is not incidental. The secondary literature reconstructing the dialogue — from Mendieta's early mediating essay (1995) to Zielinski's reconstruction of Apel's arguments (2019) — systematically reads Dussel's engagement with Habermasian communicative ethics through the documented Apel exchange. The result is that the field divides over a specific question: what did the Apel–Dussel dialogue actually establish? Two poles have emerged — a complementarity reading and an irreducible-difference reading — and all subsequent scholarship on the material-versus-formal question inherits this division.

### 1.1 The Part A / Part B Response and the Complementarity Thesis

Apel's published response to Dussel (Apel and Mendieta 1996) turns on a structural distinction within discourse ethics itself. Part A concerns the transcendental-pragmatic grounding of the validity principle: the ideal communication community as the counterfactual standard presupposed in any argumentative act. Part B concerns the application of this standard to historical-material conditions, including the asymmetries produced by capitalism and colonial exclusion. Apel's defense is that liberation philosophy misreads discourse ethics by treating the ideal speech situation as a sociological description of existing communicative relations; properly understood, the ideal community is a normative standard *against which* real exclusions can be criticized. Part B, he argues, can and must address the material asymmetries that Dussel foregrounds.

Several readings of the exchange accept this defense as at least partially adequate. Mendieta's 1995 essay develops what can be called the complementarity thesis: discourse ethics provides formal criteria for normative validity, while liberation ethics supplies the material criterion of the dignity of life and the perspective of the excluded Other who calls from outside the existing communication community. On this reading, neither ethics is complete alone — discourse ethics lacks a material principle, liberation ethics lacks formal universalizability criteria — and their encounter is productive precisely because each corrects a deficit in the other (Mendieta 1995). Mendieta's 1999 review essay sustains this framing, situating the *Ethics of Liberation* as a contribution that enriches rather than replaces procedural moral theory.

Spanish-language reconstructions of the exchange read the dialogue in a similar register. Hurtado López (2012) argues that both Apel and Dussel agree on the need for a universal ethics and that their divergence concerns not universalism as such but the ground on which it rests — formal-pragmatic presuppositions versus material conditions of life. The exchange, on this reconstruction, maps a genuine philosophical convergence in which Dussel's material criterion corrects a formalist deficit without dismantling the Apelian project. Vilchis Carlos (2020) reads Dussel's own texts engaging Apel — particularly his appropriation of Apel's transformation of Kantian ethics — as a productive dialogue in which Dussel distinguishes validity from truth and assigns each a complementary role, with truth tied to the affirmation of human life. Rodríguez Reyes (2024), writing as a tribute to Dussel, frames the entire exchange as a paradigm case of intercultural North–South philosophical dialogue, emphasizing its character as genuine mutual engagement across geopolitical asymmetry.

### 1.2 Apel's Concessions and What Remained Contested

What Apel conceded is itself contested in the literature. Zielinski (2019) reconstructs the textual record of Apel's responses and argues that Apel acknowledged the reality of North–South asymmetries and accepted that Part B of discourse ethics must attend to material conditions — but steadfastly refused to surrender the ideal communication community as a necessary regulative principle. The ideal community is not, for Apel, a Eurocentric presupposition; it is a transcendental condition of argumentation as such, available in principle to any participant in rational discourse. This distinction — between acknowledging real asymmetries at the level of application and refusing to abandon the transcendental structure — is the precise line that separates concession from defended ground. Rivera Sepúlveda (2013), tracing the debate across Kant, Apel, and Dussel, argues that Dussel's sharpest objection concerns not the ideal/real distinction as such but the assumption embedded in the ideal community itself: it was designed by and for Enlightenment societies of the North and presupposes conditions of participation that Latin American precarity systematically forecloses.

Dussel's own account of the dialogue — presented in the 1997 "Architectonic" article and in the *Philosophy Today* essay of the same year — is one of critical appropriation rather than rejection. He takes the formal validity principle from Apel, recognizing it as a necessary moment in any adequate ethics, but insists on subordinating it to the material criterion: the reproduction of human life is the transcendental condition that makes argumentation possible at all, not the other way around (Dussel 1997a; 1997b). The formal discourse-ethical principle is incorporated as the second of three structural moments in the ethics of liberation — the material principle comes first, the feasibility principle third. This move simultaneously honors Apel's contribution and limits its scope.

Roldán (2015) offers a threefold reconstruction of the dialogue that captures its complexity. Dussel's ethics of liberation, on Roldán's reading, is constituted through critical mediation of three traditions: it assimilates the universalizability demand from discourse ethics, assimilates the demand for a material ethics of content from communitarianism, and refuses the formalism of the first and the relativism of the second. The encounter with Apel is therefore not simply a bilateral dialogue but a triangulated appropriation in which the formal discourse-ethical moment is preserved, transformed, and subordinated.

What the exchange left unresolved — and what the secondary literature registers as such — is whether Part B constitutes an adequate answer to Dussel's challenge or a deferral of it. The Part B strategy acknowledges that liberation ethics identifies a real problem, while preserving the transcendental core of discourse ethics intact. Dussel's position, and that of scholars who read him as making an irreducible claim, is that Part B is insufficient precisely because the material condition of life is not a problem of application but a problem of grounding: it concerns what must be presupposed before discourse can begin. This disagreement — concession at the level of application versus challenge at the level of foundations — is the raw material for the subsequent debate over whether the material principle supplements discourse ethics or constitutes an independent foundation that discourse ethics cannot provide.
