## Introduction

Between 1995 and 2021 a sustained philosophical exchange developed between Enrique Dussel's ethics of liberation and the discourse ethics tradition associated with Jürgen Habermas and Karl-Otto Apel. The dialogue was institutionalized in the pages of *Philosophy & Social Criticism* and *Philosophy Today*, where Dussel published the architectural statements of his ethics (Dussel 1997a; 1997b) and Apel produced his written response (Apel and Mendieta 1996). Eduardo Mendieta, as Dussel's principal English-language translator and interpreter, mediated this exchange for Anglophone readers and supplied it with its earliest organizing framework (Mendieta 1995; 1999). Two decades later the encounter was reopened and reframed by the edited volume *Decolonizing Ethics* (Allen and Mendieta 2021), which placed the exchange within a broader decolonial critique of critical theory's normative foundations.

The philosophical crux of the encounter is the relationship between formal-procedural validity and material ethical content. Habermas and Apel ground normative validity in the symmetry conditions that ideal communicative discourse presupposes; the idealized community of arguers defines the standard against which actual discourse can be criticized. Dussel's counter-argument, laid out systematically in his 1997 architectonic article, holds that this formal principle is necessary but insufficient (Dussel 1997a). Any ethics that brackets the bodily, material conditions required for the reproduction of human life — and that presupposes as its starting point an already-included community of communicators — has excluded in advance precisely those whose exclusion makes ethical critique urgent: the poor, the colonized, the victims of the world system. The material principle of life, in Dussel's framework, is not derived from discourse but is its necessary prior condition.

A structural feature of this field requires explicit notice. The documented interlocutor is Apel, not Habermas. Apel participated in the Frankfurt and Mainz dialogues and produced written philosophical responses; the Part A / Part B distinction that became the pivot of subsequent debate is Apel's formulation (Apel and Mendieta 1996). Habermas's engagement with Dussel's challenge — particularly with the 1492 thesis that places colonial conquest at the origin of Western modernity rather than at its margins — is mediated at best, and a direct Habermasian reply to the ethics of liberation on its own terms is largely absent from the textual record. Secondary literature consequently reads the Dussel–Habermas relation through the Apel exchange. Allen's contribution to *Decolonizing Ethics* identifies this asymmetry as a structural feature of the field rather than an incidental gap: Apel stands in for the Frankfurt School in these debates, and Habermas's communicative rationality is engaged by Dussel at the level of its implications rather than through direct dialogue (Allen 2021).

This review tracks three interpretive positions that organize the secondary literature. The first, associated with Mendieta and Schelkshorn, reads the two ethics as complementary: each supplies what the other lacks, and their combination points toward a more adequate universalist ethics. The second position reads Dussel as offering an immanent correction of discourse ethics — incorporating its formal validity principle while subordinating it to a material criterion that discourse ethics cannot itself generate. The third position holds that liberation ethics displaces rather than supplements discourse ethics: the two projects are grounded in irreducibly different starting points, and the material principle of life constitutes an independent foundation that Apel's Part B cannot absorb.

The review proceeds as follows. Section 1 reconstructs the textual and historical exchange and charts the initial division between complementarity and irreducible-difference readings. Section 2 examines the philosophical crux — the status of the material principle — across the three interpretive positions, including a reading of Dussel's critique as ideology critique of the ideal speech situation. Section 3 moves to the macro-philosophical dimensions: the challenge to Habermas's genealogy of modernity and to the formal-pragmatic account of valid interlocutors. Section 4 assesses the critique from the other direction, examining internal tensions in Dussel's framework and situating his Eurocentrism charge within a wider field of convergent critiques.

## Section 1: Reconstructing the Exchange: Convergence or Irreducible Difference?

The intellectual encounter between Enrique Dussel and the Frankfurt tradition unfolded in a specific institutional and textual form: a series of exchanges published in *Philosophy & Social Criticism* and *Philosophy Today* in the mid-1990s, culminating in Dussel's systematic statement in the *Ethics of Liberation* (1998). The interlocutor who responded in writing was not Habermas but Karl-Otto Apel. This asymmetry is not incidental. The secondary literature reconstructing the dialogue — from Mendieta's early mediating essay (1995) to Zielinski's reconstruction of Apel's arguments (2019) — systematically reads Dussel's engagement with Habermasian communicative ethics through the documented Apel exchange. The result is that the field divides over a specific question: what did the Apel–Dussel dialogue actually establish? Two poles have emerged — a complementarity reading and an irreducible-difference reading — and all subsequent scholarship on the material-versus-formal question inherits this division.

### 1.1 The Part A / Part B Response and the Complementarity Thesis

Apel's published response to Dussel (Apel and Mendieta 1996) turns on a structural distinction within discourse ethics itself. Part A concerns the transcendental-pragmatic grounding of the validity principle: the ideal communication community as the counterfactual standard presupposed in any argumentative act. Part B concerns the application of this standard to historical-material conditions, including the asymmetries produced by capitalism and colonial exclusion. Apel's defense is that liberation philosophy misreads discourse ethics by treating the ideal speech situation as a sociological description of existing communicative relations; properly understood, the ideal community is a normative standard *against which* real exclusions can be criticized. Part B, he argues, can and must address the material asymmetries that Dussel foregrounds.

Several readings of the exchange accept this defense as at least partially adequate. Mendieta's 1995 essay develops what can be called the complementarity thesis: discourse ethics provides formal criteria for normative validity, while liberation ethics supplies the material criterion of the dignity of life and the perspective of the excluded Other who calls from outside the existing communication community. On this reading, neither ethics is complete alone — discourse ethics lacks a material principle, liberation ethics lacks formal universalizability criteria — and their encounter is productive precisely because each corrects a deficit in the other (Mendieta 1995). Mendieta's 1999 review essay sustains this framing, situating the *Ethics of Liberation* as a contribution that enriches rather than replaces procedural moral theory.

Spanish-language reconstructions of the exchange read the dialogue in a similar register. Hurtado López (2012) argues that both Apel and Dussel agree on the need for a universal ethics and that their divergence concerns not universalism as such but the ground on which it rests — formal-pragmatic presuppositions versus material conditions of life. The exchange, on this reconstruction, maps a genuine philosophical convergence in which Dussel's material criterion corrects a formalist deficit without dismantling the Apelian project. Vilchis Carlos (2020) reads Dussel's own texts engaging Apel — particularly his appropriation of Apel's transformation of Kantian ethics — as a productive dialogue in which Dussel distinguishes validity from truth and assigns each a complementary role, with truth tied to the affirmation of human life. Rodríguez Reyes (2024), writing as a tribute to Dussel, frames the entire exchange as a paradigm case of intercultural North–South philosophical dialogue, emphasizing its character as genuine mutual engagement across geopolitical asymmetry.

### 1.2 Apel's Concessions and What Remained Contested

What Apel conceded is itself contested in the literature. Zielinski (2019) reconstructs the textual record of Apel's responses and argues that Apel acknowledged the reality of North–South asymmetries and accepted that Part B of discourse ethics must attend to material conditions — but steadfastly refused to surrender the ideal communication community as a necessary regulative principle. The ideal community is not, for Apel, a Eurocentric presupposition; it is a transcendental condition of argumentation as such, available in principle to any participant in rational discourse. This distinction — between acknowledging real asymmetries at the level of application and refusing to abandon the transcendental structure — is the precise line that separates concession from defended ground. Rivera Sepúlveda (2013), tracing the debate across Kant, Apel, and Dussel, argues that Dussel's sharpest objection concerns not the ideal/real distinction as such but the assumption embedded in the ideal community itself: it was designed by and for Enlightenment societies of the North and presupposes conditions of participation that Latin American precarity systematically forecloses.

Dussel's own account of the dialogue — presented in the 1997 "Architectonic" article and in the *Philosophy Today* essay of the same year — is one of critical appropriation rather than rejection. He takes the formal validity principle from Apel, recognizing it as a necessary moment in any adequate ethics, but insists on subordinating it to the material criterion: the reproduction of human life is the transcendental condition that makes argumentation possible at all, not the other way around (Dussel 1997a; 1997b). The formal discourse-ethical principle is incorporated as the second of three structural moments in the ethics of liberation — the material principle comes first, the feasibility principle third. This move simultaneously honors Apel's contribution and limits its scope.

Roldán (2015) offers a threefold reconstruction of the dialogue that captures its complexity. Dussel's ethics of liberation, on Roldán's reading, is constituted through critical mediation of three traditions: it assimilates the universalizability demand from discourse ethics, assimilates the demand for a material ethics of content from communitarianism, and refuses the formalism of the first and the relativism of the second. The encounter with Apel is therefore not simply a bilateral dialogue but a triangulated appropriation in which the formal discourse-ethical moment is preserved, transformed, and subordinated.

What the exchange left unresolved — and what the secondary literature registers as such — is whether Part B constitutes an adequate answer to Dussel's challenge or a deferral of it. The Part B strategy acknowledges that liberation ethics identifies a real problem, while preserving the transcendental core of discourse ethics intact. Dussel's position, and that of scholars who read him as making an irreducible claim, is that Part B is insufficient precisely because the material condition of life is not a problem of application but a problem of grounding: it concerns what must be presupposed before discourse can begin. This disagreement — concession at the level of application versus challenge at the level of foundations — is the raw material for the subsequent debate over whether the material principle supplements discourse ethics or constitutes an independent foundation that discourse ethics cannot provide.

## Section 2: The Material/Formal Distinction: Supplement, Alternative, or Ideology Critique?

The philosophical crux of the Dussel–Habermas encounter is not primarily a dispute about scope or application but about the grounding of normativity itself. Where Habermasian and Apelian discourse ethics locate the ultimate criterion of moral validity in the formal-pragmatic presuppositions of argumentation — the symmetry conditions of the ideal communication community — Dussel insists that this formal criterion is both dependent upon and insufficient for a material principle grounded in the bodily reproduction of life and the standpoint of the excluded victim. How the secondary literature characterizes the relationship between these two grounding claims determines, in large measure, which picture of the debate one accepts: productive complementarity, immanent correction, irreducible difference, or ideological unmasking.

### 2.1 The Complementarity and Contextual-Differentiation Readings

The most widely represented position in the English-language secondary literature holds that discourse ethics and the ethics of liberation, rather than being simply opposed, address different theoretical registers of a complete moral theory. Mendieta (1995) advances this thesis explicitly: discourse ethics supplies formal criteria for the universal validity of norms, while liberation ethics supplies the material criterion — the dignity of concrete life and the obligation toward the Other who calls from outside the existing communication community. On this reading, each tradition suffers from the absence of what the other provides, and their encounter is philosophically productive rather than mutually undermining.

Schelkshorn (2018) develops a more contextually differentiated version of this position. He argues that discourse ethics and the ethics of liberation represent two distinct forms of universalistic moral theory: both are committed to universalism but ground it from different starting points responding to different historical and geopolitical situations. Discourse ethics inherits the European Enlightenment tradition of rational adjudication between conflicting interests and extends it to conditions of fallibilism and cultural pluralism; liberation ethics arises from and remains oriented toward the perspective of communities in the Global South for whom the conditions of symmetric argumentation have been systematically denied. For Schelkshorn, the two ethics are not merely formally complementary — they represent different contextual embedings of universalist aspiration. This is a more demanding claim than Mendieta's: it is not that the two theories jointly exhaust moral theory, but that they are responses to structurally different predicaments that nonetheless converge on universalist commitments.

Mills (2018) treats Dussel's critical appropriation of discourse ethics as the dominant mode of their relationship: liberation ethics takes the formal validity principle on board as a necessary but insufficient moment, incorporating it into the architectonic as the second of three structural levels — after the material principle of life-reproduction and before the feasibility principle. On this expository reconstruction, the formal-pragmatic criterion is not rejected but sublated into a richer structure.

Cobos (2015) pushes the complementarity reading to a more assertive conclusion. Examining how Latin American political thought responds to the discourse ethics tradition, Cobos argues that Apel's framework can maintain internal consistency only if subsumed under the program of liberation philosophy. This is a stronger claim than Schelkshorn's contextual differentiation: not that the two ethics respond to different situations and can coexist, but that discourse ethics requires the material and geopolitical grounding that liberation philosophy provides in order to avoid reproducing, despite itself, the very exclusions it claims to oppose. Cobos's reading thus occupies a middle position between complementarity and the irreducibility thesis developed below.

### 2.2 The Irreducibility Thesis

Against the complementarity readings, a distinct strand of the scholarship argues that the material principle of life cannot be derived from, reduced to, or even straightforwardly supplemented by the formal-pragmatic presuppositions of argumentation. Zúñiga (2021) advances this claim most directly in the Allen–Mendieta volume. He reconstructs Dussel's critique by showing that the grounding of moral norms in the formal structure of argumentative discourse leaves open the question of what is materially at stake in argument: which claims matter, and for whose lives. The material criterion — the obligation to produce and reproduce the conditions under which living subjects can survive and flourish — is not derivable from any structural feature of communicative competence. It is a genuinely independent transcendental claim that discourse ethics cannot generate from within its own resources.

This irreducibility thesis carries direct implications for how Dussel's three-moment architectonic is to be read. In his own formulation (Dussel 1997a), the formal-discursive moment is the second of three necessary levels, preceded by the material moment that grounds it and followed by the feasibility principle that orients it toward strategic action. Zúñiga's reading makes explicit what this architectonic implies: the formal is not self-sufficient and cannot supply its own material telos. The grounding relation runs from life to discourse, not the reverse — making discourse ethics, taken alone, not merely incomplete but structurally dependent on a prior criterion it cannot articulate.

Sáenz (2017) approaches the same conclusion from a different angle. Examining the role of biological and social life-reproduction in Dussel's ethics, he argues that Dussel's life-centered materialism constitutes a genuinely different ethical orientation from Habermasian proceduralism — not a supplement or a correction applied to an otherwise intact framework, but a reconception of the conditions under which ethics is possible at all. The distinction is between a theory that asks "which norms can be justified through symmetric argumentation?" and one that asks "what must be the case for living subjects to be in a position to argue at all?" These are different questions, and the second, on Sáenz's reading, is more fundamental.

Together, Zúñiga and Sáenz mark the "displacement" pole of the interpretive spectrum: the material principle does not extend or complete discourse ethics but registers a priority claim that discourse ethics is structurally unable to accommodate.

### 2.3 Dussel's Critique as Ideology Critique

A third, sharper reading recasts the entire dispute not as a question about the relation between two ethical theories but as a question about the ideological function of one of them. Sørensen (2011) argues that Dussel's critique of Habermasian discourse ethics is most accurately understood as a critique of ideology in the Frankfurt School sense: the ideal speech situation does not merely fail to include the poor and colonized as a contingent matter of application — it functions ideologically by naturalizing their exclusion. By grounding moral validity in the formal symmetry conditions of actual argumentation among existing participants, discourse ethics implicitly ratifies the inequalities that prevent the excluded from entering communicative communities on equal terms. The ideal is not a regulative counterfactual that exposes real asymmetries as deficits to be remedied; it is a normative standard that, in practice, takes the prevailing communication community as its reference point and thereby occludes the voices of those systematically excluded from it.

On Sørensen's reading, Dussel's counter-move — grounding ethics in the materiality of victims, in the embodied negativity of those the existing system renders invisible — is not a supplement to discourse ethics but an unmasking of its ideological character. Liberation ethics, from this standpoint, does not stand alongside discourse ethics in a complementary relationship; it exposes a structural feature of discourse ethics that complementarity readings obscure.

Dallmayr (2004) situates this critique within a longer genealogy of thinking from the "underside" of Eurocentric reason. Drawing a comparative line between Adorno's negative dialectics and Dussel's philosophy of exteriority, Dallmayr argues that both thinkers share a commitment to thinking what the dominant rationality excludes: Adorno's "non-identity" and Dussel's "exteriority of the Other" name structurally analogous moves against the closure of Western reason. Applied to the Habermas encounter, this framing suggests that the communicative ideal — for all its emancipatory intent — reproduces modernity's exclusionary logic at the level of formal normativity. Dussel's challenge is thus located not only within debates about moral theory but within the broader critical-theoretical tradition's unresolved tension between its universalist aspirations and the historical conditions of its emergence.

The ideology-critique reading sharpens the stakes of the entire debate. Where the complementarity reading frames the Dussel–Habermas encounter as a cooperative refinement of moral theory — each side contributing what the other lacks — Sørensen's and Dallmayr's readings frame it as a structural confrontation: whether the normative core of discourse ethics can be made to account for the excluded, or whether its very structure is part of what needs to be overcome. This distinction maps directly onto the supplement/correction/displacement spectrum that organizes the broader secondary literature, placing the ideology-critique reading firmly at the displacement pole while also connecting it to the question, developed in Section 3, of whether Habermas's genealogy of modernity can accommodate the colonial genealogy Dussel insists upon.

The material/formal distinction thus ramifies into three distinct scholarly positions: a complementarity or contextual-differentiation reading (Mendieta, Schelkshorn, Mills, Cobos), an irreducibility thesis (Zúñiga, Sáenz), and an ideology-critique reading (Sørensen, Dallmayr). What remains unresolved across these positions is the grounding question: whether the material principle of life is an independent foundation that discourse ethics presupposes without acknowledging, or whether it represents a genuinely external standpoint from which the formalist tradition can be exposed and surpassed.

## Section 3: Modernity and Communicative Rationality: Eurocentrism at the Genealogical and Pragmatic Levels

Dussel's challenge to Habermas operates at two analytically separable but historically entangled levels. At the genealogical level, the 1492 thesis contests Habermas's intra-European account of the "philosophical discourse of modernity," arguing that colonialism is constitutive rather than peripheral to modern rationality. At the pragmatic level, the charge is that the formal-pragmatic reconstruction of communicative reason presupposes an already-included lifeworld community from which the victims of colonial modernity are excluded by structural design, not incidental accident. Both levels converge on the same interpretive question that structures the broader debate: does Dussel's critique supplement the Habermasian project, correct it from within, or displace it?

### 3.1 Transmodernity versus the "Unfinished Project"

Habermas's account of modernity as an "unfinished project" treats modernity's pathologies as internal contradictions generated by the selective rationalization of a lifeworld that communicative reason could, in principle, rectify. For Dussel, this framing is symptomatic of the very problem it seeks to diagnose: it proceeds from within the European philosophical tradition, taking the Reformation, the Enlightenment, and Kantian subject philosophy as the genealogical origins of modernity. Dussel's counter-genealogy begins with 1492 — the year that, in his account, Europe first constituted itself as the "center" of a world-system by subjugating its colonial "Other." The "myth of modernity" is not an internal contradiction but a sacrificial ideology that justified colonial violence as the price of bringing civilization to the periphery. The conquest, on this account, is not peripheral to modernity but generative of it (Maldonado-Torres 2008).

Pinton (2025) offers a focused reconstruction of the genealogical argument's philosophical core: the Cartesian *ego cogito* — which Habermas treats as the origin of subject-centered reason — is historically posterior to the colonial *ego conquiro*, the subjectivity constituted through the conquest and objectification of the Americas. On Dussel's account, the philosophical foundations of modernity's self-understanding are built on an erasure. Pimenta (2025) extends this genealogical reconstruction across five phases of Dussel's intellectual career, showing that the intervention is not merely a contextual supplement to European philosophy but a "geopolitical turn" that relocates the question of modernity from European self-reflection to a world-historical framework in which the Americas, Africa, and Asia are constitutive rather than peripheral. World-systems analysis (Wallerstein) functions in this framework as a philosophical resource, not merely background context.

Against the Habermasian option of completing modernity through communicative reason, Dussel proposes transmodernity: a pluriversal project in which the cultures and traditions that modernity colonized but did not eliminate contribute their excluded resources to a genuinely global dialogue. The key distinction, as Alcoff (2012) reconstructs it, is between Habermas's "internalism" — the view that modernity's rational potential can be recovered through an internal reconstruction of communicative reason from within the European tradition — and the transmodern claim that such internalism reproduces the colonial erasure of non-European contributions. Alcoff situates this against feminist standpoint epistemology to argue that transmodernism is not merely a cultural claim but a substantive epistemological one: the standpoint from which rationality is reconstructed is not neutral, and the Habermasian starting point forecloses the contributions it would need to be genuinely universal.

Grosfoguel (2011) presses this argument in a world-systems register, contending that Habermasian communicative rationality and its universal validity claims are products of a particular locus of enunciation — the European *ego cogito* rooted in the *ego conquiro* — and cannot serve as the basis for cross-cultural validity claims without reproducing coloniality. Transmodernity, in this reading, names not the completion of modernity but its pluralization: a framework that integrates decolonized epistemological contributions rather than subordinating them to a formally reconstructed European rationalism. Dussel himself (2012) formulates transmodernity as a "South-South dialogue" among peripheral cultures, proposing the analectic method as the philosophical procedure appropriate to this pluriversal project and explicitly identifying interculturality — symmetric dialogue among excluded traditions — as replacing rather than supplementing the Habermasian ideal speech situation. His late essay (Dussel 2025) extends this to a critique of the Eurocentric periodization of world history that Habermas's "philosophical discourse of modernity" presupposes, arguing that postmodernism's rejection of modernity fails because it remains within the European frame.

A notable gap in this body of scholarship is sustained philosophical comparison at the level of *normative* theory: most treatments address the historical-genealogical dimension without following through to the implications for communicative-rationality-based normativity. How transmodernity's pluriversal horizon would generate binding normative standards — rather than a standpoint claim — remains largely unexplored in the secondary literature. Maldonado-Torres (2011) comes closest in his reconstruction of how Dussel's analectic method enables a position that neither accepts the "unfinished project" framing nor retreats to postmodern relativism, but does not resolve the normative question. Suárez (2021) treats transmodernity as a philosophical *method* rather than a political program, tracing its uptake in Castro-Gómez's transmodern republicanism, but the normative articulation of the transmodern alternative is a structural gap the secondary literature consistently flags without filling.

### 3.2 Communicative Rationality, Analectics, and Interculturality

If the genealogical critique targets the philosophy of history presupposed by Habermas's account of modernity, the analectical critique targets the formal-pragmatic core of communicative rationality itself. Dussel's charge, as Mendieta (1995) maps it, concerns precisely who counts as a valid interlocutor: discourse ethics grounds normativity in the formal presuppositions of argumentation and an ideal communication community, but this community already presupposes the inclusion of participants who share certain background lifeworld conditions. The victims and excluded peoples of the world-system do not participate in this community; their exclusion is structurally produced by the same colonial modernity that generates the lifeworld from which communicative rationality is reconstructed.

Dussel's response is to argue that the community of communication must be reconstructed from the standpoint of those excluded from it. He accepts Apel's formal principle — that valid norms require the consent of affected participants — but subordinates it to two additional principles: a material principle of life that requires ethics to begin from the demand to sustain human life, and a feasibility principle that anchors ethical proposals to historical realizability (Dussel 1997). In this architecture, universal pragmatics captures only the formal-procedural dimension and cannot generate the critical negativity required when interlocutors are structurally excluded from any actual or counterfactual communication community.

Kim (2019) provides the most careful English-language reconstruction of the analectical standpoint as an epistemological response to this problem, distinguishing between philosophies that are "alterior" — emerging from culturally different but non-subordinated positions — and those that are "analectical" in Dussel's sense: emerging from a politically subordinated, excluded position within the modern world-system. The analectical standpoint speaks from outside the existing community of communication; it is not merely culturally other but structurally marginalized. On this reading, analectics does not merely extend or radicalize formal pragmatics but reconstructs the community of communication from a standpoint it had systematically excluded. Whether this constitutes a genuine alternative or a radicalization from within turns on the question Irvine raises — addressed in Section 4 — of whether such a standpoint is achievable without relying on the philosophical tools it claims to transcend.

Mendieta (2018) articulates the reformist response to this challenge, proposing an ethics of "global co-responsibility" that preserves universal pragmatics' universalist aspirations while acknowledging the asymmetric material conditions liberation philosophy identifies. On this account, discourse ethics needs extension rather than replacement: a modified universal pragmatics that takes global asymmetry as part of its starting conditions can absorb the challenge without abandoning the formal-pragmatic framework. Mekonnen (2018) occupies a related position, arguing that communicative rationality can achieve genuine universality only if reconstructed from the standpoint of the excluded — a reformist reading in which the emancipation of the Other is the precondition, not the endpoint, of universal communication.

The most thoroughgoing replacement position comes from the intercultural philosophy tradition. Demenchonok (2025) traces how Fornet-Betancourt's "intercultural transformation of philosophy" proposes a "contextualized and concrete universality" grounded in the cultural embeddedness of each tradition, explicitly replacing Habermasian abstract universalism rather than supplementing it. Where Mendieta sees extension as sufficient, Fornet-Betancourt (via Demenchonok) argues that the formal-pragmatic reconstruction is itself the problem: it abstracts from cultural embeddedness in a way that systematically privileges those traditions whose cultural particularity has been rendered invisible by colonial universalization.

At both levels — the genealogical and the pragmatic — the organizing question recurs in the same form: is the Eurocentric structure of Habermasian modernity a correctable limitation or a constitutive feature? The secondary literature does not resolve this. What it does consistently show is that the challenge at the pragmatic level is more philosophically precise than the challenge at the genealogical level: the dispute over who counts as a valid interlocutor in the community of communication is a determinate philosophical question to which analectics, global co-responsibility, and intercultural transformation offer competing answers. Whether any of these answers generates an operable normative theory — rather than a standpoint claim or a methodological orientation — is the question that the literature foregrounds without settling.

## Section 4: Assessing the Critique: Defenses, Internal Tensions, and Comparative Standpoints

### 4.1 Internal Critiques of Dussel's Framework

The secondary literature does not only adjudicate between Dussel and Habermas; it also presses back on whether Dussel's own counter-position is philosophically coherent. Two lines of internal criticism stand out.

Irvine (2011) mounts the most direct ontological challenge. Dussel's analectic method purports to gain a standpoint outside Western ontological closure by orienting thought toward the "revelation of the Other" — an ethical demand that comes from beyond being and inaugurates the movement toward trans-ontology. Irvine argues that this move is unavailable to any philosophy that draws, as Dussel does, on the phenomenological resources of Levinas and Heidegger. The conceptual apparatus of alterity, exteriority, and the face of the Other remains intelligible only within the Western ontological horizon it is meant to transcend. For Irvine, Dussel's positive alternative to communicative rationality thus faces a performative tension: it cannot establish the standpoint of the excluded Other without deploying the philosophical tools it claims to surpass. If this objection holds, it bears directly on the Habermas debate — the critical leverage of the analectic depends on a claim to exteriority that may not be coherently available.

Quintana-Paz (2014) identifies a different internal tension, this time at the level of Dussel's political philosophy. Dussel appropriates three criteria from Apel and Habermas — a formal-discursive criterion (valid norms require the consent of those affected), a material criterion (the welfare of all), and a feasibility criterion — to legitimize political revolution including the use of violence. Quintana-Paz argues that each of these criteria is conceptually incompatible with violence: the formal criterion requires unconstrained discourse rather than coercion; the material criterion presupposes the preservation of life; and the feasibility criterion, when applied consistently, rules out violence as an adequate means. The upshot is that Dussel's effort to justify political violence on liberation-ethical grounds exposes an unresolved dependence on Habermasian-Apelian normative categories — the very categories his critique of formalism was supposed to move beyond.

Naicker (2023) extends the criticism to decolonial theory as a broader intellectual formation. He argues that the decolonial turn, whose contemporary versions draw heavily on Dussel's foundational arguments, reduces Western epistemology to a monolithic subject-object dualism and thereby misrepresents the actual heterogeneity of European philosophical traditions. This reductive move, Naicker contends, amounts to a form of essentialism that mirrors the colonial stereotypes decolonial theory aims to overcome — making the framework self-defeating. A further angle is supplied by Guennouni Idrissi (2025) through a South–South comparison: reading Dussel alongside the Moroccan philosopher Taha Abderrahmane, he shows that Dussel's pluriversal framework can itself misread a non-Western interlocutor's project as abstract universalism when it is in fact a form of decolonial ethical specificity rooted in the Islamic tradition. Dussel's critique of Habermasian universalism does not automatically generate sensitivity to non-Latin-American forms of decolonial thought.

### 4.2 Comparative Critiques of Habermasian Universalism

Situating Dussel's critique within the broader field of objections to Habermasian universalism reveals both its distinctiveness and the points at which it converges with other lines of critique.

The feminist critique represented by Meehan (2000) targets Habermas's formalism and proceduralism from a different angle. Where Dussel faults discourse ethics for ignoring the material suffering of those who cannot enter any communication community, feminist critics fault it for presupposing a subject of discourse that is implicitly gendered — rationality construed as identical with language use, and intersubjectivity reconstructed without attention to embodied, affective dimensions of selfhood. Meehan argues that while Habermas's account of radically intersubjective subjectivity offers resources useful to feminist theory, his reductive identification of subjectivity with linguistic rationality requires revision. The two critiques — liberation-philosophical and feminist — thus converge on formalism as the target but locate its problematic consequences differently: in the erasure of the victim's material cry, on Dussel's account, and in the erasure of gendered embodiment, on the feminist account.

Papastephanou (2011) pursues the Eurocentrism charge from within European scholarship, examining whether Habermas and Derrida's 2003 joint plea for a new European political identity can escape Eurocentrism simply by endorsing post-national universalism. Her conclusion — that subtler Eurocentrisms pervade even the most self-critical European universalism — converges with Dussel's diagnosis but arrives through a different route: not from the material standpoint of the colonized victim but from an analysis of the structural assumptions embedded in European self-narration. Radhakrishnan (2025) pursues the most methodologically fundamental version of this challenge: the Frankfurt School's commitment to immanent critique — judging social practices by norms internal to those practices — is structurally unable to address colonial legacies because the norms immanent to modernity have themselves been shaped by colonial power. Decolonial critique, on this account, cannot be a radicalization of immanent critique; it requires a methodological step outside it. This challenges not only Habermas but also those readings of Dussel, such as Allen's (2021), that assimilate liberation ethics to an immanent-critical framework.

Ahmad (2025) offers a recent parallel critique, arguing that Habermas's universalism is structurally oriented "to all" rather than "from all" — delivered outward from a particular European-Enlightenment standpoint rather than constructed through genuinely inclusive participation. The charge echoes Dussel's critique of the ideal communication community but is formulated independently, from the vantage of Habermas's public interventions on Palestine. The recurrence of this structural objection across otherwise unconnected critical traditions lends weight to the claim that the issue is systematic rather than contingent on Dussel's specific philosophical framework.

What the comparative picture reveals is that defenses of Habermas specifically against the Eurocentrism charge — as opposed to defenses against feminist or communitarian objections — remain thin in the English-language secondary literature. Apel's Part A/Part B distinction, explored in domain-1 scholarship, is the closest to such a defense, but it operates at the level of discourse ethics rather than at the level of Habermas's modernity thesis or his concept of the lifeworld. This asymmetry in the secondary record is itself a finding: the liberation-philosophical charge has not been met by a sustained Habermasian reply.

## Conclusion

The secondary literature on Dussel's critique of Habermas organizes itself, with notable consistency, around a three-position spectrum. Toward the complementarity end stand Mendieta and Schelkshorn: Mendieta (1995) argues that discourse ethics and liberation ethics each supply what the other lacks, making them jointly necessary components of a complete moral theory, while Schelkshorn (2018) preserves a similar complementarity reading by grounding the two ethics in different geopolitical situations rather than treating one as the corrective of the other. Toward the displacement end stand Zúñiga (2021), who argues that the material principle of life cannot be derived from the formal-pragmatic presuppositions of argumentation and thus represents an independent foundation that discourse ethics cannot absorb, alongside Maldonado-Torres and Grosfoguel, whose decolonial readings treat communicative universalism as a particular locus of enunciation rather than a genuinely post-conventional standard. Between these poles, Sørensen (2011) and Radhakrishnan (2025) occupy a structurally distinct position: for them the question is not supplementation or displacement but unmasking — discourse ethics and the broader methodology of immanent critique function ideologically, naturalizing the exclusions that prevent the poor and colonized from entering communicative communities on equal terms.

A recurring structural feature runs through all positions: Apel, not Habermas, is the documented interlocutor. The foundational written exchange is between Apel and Dussel (Apel and Mendieta 1996), and secondary literature consistently reads the Dussel–Habermas relationship *through* this Apel mediation. The strongest disputes in the secondary literature — over the genealogy of modernity, the structure of valid interlocutorship in universal pragmatics, and the adequacy of immanent critique for colonial critique — lack a direct Habermasian reply. Defenses of Habermas specifically against the Eurocentrism charge are sparse; the available responses to feminist (Meehan 2000) and European-internal (Papastephanou 2011) versions of the charge do not translate easily to Dussel's material-liberation standpoint. Ahmad's (2025) recent "ethnic universalism" critique, developed independently of the liberation-philosophy tradition, suggests the absence of a direct Habermasian self-defense is a genuine gap in the field rather than a resolved question.

Four open questions emerge from this survey. First, the grounding status of the material principle of life remains contested: Zúñiga (2021) makes the most rigorous case for its irreducibility, but this claim has not received a sustained discourse-ethical counter-argument. Second, it remains unclear whether transmodernity and analectics constitute an operable normative theory or a standpoint claim — the literature provides the diagnosis but not a worked-out alternative that could be tested against concrete normative problems. Third, Radhakrishnan (2025) and Allen (2021) raise in sharpest form whether Frankfurt School immanent critique can be decolonized from within or must be replaced; the question cuts across the supplement/correction/displacement spectrum and has not been resolved. Fourth, direct defenses of Habermas against the liberation-ethics charge of Eurocentrism are scarce: Apel's Part A/Part B distinction is the standing response, but its adequacy is precisely what the irreducibility readings contest.

A future contribution might position itself most productively at the intersection of the second and third gaps — testing whether analectics can be rendered as normative theory, and engaging the Irvine (2011) and Quintana-Paz (2014) objections about internal incoherence, while confronting Radhakrishnan's challenge that any methodology anchored in immanent norms of modernity forecloses rather than enables the critique it promises.
