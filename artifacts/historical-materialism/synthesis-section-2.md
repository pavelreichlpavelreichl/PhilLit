## Section 1: Codifying the Doctrine — Engels, Second International Orthodoxy, and the Genealogy of "Economic Determinism"

"Economic determinism" functions in later Marxist debate as a target label more than a position anyone straightforwardly held, and locating its origin is itself a contested historiographical exercise. A significant strand of recent scholarship treats the doctrine as a product of Engels and the Second International's systematizers—Kautsky and Plekhanov above all—rather than of Marx himself. Byron (2022) states this divergence thesis in its strongest form, arguing that "Dialectical Materialism" and a law-governed, scientific "Historical Materialism" are terminological and doctrinal inventions attributable to Engels and the Second International: Marx himself never used either phrase, and his mature works and letters, on Byron's reading, explicitly reject their content. On this account Engels functioned as the intermediary who transmitted a distorted "Marxism" to the Second International. Yet even this target is not stable under scrutiny. A body of rehabilitative scholarship on Engels, Kautsky, and Plekhanov individually complicates any clean two-stage narrative in which Marx's thought was subtle and his epigones crude.

### Subsection 1.1: Engels and *Anti-Dühring* as the Only Systematic Statement

*Anti-Dühring* (Engels 1962 [1878]) occupies a structurally unusual position in this debate because, in Hal Draper's phrase as cited by Blackledge (2017), it is "the only more or less systematic presentation of Marxism" produced by either Marx or Engels in their lifetimes, and it carried Marx's own "seal of approval." Blackledge (2017) argues from this that any reinterpretation of Marx which seeks to reject or downplay Engels's contribution must first contend with Marx's endorsement of the very text scholars use to indict Engels—a fact that complicates Byron's (2022) effort to separate a divergent "Engelsian Marxism" from Marx's own critical method. Blackledge (2025) extends this rehabilitative reading empirically, arguing that Engels's military journalism on the Crimean War displays a non-mechanical historical materialism, analyzing military organization through an interplay of technology, social relations, and contingent political struggle rather than the "mechanical materialism and economic reductionism" for which Engels is often criticized. Hollander (2011) supplies an economics-focused counterweight, treating Engels as an independent, competent political economist through his early writings and his editorial completion of *Capital* volumes 2 and 3, which likewise complicates any simple story of Engels as a vulgarizing distorter.

### Subsection 1.2: Kautsky, Plekhanov, and the Bernstein Revisionism Controversy

Kautsky's reputation follows a similar rehabilitative arc. Blackledge (2006) argues that the dismissal of Kautsky's Marxism as "mechanical or even Darwinian evolutionism" is a caricature that obscures a sophisticated historiographical practice—evident in his studies of the early Church, the Reformation, and contemporary Russia and America—especially before 1910, distinguishing this earlier work from his subsequent political decline. Gronow (2016) traces how Kautsky's theory of capitalism nonetheless became identified with "Marxism" as such for the Second International through specific interpretive choices vis-à-vis Marx's own critique of political economy, while Lewis (2017) situates the broader historiographical stakes of rehabilitating Kautsky against the "renegade" reputation fixed by Lenin's polemics. Rogers (2015) reconstructs the prehistory of the Bernstein-Kautsky *Revisionismusstreit*, showing the two figures' understandings of "the meaning of Marxism" diverging before the controversy became public party conflict. Steger (1992) frames the resulting dispute as centrally ethical: Bernstein challenged Kautsky's deterministic, "scientific" self-presentation of orthodoxy by arguing that socialism required an independent Kantian ethical foundation rather than a guaranteed outcome of economic law. Plekhanov's 1895 treatise (Plekhanov 1947 [1895]) presents historical materialism as a "monist" doctrine unifying explanation of ideas, institutions, and social change under the development of productive forces—an explicit scientific self-presentation paralleling Kautsky's. Sockol (2023) argues this "vulgar Marxist" image of Plekhanov as narrowly economic-determinist and indifferent to cultural forces is itself a distorting simplification, locating his fin-de-siècle writings on art within a broader activist aesthetic and anti-Kantian polemic.

**Connection**: Baring (2023) bridges directly to Section 2 by showing that Lukács's foundational denunciation of Kautsky as "vulgar Marxism"—for transmitting Marxist conclusions without dialectical method—reproduces Kautsky's own pedagogical structure of disseminating simplified scientific socialism. The label "vulgar Marxism," which Western Marxism will define itself against, thus has a more entangled genealogy than the standard origin story assumes.
