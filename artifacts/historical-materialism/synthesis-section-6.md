## Section 5: Political Marxism — Class Relations and the Transition to Capitalism

Political Marxism offers a third post-orthodox reconstruction of historical materialism, distinct from both the functionalist and individualist positions surveyed in Section 4. Where analytical Marxism debates whether the development of productive forces or class struggle bears explanatory primacy in the abstract, Political Marxism answers the question historically, through a close reconstruction of a single transition: the emergence of agrarian capitalism in England. Brenner's founding intervention in the "Brenner Debate" argues that comparable demographic and commercial trends across late-medieval England and France produced sharply divergent long-run outcomes — capitalist agriculture in England, continued peasant proprietorship and stagnation in France — because of differences in the class structure of lordship and the contingent outcomes of lord-peasant struggle over property rights, not because of differing exposure to trade or population pressure (Brenner 1976). His 1982 extension traces this argument comparatively across England, France, and Eastern Europe, showing how the specific balance of class power determined whether agriculture was reorganized along capitalist lines (Brenner 1982). Both essays reject a premise Brenner takes liberal and orthodox-Marxist historians to share alike: that commercialization or demographic pressure can, by themselves, explain the rise of capitalism.

Wood generalizes Brenner's historical argument into a systematic theory of capitalism-specific "social property relations": capitalism is historically distinctive in separating "the economic" from "the political," extracting surplus through market-dependent economic compulsion rather than the direct politico-coercive extraction characteristic of feudal or ancient modes of production (Wood 1995). Her later synthesis argues that capitalism accordingly emerged only once, in the specific and contingent conditions of English agrarian class relations, and that treating capitalism as a transhistorical extension of trade or "improvement" obscures this historical specificity — the "commercialization model" shared, on her reading, by both liberal and orthodox-Marxist historiography (Wood 2002). Comninel extends the same class-relational method to the paradigmatic case of "bourgeois revolution," arguing that the French Revolution is better explained as an internal conflict within a single office-holding, rent-extracting ruling class over the political forms of surplus extraction than as a struggle between a rising capitalist bourgeoisie and a feudal aristocracy — a reconstruction offered against both revisionist historians (Furet, Cobban) and orthodox "bourgeois revolution" narratives (Comninel 1987). Teschke identifies a further gap in this domestic-class focus, arguing that Political Marxism's critique of bourgeois-revolution theory needs to be extended to the international and geopolitical dimensions of state formation that both orthodox accounts and Political Marxism's own agrarian-centered analyses have neglected (Teschke 2005); Post continues this line of debate, defending a social-property-relations account of specific revolutionary conjunctures against Neil Davidson's attempted rescue of the bourgeois-revolution concept (Post 2019).

In brief, Brenner's own essay already charges Wallerstein and Frank's world-systems and dependency approaches with a "neo-Smithian Marxism" that locates capitalism's dynamism in trade and exchange rather than in transformed relations of production — a dispute Section 7.1 adjudicates in full.

Political Marxism's coherence has since been contested on two fronts. From within, Knafo and Teschke (2020) argue that the tradition, despite its historicist origins in Brenner's emphasis on class agency and conflict, has increasingly drifted toward a structuralist account of capitalism's self-reproducing "rules of reproduction" — the very determinism it was meant to displace — tracing this ambivalence back to Brenner's own founding texts; Pal's (2021) introduction to the resulting *Historical Materialism* symposium surveys the resulting range of positions, from radical historicist reformulation to defenses of structural "rules." From without, Anievas and Nişancıoğlu (2013) charge Brenner's Anglo-centric account with Eurocentrism, arguing via Trotsky's theory of uneven and combined development that international determinations — Mongol invasion, Ottoman expansion, New World colonization — actively shaped capitalism's north-western European trajectory in ways an intra-European class-relational account omits. Moreno Zacarés (2018) supplies an empirical test of the thesis outside its paradigm case, finding that pre-capitalist property relations persisted in Catalan agriculture and that the region's capitalist breakthrough instead originated in eighteenth-century proto-industrial struggles, contrary to Brenner's own suggestion of a parallel Catalan transition. As Chibber's (2011) later comparative assessment of Brenner's and Cohen's rival reconstructions of historical materialism confirms, Political Marxism remains, alongside analytical Marxism, one of the two principal post-1970s reconstructions against which subsequent historical-materialist theorizing measures itself.
