## Section 8: Contemporary Defenses and the Postcolonial Challenge

Writing after the collapse of the political conditions—an ascendant labor movement, an existing socialist state—that once lent historical materialism external plausibility, Chibber (2011) offers the review's most direct post-Cohen verdict on the primacy thesis. Examining two influential attempts to repair Cohen's classical, technologically deterministic reconstruction—by Erik Olin Wright and colleagues, and by Alan Carling—Chibber (2011) argues that both rescue attempts are largely unsuccessful, and that insofar as they do render the theory more plausible, they do so only by converting it, "willy-nilly," into a class-struggle theory. He takes this to spell the demise of the classical, technologically deterministic version of historical materialism, while insisting the result is not a lapse into voluntarism: a class-struggle-centered materialism survives the collapse of the productive-forces-primacy core. Read alongside Section 4's internal analytical-Marxist debate between functionalists, individualists, and anti-reductionists, and Section 5's Political Marxist reconstruction of historical change around class-relational property forms, Chibber's assessment converges with both currents in relocating explanatory weight from technological development to class struggle, even though the three literatures arrive at this convergence largely independently of one another.

Chibber's second major intervention shifts from adjudicating internal analytical-Marxist disputes to defending historical materialism's geographic scope. Against Subaltern Studies and postcolonial theory's claim that capital's "universalizing tendencies" are a Eurocentric fiction imposed on non-Western social formations, Chibber (2013) argues that historical materialism's core categories—class formation, capitalist competition, structural compulsion—generalize across colonial and postcolonial contexts, and that postcolonial theorists' insistence on the radical difference of the non-Western world rests on mischaracterizations of both Marx's theory and the actual history of bourgeois revolution in the West. This argument, and the sustained controversy it provoked, is documented in Lazarus's (2016) sympathetic-critical response. Lazarus credits Chibber's demonstration that capital's universalizing tendency need not entail cultural homogenization or the progressive spread of "Western" norms as a genuine materialist contribution, but argues that Chibber conflates the historiographic project of Subaltern Studies with "postcolonial theory" more broadly, misrepresenting both traditions and the institutional relationship between them. Lazarus further contends that Chibber's diagnosis of postcolonial theory as "Eurocentric" mischaracterizes what is better understood as a conjuncturally distinct version of "Third Worldism"—a dispute that, on Lazarus's own account, remains unresolved rather than settled by either side, and that connects back to Section 7.2's treatment of Eurocentrism charges against world-systems and dependency theory.

Closing this arc, the introduction to the *Oxford Handbook of Karl Marx* (Prew, Rotta, Smith, and Vidal 2018) offers a synthetic retrospective across sociology, economics, political science, philosophy, geography, and ecology, arguing that Marx's foundational categories—historical materialism among them—continue to be "defended, elaborated and applied" in contemporary social science rather than superseded by repeated declarations of Marxism's obsolescence.
