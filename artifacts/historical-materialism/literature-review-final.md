## Introduction

Historical materialism is conventionally summarized through the 1859 "Preface" to *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy*: society's economic structure, comprising definite relations of production, arises on a given level of development of the material productive forces; this structure forms the real foundation on which a legal and political superstructure arises; and social revolution occurs when relations of production, having long functioned as forms of development of the productive forces, turn into their fetters (Wolff and Leopold 2025). This formulation has, in Wolff and Leopold's (2025) description, "achieved canonical status" as the compressed statement of the theory. Yet its canonicity is in part an artifact of timing rather than of any deliberate ranking by Marx himself: the Preface was composed within roughly a year of the *Grundrisse* (1857-58), a manuscript whose treatment of production, labor, and historical categories is considerably less deterministic in cast, and which remained substantially unavailable to readers until its first complete translation appeared in 1973 (Musto 2008). The theory's dominant twentieth-century face was therefore shaped as much by which texts were in print as by any settled judgment about which most fully expressed Marx's views.

Underneath this reception-historical asymmetry lies a tension internal to Marx's own writing that the chapters that follow will repeatedly revisit. Satz (1989) identifies two distinct, never explicitly reconciled explanations of historical change in Marx's corpus: in *The German Ideology*, the growth of productive powers is the fundamental cause, with social forms changing to accommodate further productive development; in the *Communist Manifesto* and the *Grundrisse*, by contrast, it is class struggle over specific conditions of oppression that determines both when an old social form is replaced and what replaces it. Marx nowhere specifies whether these are one mechanism or two (Satz 1989). Every subsequent current surveyed in this review — orthodox, humanist, structuralist, analytical, class-relational — can be read as adjudicating this unresolved dualism between productive-forces primacy and class-relational primacy.

Compounding the difficulty, the *German Ideology*'s "Feuerbach chapter," long read as Marx and Engels's first systematic statement of the theory, is now understood as a twentieth-century editorial construction rather than a manuscript its authors intended to publish as a unified text (Carver and Blank 2014); Comninel (2018) further shows that its treatment of the division of labor and the origins of class society is less determinate than the compressed 1859 Preface later suggested. A parallel philological debate concerns periodization: whether a "young" Marx of alienation and Left Hegelian humanism gives way to a "mature," scientific Marx, or whether this narrative is itself a retrospective effect of when Marx's manuscripts were published and read (Musto 2015; Cowling 2023). Fracchia (2004) adds that even the question of which text represents Marx's methodological maturity — the 1845 *German Ideology*, per Marx's own 1859 self-assessment, or the 1857-58 *Grundrisse*, per Postone's later reading — remains contested.

This review excludes New Materialism and Actor-Network approaches and does not claim comprehensive coverage of non-Anglophone (Chinese, Latin American) reception; these are acknowledged limitations rather than judgments of irrelevance. What follows traces how successive post-Marx currents each define themselves against a prior current's reading of the base/superstructure relation — beginning with the doctrine's codification into "orthodox" determinism.

## Section 1: Codifying the Doctrine: Engels, Second International Orthodoxy, and the Genealogy of "Economic Determinism"

"Economic determinism" functions in later Marxist debate as a target label more than a position anyone straightforwardly held, and locating its origin is itself a contested historiographical exercise. A significant strand of recent scholarship treats the doctrine as a product of Engels and the Second International's systematizers—Kautsky and Plekhanov above all—rather than of Marx himself. Byron (2022) states this divergence thesis in its strongest form, arguing that "Dialectical Materialism" and a law-governed, scientific "Historical Materialism" are terminological and doctrinal inventions attributable to Engels and the Second International: Marx himself never used either phrase, and his mature works and letters, on Byron's reading, explicitly reject their content. On this account Engels functioned as the intermediary who transmitted a distorted "Marxism" to the Second International. Yet even this target is not stable under scrutiny. A body of rehabilitative scholarship on Engels, Kautsky, and Plekhanov individually complicates any clean two-stage narrative in which Marx's thought was subtle and his epigones crude.

### 1.1 Engels and *Anti-Dühring* as the Only Systematic Statement

*Anti-Dühring* (Engels 1962 [1878]) occupies a structurally unusual position in this debate because, in Hal Draper's phrase as cited by Blackledge (2017), it is "the only more or less systematic presentation of Marxism" produced by either Marx or Engels in their lifetimes, and it carried Marx's own "seal of approval." Blackledge (2017) argues from this that any reinterpretation of Marx which seeks to reject or downplay Engels's contribution must first contend with Marx's endorsement of the very text scholars use to indict Engels—a fact that complicates Byron's (2022) effort to separate a divergent "Engelsian Marxism" from Marx's own critical method. Blackledge (2025) extends this rehabilitative reading empirically, arguing that Engels's military journalism on the Crimean War displays a non-mechanical historical materialism, analyzing military organization through an interplay of technology, social relations, and contingent political struggle rather than the "mechanical materialism and economic reductionism" for which Engels is often criticized. Hollander (2011) supplies an economics-focused counterweight, treating Engels as an independent, competent political economist through his early writings and his editorial completion of *Capital* volumes 2 and 3, which likewise complicates any simple story of Engels as a vulgarizing distorter.

### 1.2 Kautsky, Plekhanov, and the Bernstein Revisionism Controversy

Kautsky's reputation follows a similar rehabilitative arc. Blackledge (2006) argues that the dismissal of Kautsky's Marxism as "mechanical or even Darwinian evolutionism" is a caricature that obscures a sophisticated historiographical practice—evident in his studies of the early Church, the Reformation, and contemporary Russia and America—especially before 1910, distinguishing this earlier work from his subsequent political decline. Gronow (2016) traces how Kautsky's theory of capitalism nonetheless became identified with "Marxism" as such for the Second International through specific interpretive choices vis-à-vis Marx's own critique of political economy, while Lewis (2017) situates the broader historiographical stakes of rehabilitating Kautsky against the "renegade" reputation fixed by Lenin's polemics. Rogers (2015) reconstructs the prehistory of the Bernstein-Kautsky *Revisionismusstreit*, showing the two figures' understandings of "the meaning of Marxism" diverging before the controversy became public party conflict. Steger (1992) frames the resulting dispute as centrally ethical: Bernstein challenged Kautsky's deterministic, "scientific" self-presentation of orthodoxy by arguing that socialism required an independent Kantian ethical foundation rather than a guaranteed outcome of economic law. Plekhanov's 1895 treatise (Plekhanov 1947 [1895]) presents historical materialism as a "monist" doctrine unifying explanation of ideas, institutions, and social change under the development of productive forces—an explicit scientific self-presentation paralleling Kautsky's. Sockol (2023) argues this "vulgar Marxist" image of Plekhanov as narrowly economic-determinist and indifferent to cultural forces is itself a distorting simplification, locating his fin-de-siècle writings on art within a broader activist aesthetic and anti-Kantian polemic.

**Connection**: Baring (2023) bridges directly to Section 2 by showing that Lukács's foundational denunciation of Kautsky as "vulgar Marxism"—for transmitting Marxist conclusions without dialectical method—reproduces Kautsky's own pedagogical structure of disseminating simplified scientific socialism. The label "vulgar Marxism," which Western Marxism will define itself against, thus has a more entangled genealogy than the standard origin story assumes.

## Section 2: The Humanist Turn: Western Marxism and Critical Theory

Where Second International orthodoxy located historical change in the quasi-autonomous development of productive forces, the two currents treated in this section relocate explanatory weight toward human consciousness, culture, and normative reason. Lukács, Gramsci, and Korsch pursue this relocation through a Hegelian-inflected recovery of totality and praxis; the Frankfurt School and Habermas pursue it through critical and, later, communicative theory. Both currents target the same economistic orthodoxy, but they diverge sharply in method and in how much of historical materialism's causal architecture they are willing to abandon.

### 2.1 Hegemony, Consciousness, and Totality (Lukács, Gramsci, Korsch)

Lukács (1971 [1923]) argues that the commodity form, once generalized under capitalism, produces "reification": social relations congeal into apparently autonomous, thing-like laws that penetrate consciousness itself. Against this, he holds that only the proletariat, whose position within production makes visible the historically constructed character of social "facts," can grasp society as a dialectical totality and act as history's "identical subject-object" — a capacity that must be "imputed" to the class as ascribed rather than empirically observed consciousness. This move installs consciousness, rather than economic mechanism, as the site where historical materialism's revolutionary claims are vindicated or blocked. Feenberg (2014) defends the philosophical coherence of this reification/totality framework against readings that treat it as either metaphysically overreaching or merely a way station toward Frankfurt School critical theory.

Gramsci (1971) reconceives structure and superstructure by making civil society — schools, churches, media, common sense — the terrain on which hegemony, understood as the "intellectual and moral leadership" a dominant group exercises alongside coercion, is actually contested. The "integral state" fuses political society and civil society, and "absolute historicism" rejects treating Marxism as a deterministic positive science. Femia (1987) systematizes hegemony as an account of working-class quiescence in advanced capitalist societies operating through taken-for-granted "common sense," while insisting this revision of orthodox causality preserves rather than abandons a materialist framework and a Leninist, vanguardist strategic core. Thomas (2009) argues, drawing on the critical edition of the Prison Notebooks, that Gramsci's philosophy of praxis responds to debates internal to the Third International rather than marking an idealist departure from Marxism. Cospito (2016), tracing the Notebooks diachronically, shows that Gramsci's structure/superstructure formulations were not fixed doctrine but evolved across successive notebooks toward a position treating structure and superstructure as reciprocally conditioning rather than as cause and reflected effect — a finding that complicates efforts, including Femia's and Thomas's, to treat Gramsci's position as settled.

Korsch (1970 [1923]) supplies a reflexive critique of Second International scientism, arguing that declaring philosophy superseded by positive science reproduced the very separation of theory from revolutionary practice that historical materialism was meant to abolish; Marxist theory, on his account, must apply its own historical-dialectical method to itself. Anderson (1976, *Considerations on Western Marxism*) names the tradition running from Lukács, Korsch, and Gramsci through the Frankfurt School to Sartre and Althusser as "Western Marxism," diagnosing a common displacement — after the defeat of the 1917–23 revolutionary wave outside Russia — of theory away from economics and revolutionary strategy toward philosophy and aesthetics. This historiographic category is itself contested: Devlin (2021) argues that Korsch does not fit Anderson's diagnosis, since Korsch presupposed continued revolutionary potential rather than advocating a turn toward superstructural or cultural questions. Anderson's own companion essay (1976, "The Antinomies of Antonio Gramsci") mounts a sympathetic-critical internal reading, identifying an unresolved oscillation in Gramsci's hegemony concept between the ruling class's normal condition in the West and the working class's needed strategic acquisition prior to a seizure of state power.

Together, these positions directly answer the research question of how base/superstructure causation is revised to foreground agency: consciousness (Lukács), hegemonic civil society (Gramsci), and theory-practice unity (Korsch) each substitute for mechanical economic causation, while their proponents maintain — against charges of idealism — that the substitution remains materialist in orientation.

### 2.2 Critical Theory's Selective Inheritance (Frankfurt School, Habermas)

Horkheimer's programmatic distinction between "traditional" and "critical" theory (Horkheimer 1972) supplies the Frankfurt School's founding methodological statement: traditional theory treats social facts as independent of the historically situated subject who perceives them, whereas critical theory insists both object and knowing subject are products of social praxis, oriented toward practical transformation rather than law-like prediction. This retains Marx's demand that theory serve emancipatory practice while rejecting a "traditional," positivist reading of historical materialism as a set of verifiable developmental laws.

Benjamin's "Theses on the Philosophy of History" (Benjamin 2020 [1940]) push this rejection further, replacing the image of history as a homogeneous continuum of progress with one of catastrophe and rupture, in which the "weak messianic power" of past generations of the oppressed can be redeemed in a moment of danger. Tiedemann (1989) frames the resulting interpretive question directly — is Benjamin's contribution a heterodox radicalization of historical materialism or a political messianism that has displaced materialist categories with theological ones — without resolving it, a crux that structures subsequent reception.

Habermas's "reconstruction" project (Habermas 1975) takes the debate in a different direction, replacing Marx's single-track model of history driven by the development of productive forces (labor) with a dual model distinguishing labor from communicative interaction, and reinterpreting social evolution as a sequence of institutionalized cognitive and moral-practical learning processes rather than an economically determined succession of modes of production. Rockmore (1979) maps this project as one stage in a longer itinerary running from interpretation through critique to reconstruction and, he argues, effective partial rejection of historical materialism, questioning whether "reconstruction" names a stable position at all. Reichelt (2000) presses a related but distinct objection from a broadly open-Marxist standpoint: Habermas's turn to communicative action and validity claims, he argues, loses sight of Marx's critique of the historically specific "inverted forms" taken by social relations — commodity and value — substituting a new formal vocabulary rather than genuinely reconstructing the original one. Habermas's separate essay on Benjamin (Habermas 1979) distinguishes Benjamin's "redemptive criticism," oriented toward anamnestic solidarity with past suffering, from consciousness-raising critique oriented toward future emancipatory practice, concluding that messianic redemption of the past cannot be straightforwardly incorporated into a materialist theory of learning and progress.

Adorno's negative dialectics offers a rival strategy for retaining critical purchase without either economistic determinism or Habermas's communicative turn. Vaki (2005) stages this as a direct dispute, arguing that Adorno's refusal of any positive, reconciled totality as a standpoint for critique constitutes a more consistent claim to immanent critique than Habermas's reconstruction, which she argues covertly reintroduces a standpoint external to the social totality it claims to criticize immanently. Osborne (2020) traces Marx's presence across Adorno's career and argues that Adorno became, if anything, more rather than less Marxist over time, appropriating value, fetishism, and ideology into the vocabulary of negative dialectics rather than abandoning them. Marcuse's parallel response to the same absent revolutionary proletariat took a psychoanalytic route: Kellner (1984) situates Marcuse's trajectory — from Reason and Revolution through the integration of Freud in Eros and Civilization to the one-dimensionality thesis — as a sustained attempt to supplement historical materialism with psychoanalytic and phenomenological resources once the predicted revolutionary subject failed to materialize in advanced capitalist societies.

It is worth flagging, without developing the point here, that Habermas's departure from a single-track, labor-primacy model of history — his doubt that class-based production relations alone can anchor social explanation — anticipates themes of anti-essentialism that Section 6's discussion of post-Marxism will radicalize considerably further.

Both currents examined here relocate the explanatory weight of historical materialism away from a deterministic economic base — one toward political and cultural hegemony, one toward normative or communicative reconstruction — while disagreeing internally (Devlin against Anderson on Korsch; Adorno against Habermas on immanent critique) about how much of the "orthodox" causal structure can be surrendered before the result stops being historical materialism at all.

## Section 3: Anti-Humanist Structuralism and the Humanist Backlash

Where the currents surveyed in Section 2 relocated historical-materialist causality in consciousness, hegemony, or communicative reason, Louis Althusser proposed instead to purge Marxist theory of any residual subject-centered explanation. Althusser's *For Marx* diagnoses an "epistemological break" separating the ideological, Hegelian-Feuerbachian humanism of Marx's early writings from the scientific framework of the mature works, and introduces overdetermination to describe how economic, political, and ideological contradictions condense in a "ruptural unity" without any single contradiction ever appearing in "pure" form (Althusser 2005). *Reading Capital*, the product of a 1965 collective seminar with Étienne Balibar and others, elaborates the causal model this requires: "structural causality," the mode of determination proper to a "structure of structures" without center or telos (Althusser and Balibar 2016). Pippa (2016) reconstructs this concept as a deliberate break with both the mechanistic causality Althusser traces to Descartes and the expressive, Hegelian causality that reads every social phenomenon as an expression of a single essence — structural causality instead makes it possible to think history as a process without subject or end. Sotiris (2014) qualifies the standard periodization of this project, arguing that even Althusser's later, less-discussed writings on the "aleatory encounter" already work to loosen structural causality from a rigidly systemic reading, so that "structuralism" in Althusser is not a fixed position but one that shifts across his career. Althusser's essay on ideology reformulates base/superstructure via "relative autonomy": Ideological State Apparatuses (school, family, church) secure the reproduction of the relations of production by "interpellating" individuals as subjects, while remaining relatively autonomous from the economic base that they ultimately serve (Althusser 2002). Rehmann (2013) situates this "point of view of reproduction" as an elaboration of Gramscian civil society analysis, but also documents the criticism most consistently leveled at it: that it amounts to a functionalism viewing institutions from above and disregarding the actual contradictions and struggles within them.

Nicos Poulantzas extended these categories to state theory, arguing that the capitalist state is not an instrument simply wielded by the dominant class but a "factor of cohesion" of the social formation, whose relative autonomy from any particular capital fraction allows it to organize the power bloc's long-term unity, including through concessions to dominated classes (Poulantzas 1973). This structuralist account set the terms for Poulantzas's polemical exchange with Ralph Miliband, who emphasized instrumentalist mechanisms — elite personnel and institutional linkages — over Poulantzas's structural emphasis on the state as a "condenser" of class forces; Sorokin (2025) reconstructs the 1969–1976 *New Left Review* debate through four rounds of mutual charges of empiricism and abstraction, finding it a continuing reference point for theorizing how the state mediates class contradictions. Poulantzas's own later work revised the more rigid structuralism of his first book, redescribing the state as a "material condensation" of a relationship of forces and engaging, under growing Gramscian influence, with a strategy for a "democratic road to socialism" (Poulantzas 1978). Sotiris (2017) traces this trajectory closely, showing that the closer Poulantzas's own categories converged with Gramsci's "integral state," the more critical his explicit statements about Gramsci became — a convergence that is the point of contact with Section 2.1's hegemony theory, noted here only in passing.

Against this entire apparatus, E. P. Thompson's *The Poverty of Theory* indicts Althusserian "Theoretical Practice" as a closed, self-authenticating system that eliminates any independent check by historical evidence and reduces human agents to mere "bearers" of structures; Thompson counterposes "experience" as the category through which historical pressures are lived, contested, and transformed into class consciousness (Thompson 1978). Fuchs (2019) reframes this controversy not as a settled dispute but as an ongoing disagreement over how to relate the economic and non-economic and over the relative weight of structure and agency — Althusser stressing articulation and overdetermination, Thompson stressing lived experience — a framing Fuchs extends into a Marxist theory of communication, treating the structure/agency dialectic as still an open methodological choice for contemporary Marxist theory rather than a question Thompson's polemic definitively closed.

## Section 4: The Analytical Reconstruction: Functionalism, Individualism, and the Primacy Thesis

### 4.1 Cohen's Functionalist Defense

Cohen (1978) reconstructs historical materialism around two theses drawn from the 1859 Preface: the Development Thesis, holding that the productive forces possess an autonomous tendency to develop throughout history, and the Primacy Thesis, holding that the level of development of the productive forces explains the nature of the economic structure, that is, the relations of production. Marx also maintains that the relations of production control the development of the forces, and Cohen argues that the two claims can be rendered consistent only if the Primacy Thesis is read as a functional explanation rather than as a claim about direct causal stimulus: relations of production persist, or are revolutionized, according to whether they promote or fetter the further development of the productive forces, on the model of the Darwinian claim that birds have hollow bones because hollow bones facilitate flight. Cohen elaborates this logical form most fully in his account of functional versus consequence explanation, arguing that a well-confirmed "consequence law" — e occurred because the situation was such that an event like e would cause an event like f — can sustain a functional explanation even where the underlying feedback mechanism has not been specified (Cohen 1982). Against critics who charge that class struggle deserves a larger explanatory role, Cohen replies that class struggle "has primary political significance," but that the political dimension of society is not itself the most basic explanandum of historical materialism, so an account of why relations of production rise and fall cannot be replaced by an account of struggle alone (Cohen 1983). This functionalist reading becomes the reference point against which every subsequent position in this exchange defines itself.

### 4.2 The Individualist and Anti-Reductionist Counter-Positions

Elster (1985) mounts the principal methodological-individualist challenge, holding that macro-level social explanations — including Cohen's functional explanations — are acceptable only when microfoundations at the level of individual belief, desire, and strategic action can at least be sketched; absent such microfoundations, Elster regards functionalist historical materialism as an unexplained "black box," and proposes game-theoretic analysis of class conflict as a replacement explanatory strategy. Wood (1986) intervenes on Cohen's side, arguing that Elster's principled philosophical objection to functional explanation, and his related critique of Marx's use of teleology, both rest on a caricature of the explanatory form actually at issue; Wood does not thereby endorse every functional explanation Marx or Cohen offers, but denies that Elster has identified a defect in functional explanation as such within the social sciences. Wright, Levine, and Sober (1992) stake out a third position, "anti-reductionism," rejecting both Cohen's functionalism and Elster's strict individualism. They distinguish the ontological claim that macro-social entities are constituted by individuals from the explanatory claim that macro-social facts must therefore be explained in individual-level terms, arguing that facts realizable in multiple ways at the micro-level — evolutionary "fittingness" and the "economic survival" of capitalist firms among their examples — resist reduction to any single micro-level account, and thereby deny that the technological-determinism dispute must be settled at the level of "rock-bottom" individual explanation. The Darwin analogy anchoring both Cohen's and Levine and Sober's functionalism remained contested into the following decade: Nolan (2002) argues that the Marx-Darwin analogy does not perform the explanatory work claimed for it in underwriting historical materialism's scientific status, while Sober and Levine (2003) defend the analogy against this objection, indicating that the functional-explanation debate retained analytical vitality well beyond its 1980s origins.

### 4.3 Contesting the Primacy Thesis on Textual and Coherence Grounds

A parallel line of criticism, conducted largely in a 1982–83 *Analyse & Kritik* exchange, challenges Cohen's technological reading of Marx directly rather than through the individualism/functionalism dispute. Fisk (1982) argues that for Marx technology is neither always nor predominantly the direct causal or functional stimulus of the relations of production, that explanatory primacy concerns an underlying framework rather than a direct stimulus, and that this framework is the relations of production rather than technology; he further contends that technological development is not autonomous but depends on the cooperation of producers, reintroducing class struggle into a thesis Cohen aims to render purely functional. Suchting (1982) likewise denies that productive forces determine relations of production in the sense Cohen proposes, offering an alternative reconstruction of Marx's own concepts and identifying class struggle as the primary explanatory factor for the historical periods Marx discusses. Cohen (1983) responds to both critics, together with Van Parijs and Lukes, rebutting the claim that class struggle plays a larger role than his framework assigns it and defending the base/superstructure doctrine against skepticism. Van Parijs (1983) rejoins that Cohen's reply salvages the primacy thesis only by substantially redefining the terms of the "primacy puzzle," after which the claim becomes trivial, and proposes a non-functionalist "Veblenian scenario" as an alternative solution. Independent of this exchange, Ruben (1981) and Miller (1981) — sympathetic analytic readers situating Cohen's book against a mid-century Anglo-American tradition (Acton, Berlin, Popper, Plamenatz) that had dismissed Marx's theories as unworthy of further empirical investigation — nonetheless press their own doubts about whether the primacy thesis bears the explanatory weight Cohen assigns it. Shaw (1986) extends this scrutiny to the Development Thesis underlying the primacy claim, examining its textual grounding in Marx and its role in the broader charge that Cohen's reconstruction amounts to technological determinism.

### 4.4 The Political-Marxist Verdict from Outside the September Group

Wood (1989/1995) supplies a skeptical assessment of the entire analytical-Marxist project from outside the "September Group" from which Cohen, Elster, and Wright, Levine, and Sober all emerged. Addressing the claim, advanced on behalf of rational choice Marxism, that it constitutes "a fully fledged paradigm" capable of doing the explanatory work of historical explanation, the collective dynamics of class struggle, and the evaluation of capitalism, Wood questions whether Elster's individualist program in particular can deliver on such claims, arguing that its microfoundational reduction loses what is historically specific about Marxist categories of class and exploitation. This verdict, issued from a class-relational standpoint external to the functionalism/individualism/anti-reductionism dispute internal to analytical Marxism, forms the direct bridge to Political Marxism, which positions itself explicitly against the rational-choice program Wood here appraises.

Three decades after Cohen's initial reconstruction, no successor position had displaced functionalism by consensus: Elster's individualism, Wright, Levine, and Sober's anti-reductionism, and the textual critiques of Fisk, Suchting, Ruben, and Miller each contest a different aspect of Cohen's reading, without converging on a shared alternative, and the primacy thesis's grounding in Marx's own texts remains independently disputed from within the analytic tradition Cohen inaugurated.

## Section 5: Political Marxism: Class Relations and the Transition to Capitalism

Political Marxism offers a third post-orthodox reconstruction of historical materialism, distinct from both the functionalist and individualist positions surveyed in Section 4. Where analytical Marxism debates whether the development of productive forces or class struggle bears explanatory primacy in the abstract, Political Marxism answers the question historically, through a close reconstruction of a single transition: the emergence of agrarian capitalism in England. Brenner's founding intervention in the "Brenner Debate" argues that comparable demographic and commercial trends across late-medieval England and France produced sharply divergent long-run outcomes — capitalist agriculture in England, continued peasant proprietorship and stagnation in France — because of differences in the class structure of lordship and the contingent outcomes of lord-peasant struggle over property rights, not because of differing exposure to trade or population pressure (Brenner 1976). His 1982 extension traces this argument comparatively across England, France, and Eastern Europe, showing how the specific balance of class power determined whether agriculture was reorganized along capitalist lines (Brenner 1982). Both essays reject a premise Brenner takes liberal and orthodox-Marxist historians to share alike: that commercialization or demographic pressure can, by themselves, explain the rise of capitalism.

Wood generalizes Brenner's historical argument into a systematic theory of capitalism-specific "social property relations": capitalism is historically distinctive in separating "the economic" from "the political," extracting surplus through market-dependent economic compulsion rather than the direct politico-coercive extraction characteristic of feudal or ancient modes of production (Wood 1995). Her later synthesis argues that capitalism accordingly emerged only once, in the specific and contingent conditions of English agrarian class relations, and that treating capitalism as a transhistorical extension of trade or "improvement" obscures this historical specificity — the "commercialization model" shared, on her reading, by both liberal and orthodox-Marxist historiography (Wood 2002). Comninel extends the same class-relational method to the paradigmatic case of "bourgeois revolution," arguing that the French Revolution is better explained as an internal conflict within a single office-holding, rent-extracting ruling class over the political forms of surplus extraction than as a struggle between a rising capitalist bourgeoisie and a feudal aristocracy — a reconstruction offered against both revisionist historians (Furet, Cobban) and orthodox "bourgeois revolution" narratives (Comninel 1987). Teschke identifies a further gap in this domestic-class focus, arguing that Political Marxism's critique of bourgeois-revolution theory needs to be extended to the international and geopolitical dimensions of state formation that both orthodox accounts and Political Marxism's own agrarian-centered analyses have neglected (Teschke 2005); Post continues this line of debate, defending a social-property-relations account of specific revolutionary conjunctures against Neil Davidson's attempted rescue of the bourgeois-revolution concept (Post 2019).

In brief, Brenner's own essay already charges Wallerstein and Frank's world-systems and dependency approaches with a "neo-Smithian Marxism" that locates capitalism's dynamism in trade and exchange rather than in transformed relations of production — a dispute Section 7.1 adjudicates in full.

Political Marxism's coherence has since been contested on two fronts. From within, Knafo and Teschke (2020) argue that the tradition, despite its historicist origins in Brenner's emphasis on class agency and conflict, has increasingly drifted toward a structuralist account of capitalism's self-reproducing "rules of reproduction" — the very determinism it was meant to displace — tracing this ambivalence back to Brenner's own founding texts; Pal's (2021) introduction to the resulting *Historical Materialism* symposium surveys the resulting range of positions, from radical historicist reformulation to defenses of structural "rules." From without, Anievas and Nişancıoğlu (2013) charge Brenner's Anglo-centric account with Eurocentrism, arguing via Trotsky's theory of uneven and combined development that international determinations — Mongol invasion, Ottoman expansion, New World colonization — actively shaped capitalism's north-western European trajectory in ways an intra-European class-relational account omits. Moreno Zacarés (2018) supplies an empirical test of the thesis outside its paradigm case, finding that pre-capitalist property relations persisted in Catalan agriculture and that the region's capitalist breakthrough instead originated in eighteenth-century proto-industrial struggles, contrary to Brenner's own suggestion of a parallel Catalan transition. As Chibber's (2011) later comparative assessment of Brenner's and Cohen's rival reconstructions of historical materialism confirms, Political Marxism remains, alongside analytical Marxism, one of the two principal post-1970s reconstructions against which subsequent historical-materialist theorizing measures itself.

## Section 6: External Critiques: Historicism, Multi-Causality, and Post-Marxism

The currents examined so far all revise historical materialism from a standpoint that remains recognizably Marxist. This section turns to critiques mounted from outside Marxism altogether — Popperian philosophy of science and Weberian sociology — and to post-Marxism, which retains Gramscian vocabulary while explicitly abandoning economic determination, raising the question of what, if anything, survives as "Marxist" once that move is made.

### 6.1 Historicism and Multi-Causality

Popper (1957) coined "historicism" to name the view that history is governed by discoverable laws yielding a determinate end-point, naming Marx's dialectical materialism as a paradigm case and linking it to "holism" and dangerous "utopian social engineering." Popper's target has proven contested even among readers unsympathetic to vulgar readings of Marx. Suchting (1972) distinguishes two logically distinct types of "laws of historical development" that Popper's argument might address, argues Popper's objections fail against both when precisely formulated, and tests four candidate models of Marxian determinism against the charge. Passmore (1975) presses a further meta-critical point: Popper's own usage of "historicism" shifts across his writings — sometimes a thesis about the proper aims of social science, sometimes a practice, sometimes the broader Weltanschauung of German Historismus — an instability that, Passmore argues, undermines the force of the charge before it is even applied to Marx specifically. Hudelson (1980) isolates the inferential steps connecting the unpredictability of future knowledge to the impossibility of long-range social prediction and tests whether they hold against Marx's actual predictive claims about capitalism's trajectory, offering a more measured assessment than outright dismissal or endorsement.

A parallel line of critique, developed from Weberian sociology, targets the primacy-of-the-economic claim directly. Löwy (1989) reads Weber's *Protestant Ethic* as a sustained, largely implicit polemic against monocausal materialist explanation, in which Calvinist doctrine is shown to possess independent causal efficacy in shaping capitalism's "spirit" — a methodological counter-example to treating ideas as epiphenomenal, though one Löwy reads as stopping short of a simple idealist inversion of Marx. Giddens (1981) generalizes this into a systematic critique, arguing that base/superstructure architecture fails for pre-capitalist societies where kinship, military, and normative structures are not reducible to relations of production, and proposing "allocative" and "authoritative" power as independent axes against any general claim that the economic is determinant "in the last instance." Parkin (1979) presses the class-reductionism version of the charge, arguing Marxist class analysis illegitimately collapses the multiple bases of social closure (property, credentials, status, gender, ethnicity) into a single relation to the means of production — a reductionism charge that Laclau and Mouffe will later develop from within a post-Marxist rather than Weberian framework.

A further cluster targets technological determinism and the mode-of-production concept specifically. Bimber (1990), writing from science and technology studies, distinguishes three senses in which a theory might be called technologically determinist and argues that Marx's texts and their analytical reconstruction (including Cohen's) conflate these senses, so that some of the dispute is an artifact of imprecise terminology. Duquette (1992) argues more directly against Cohen's and Shaw's productive-forces-primacy readings, contending that they cannot adequately capture the causal role Marx's own texts assign to class struggle. Doubts about the coherence of "mode of production" as a general category have also emerged from within the structuralist tradition itself: Hindess and Hirst (1977) repudiate their own earlier Althusserian apparatus, concluding that the concept cannot generate determinate laws of motion for a historical epoch and that specific social formations cannot be derived from an abstract typology. The Asiatic-mode-of-production controversy supplies a further test case, with Bailey and Llobera (1979) reassessing Wittfogel's hydraulic-society thesis and arguing that the category as deployed in twentieth-century debate conflates the specificity of non-European state power, ecological determinants of irrigation, and Cold War polemics about "Oriental despotism," obstructing clear anthropological assessment.

### 6.2 Post-Marxism's Dissolution of Base/Superstructure

Laclau and Mouffe (1985) argue that both the base/superstructure model and the classical expectation of proletarian unity rest on an untenable essentialism: the assumption that social agents possess objectively given interests fixed by their position in relations of production. Drawing on post-structuralist discourse theory, they reconceive society as constitutively open, structured by contingent "articulations" of antagonistic elements, and generalize Gramsci's "hegemony" into a general theory of the political no longer anchored in class as a privileged category — retaining Marxist and Gramscian vocabulary (hegemony, antagonism, articulation) while explicitly abandoning economic determination and class reductionism.

This move has drawn a Gramscian rebuttal from within the tradition it claims to generalize. Ives (2005) argues that Laclau and Mouffe's appropriation of "hegemony" strips away Gramsci's own linguistically grounded account of how coercion and consent interrelate, and contends that returning to Gramsci's actual writings on language addresses the same problems without sacrificing attention to concrete institutional and economic conditions — directly recalling the hegemony theory examined in Section 2.1. Jacobs (2018) offers a more descriptive assessment of the same trajectory, characterizing post-structuralist discourse theory as radicalizing Marxism's own move away from economic essentialism at an analytical cost: class struggle and relations of production lose explanatory purchase, and political practice becomes difficult to distinguish from contingent, unanalyzable contestation. The exchange between Norman Geras and Laclau, revisited by Townshend (2017), presses a textual objection from the opposite direction — that *Hegemony and Socialist Strategy* misattributes to Marx and Engels a crude economic reductionism they did not hold, while covertly reintroducing the essentialist and universalist commitments it officially disavows; Townshend's retrospective judges that Geras was "more of a post-Marxist than he thought he was, and became more so over time." Goldstein (2019) traces how far the joint 1985 position later diverged, as Laclau developed a rhetorical and psychoanalytic theory of populism and Mouffe an "agonistic" pluralism, and asks how much of the original anti-essentialist, anti-class-reductionist argument survives in each trajectory.

Taken together, the historicist, multi-causal, and post-Marxist critiques converge in doubting that a single privileged causal factor — the economic, or class — can do the explanatory work classical historical materialism assigns it, even as they diverge sharply on whether anything recognizably materialist survives the resulting revisions.

## Section 7: Extending the Theory: Feminism, World-Systems, and Ecology

### 7.1 Feminist Historical Materialism

Marxist-feminist scholarship divides over whether women's oppression requires a second explanatory system alongside historical materialism's categories of production or can be explained from within a single, unitary framework. Hartmann (1979) argues that Marxist categories are "sex-blind": they explain capital's drive to accumulate but not why women specifically perform unpaid domestic labor and occupy subordinate wage positions. Her "dual-systems theory" treats patriarchy as a distinct social structure, predating and interacting with capitalism, grounded materially in men's historical control over women's labor power. Vogel (2013 [1983]) rejects this bifurcation, locating women's subordination within a single capitalist totality: the generational and daily reproduction of labor power is unpaid work that capital does not remunerate but structurally requires. Bhattacharya (2017) extends this unitary position into contemporary social reproduction theory, treating the production of things and the production of the people who produce things as two vantage points on one process, thereby dissolving rather than merely qualifying the base/superstructure distinction between "real" production and derivative domestic labor.

The adequacy of Engels's *Origin of the Family* as a materialist account of gender oppression is adjudicated differently from within this same unitary camp. Vogel (1996) credits Engels with linking private property's rise to women's subordination but faults him for treating the household as a holdover from earlier modes of production rather than as integral to capitalism itself; Blackledge (2018) instead defends a sympathetic reconstruction of Engels as still furnishing the resources for a unitary theory, against critics who treat *Origin* as either reductionist or superseded by later kinship anthropology. Portella and Busk (2024) attempt to bridge the dual-systems/unitary split directly by applying Marx's categories of formal and real subsumption, explaining both capitalism's appropriation of pre-existing patriarchal forms and its production of new, specifically capitalist forms of gendered oppression without granting patriarchy transhistorical autonomy. Ferguson (2016) identifies a further tension internal to unitary theory: whether its totalizing ontology of a differentiated capitalist whole can accommodate intersectionality's insistence on the irreducibility of race, gender, and class as distinct axes, or competes with it. Falquet (2024) recovers a comparatively neglected variant, francophone materialist feminism's "domestic mode of production" thesis (Delphy), independently anticipating the sex/race/class interlocking that later Anglophone debates approached separately, while Gabrielli (2024) traces the Wages for Housework current (Dalla Costa, Fortunati) that historically preceded, and against which, both Hartmann's and Vogel's positions were formulated.

### 7.2 World-Systems and Dependency Theory

World-systems analysis (Wallerstein 2004) and dependency theory (Amin 1976) relocate historical materialism's unit of analysis from the single social formation to the capitalist world-economy as a whole, structured into core, periphery, and semi-periphery zones linked by unequal exchange. As Section 5 notes, Brenner's charge of "neo-Smithian Marxism" holds that this move substitutes a generic, Smithian logic of trade and profit-maximization for Marx's analysis of historically specific class relations of production. Denemark and Thomas (1988) reconstruct this dispute as one in which Brenner and Wallerstein largely talk past each other: Brenner addresses capitalism's causal origins in specific agrarian class relations, while Wallerstein addresses the systemic reproduction of an already-constituted world-economy — distinct explanatory projects rather than straightforwardly competing empirical claims. Song (2015) reframes the disagreement similarly, arguing that the "trade versus production" dichotomy obscures what is actually at stake: a dispute over the proper scale and boundary of the unit of analysis (a bounded social formation versus a globally totalized world-economy) rather than a genuine choice between exchange- and production-centered explanation. Kvangraven (2020) mounts a more direct rebuttal, reconstructing dependency theory as a research programme defined by four tenets — a global historical approach, theorization of capitalism's polarizing tendencies, an explicit focus on structures of production, and attention to peripheral economies' specific constraints — and arguing that Brenner-style critiques caricature a tradition that already theorizes production relations, only at a world scale.

A second, largely independent axis concerns the tradition's own relation to Eurocentrism. Chian (2025) traces Wallerstein's Marxist turn to his decade of engagement with African decolonization and thinkers including Fanon, Nkrumah, and Cabral, situating world-systems analysis's genesis in anti-colonial struggle rather than metropolitan theory subsequently applied to the periphery. De Oliveira and Kvangraven (2023) recover the 1972 Dakar conference, organized by Amin, as a suppressed founding moment of a Global South-centered political economy, arguing dependency theory's marginalization from mainstream disciplinary histories was itself a form of epistemic Eurocentrism. Pradella (2017) supplies a textual complement from within Marx's own corpus, arguing his later writings and notebooks already incorporate colonialism and imperialism into a global, anti-Eurocentric analysis of accumulation.

### 7.3 Ecological Extensions

Foster (1999) reconstructs Marx's late engagement with Justus von Liebig's soil chemistry to argue that Marx diagnosed a "metabolic rift": capitalism's town/country division of labor severs the material exchange between humanity and the soil, rupturing natural nutrient cycling. Burkett (1999) grounds the same claim in value theory, arguing nature's role as a "free gift" appropriated without cost is constitutive of, rather than incidental to, Marx's critique of political economy. Saito (2017; 2023) extends this reading philologically, using Marx's unpublished natural-science notebooks (published in the ongoing MEGA2) to argue that Marx's ecological thought deepened after *Capital*, culminating in a late turn toward "degrowth communism" — a vision of post-capitalist society organized around sustainable use-value production rather than continued expansion of exchange-value. This reading bears directly on Cohen's primacy thesis discussed in Section 4: if Saito is correct, Marx himself came to doubt that developing the productive forces is either historically inevitable or emancipatory, requiring reconstruction rather than mere defense of the classical theory's technological core.

The metabolic-rift program has itself become internally contested. Moore (2017) charges rift-thinking with reproducing a Cartesian nature/society dualism that treats nature as an external object acted upon by an internal society, proposing instead a "world-ecology" in which capital and nature are co-produced. Malm (2019) defends metabolic rift's dualism as a considered "property dualism" — nature and society are equally material but really distinct relations — and argues Moore's hybridism, traced to Latour, dissolves the distinction needed to identify capitalism's specific responsibility for ecological harm; Foster and Burkett (2016) mount a parallel self-defense against a decade of such criticism. Gellert (2018) compares metabolic rift, world-ecology, and Bunker's ecologically unequal exchange, asking whether the three name substantively different mechanisms or largely redescribe the same diagnosis in rival vocabularies — a comparison that connects this dispute back to Subsection 7.2's unequal-exchange tradition.

**Section Summary**: Each extension revises a specific historical-materialist category — production (feminism), unit of analysis (world-systems), and human/nature metabolism (ecology) — rather than simply applying the theory unchanged, and each generates its own internal defender/critic structure paralleling the debates of Sections 4–6.

## Section 8: Contemporary Defenses and the Postcolonial Challenge

Writing after the collapse of the political conditions—an ascendant labor movement, an existing socialist state—that once lent historical materialism external plausibility, Chibber (2011) offers the review's most direct post-Cohen verdict on the primacy thesis. Examining two influential attempts to repair Cohen's classical, technologically deterministic reconstruction—by Erik Olin Wright and colleagues, and by Alan Carling—Chibber (2011) argues that both rescue attempts are largely unsuccessful, and that insofar as they do render the theory more plausible, they do so only by converting it, "willy-nilly," into a class-struggle theory. He takes this to spell the demise of the classical, technologically deterministic version of historical materialism, while insisting the result is not a lapse into voluntarism: a class-struggle-centered materialism survives the collapse of the productive-forces-primacy core. Read alongside Section 4's internal analytical-Marxist debate between functionalists, individualists, and anti-reductionists, and Section 5's Political Marxist reconstruction of historical change around class-relational property forms, Chibber's assessment converges with both currents in relocating explanatory weight from technological development to class struggle, even though the three literatures arrive at this convergence largely independently of one another.

Chibber's second major intervention shifts from adjudicating internal analytical-Marxist disputes to defending historical materialism's geographic scope. Against Subaltern Studies and postcolonial theory's claim that capital's "universalizing tendencies" are a Eurocentric fiction imposed on non-Western social formations, Chibber (2013) argues that historical materialism's core categories—class formation, capitalist competition, structural compulsion—generalize across colonial and postcolonial contexts, and that postcolonial theorists' insistence on the radical difference of the non-Western world rests on mischaracterizations of both Marx's theory and the actual history of bourgeois revolution in the West. This argument, and the sustained controversy it provoked, is documented in Lazarus's (2016) sympathetic-critical response. Lazarus credits Chibber's demonstration that capital's universalizing tendency need not entail cultural homogenization or the progressive spread of "Western" norms as a genuine materialist contribution, but argues that Chibber conflates the historiographic project of Subaltern Studies with "postcolonial theory" more broadly, misrepresenting both traditions and the institutional relationship between them. Lazarus further contends that Chibber's diagnosis of postcolonial theory as "Eurocentric" mischaracterizes what is better understood as a conjuncturally distinct version of "Third Worldism"—a dispute that, on Lazarus's own account, remains unresolved rather than settled by either side, and that connects back to Section 7.2's treatment of Eurocentrism charges against world-systems and dependency theory.

Closing this arc, the introduction to the *Oxford Handbook of Karl Marx* (Prew, Rotta, Smith, and Vidal 2018) offers a synthetic retrospective across sociology, economics, political science, philosophy, geography, and ecology, arguing that Marx's foundational categories—historical materialism among them—continue to be "defended, elaborated and applied" in contemporary social science rather than superseded by repeated declarations of Marxism's obsolescence.

## Conclusion

Traced across eight sections, the post-Marx history of historical materialism displays a recurring pattern rather than a linear progression: each successive current defines itself by relocating the site of historical-materialist explanation away from the compressed, quasi-lawlike causality of the 1859 Preface, without any successor framework achieving uncontested consensus. Lukács, Gramsci, and Korsch relocated explanatory weight to consciousness and hegemony; Althusser relocated it to structural causality and "relative autonomy"; Cohen's functionalism and its individualist and anti-reductionist critics relocated it to the logical form of the explanation itself; Political Marxism relocated it to class-relational property forms; Laclau and Mouffe relocated it to discourse; feminist, world-systems, and ecological extensions relocated it to social reproduction, the world-economy, and human/nature metabolism respectively. What unites these otherwise divergent moves is a shared rejection of a single privileged, economically determinant cause — and a shared failure, so far, to settle on what should replace it. Tellingly, several of the most methodologically developed successor frameworks have themselves attracted internal critique on grounds structurally similar to those leveled at the original orthodoxy: Knafo and Teschke (2020) argue that Political Marxism, despite its historicist origins in Brenner's account of class agency, has drifted toward a structuralist theory of capitalism's self-reproducing "rules of reproduction" — the very determinism it was meant to displace — and Moore (2017) charges metabolic-rift theory with reproducing the Cartesian nature/society dualism it set out to overcome. The pattern of relocation-followed-by-renewed-contestation, in other words, does not stop with the classical orthodoxy; it recurs within the post-Marx reconstructions themselves.

This arc leaves several cross-cutting questions unresolved rather than settled. First, whether "economic determinism" ever accurately described Marx's own texts or only his codifiers remains open, and every later current's self-positioning against a supposed orthodoxy (Section 1) implicitly restages this question without resolving it. Second, whether functional, class-relational, or discourse-theoretic categories can perform the explanatory work classical historical materialism claimed for the primacy of the productive forces remains genuinely contested three decades after the analytical Marxism debates began (Sections 4–6), with no single successor position having displaced Cohen's original formulation. Third, whether the theory's core categories generalize beyond their originally European frame of reference is disputed on multiple fronts — Brenner's Anglo-centric transition thesis, the Eurocentrism charge against world-systems theory, and Chibber's (2011) defense of capital's "universalizing tendencies" against postcolonial theory all bear on this question without converging on a shared verdict (Sections 5, 7.2, 8).

As the most recent scholarship surveyed here confirms — including the 2020–2021 Political Marxism symposium, continuing metabolic-rift/world-ecology exchanges, and the retrospective assessment offered by Prew, Rotta, Smith, and Vidal (2018) that historical materialism's categories continue to be "defended, elaborated and applied" — live disagreement persists on all three axes rather than converging toward a settled post-Marx consensus. The theory remains an active research program precisely because none of its post-Marx reconstructions has proven immune to the kind of internal challenge it originally leveled against orthodoxy.

Two limitations bound this account. The review does not address non-Anglophone reception (Chinese and Latin American Marxism in particular), leaving open whether the debates traced here recur, or take different form, outside the Anglophone and European literatures surveyed. It also excludes New Materialism and Actor-Network approaches, whose own relocation of agency toward non-human and hybrid actants raises questions about the boundary between historical materialism and its neighboring traditions that lie beyond the present scope. Both mark plausible directions in which the debate may yet extend.
