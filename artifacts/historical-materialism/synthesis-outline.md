# Literature Review Outline

**Research Project**: Historical Materialism in Marx and, Especially, After Marx
**Date**: 2026-09-12
**Total Literature Base**: ~180 papers (post-dedup) across 11 domains; this outline draws on ~110 of the highest-importance entries, weighted heavily toward post-Marx reception per the project's explicit brief
**Target Review Length**: ~6000-6500 words (above the standard 3000-4000 default, justified by the 11-domain scope and the comprehensiveness priority)

---

## Overall Architecture

The review does **not** proceed domain-by-domain. It follows the theory's own dialectical career: Marx's own compressed and internally tensioned formulation (brief); its hardening into orthodox "scientific" doctrine (Domain 2); the humanist revolt against that doctrine (Domains 3-4); the anti-humanist structuralist counter-revolt (Domain 5); the two most methodologically rigorous late-twentieth-century reconstructions — functionalist-analytical (Domain 6, special emphasis) and class-relational Political Marxism (Domain 7); the external critiques that test the theory from outside Marxism altogether, including post-Marxism (Domain 8); and finally the extensions into terrain Marx addressed unevenly — gender (Domain 9), the world-system (Domain 10), and nature (Domain 11) — closing with contemporary defenses (also Domain 11). Roughly 85% of the review's substantive space goes to post-Marx material, consistent with the "especially after Marx" brief.

## Introduction (~500 words)

**Purpose**: State Marx's core theses concisely and identify the specific internal tension every later current reacts to, then preview the post-Marx arc.

**Content**:
- State the canonical formulation as compressed in the 1859 Preface (forces/relations of production, base/superstructure, revolutionary epochs) and note its status as retrospectively "canonical" despite being written in the same period as the far less deterministic *Grundrisse*.
- Flag the specific textual tension the whole review will keep returning to: a productive-forces-driven account (*German Ideology*, echoed in the Preface) versus a class-struggle-driven account (*Manifesto*, *Grundrisse*) that Marx never explicitly reconciled (Satz 1989).
- Note the philological complications: the "German Ideology" as read for a century is a twentieth-century editorial construction rather than a text Marx and Engels intended to publish (Carver and Blank 2014; Comninel 2018), and the *Grundrisse* was unavailable to most twentieth-century readers until 1973 (Musto 2008), meaning the Preface's dominance is partly an accident of publication history.
- Note the young/mature Marx periodization debate (Musto 2015; Cowling 2023) as a recurring interpretive fault line.
- State scope and boundaries: the review excludes New Materialism/Actor-Network approaches and does not claim comprehensive coverage of non-Anglophone (Chinese, Latin American) reception; flag this as a limitation.
- Preview: the bulk of the review traces how each post-Marx current defines itself against a prior current's reading of the base/superstructure relation.

**Key Papers**: Wolff and Leopold 2025 (SEP "Karl Marx"); Satz 1989; Carver and Blank 2014; Musto 2008; Musto 2015; Cowling 2023; Fracchia 2004.

## Section 1: Codifying the Doctrine — Engels, Second International Orthodoxy, and the Genealogy of "Economic Determinism" (~550 words)

**Section Purpose**: Establish the "orthodox" determinist target every later current (Western Marxism, structuralism, analytical Marxism, Political Marxism) defines itself against, while showing this target is itself contested.

**Main Claims**:
1. "Economic determinism" as a doctrinal label is arguably a Second International construction (Engels, Kautsky, Plekhanov) rather than Marx's own position, though the boundary is contested even for its primary target, Engels.
2. Recent scholarship increasingly rehabilitates Engels, Kautsky, and Plekhanov against the "vulgar Marxist" caricature, complicating any clean two-stage story (Marx: subtle; epigones: crude).

**Subsection 1.1: Engels and *Anti-Dühring* as the Only Systematic Statement**
**Papers**: Engels 1962 [1878]; Blackledge 2017; Blackledge 2025; Byron 2022; Hollander 2011.
**Content**: *Anti-Dühring* carried Marx's own "seal of approval," complicating any effort (Byron 2022) to separate a divergent "Engelsian Marxism" from Marx's own critical method; Blackledge's rehabilitative readings (2017, 2025) versus Byron's strong divergence thesis frame the debate.

**Subsection 1.2: Kautsky, Plekhanov, and the Bernstein Revisionism Controversy**
**Papers**: Blackledge 2006; Shandro 2014; Lewis 2017; Gronow 2016; Rogers 2015; Steger 1992; Plekhanov 1947 [1895]; Sockol 2023; Mayer 1993.
**Content**: Kautsky's historiographical practice (pre-1910) versus his later political decline; the Bernstein-Kautsky *Revisionismusstreit* as a dispute over whether socialism requires deterministic "science" or independent ethical foundations; Plekhanov's "monist conception of history" and revisionist attempts (Sockol 2023) to rescue him from the "vulgar Marxist" label.

**Connection**: Baring 2023 ("Who Are You Calling Vulgar?") bridges directly to Section 2 by showing Lukács's foundational polemic against Kautskyan "vulgar Marxism" reproduces Kautsky's own pedagogical structure — flag this bridge explicitly rather than re-narrating in Section 2.

**Section Summary**: The "orthodoxy" against which later currents rebel is less monolithic than usually assumed; this instability matters because Western Marxism, structuralism, and Political Marxism all rhetorically position themselves against a version of "orthodoxy" that recent scholarship suggests may be a caricature.
**Word Target**: 550 words

## Section 2: The Humanist Turn — Western Marxism and Critical Theory (~950 words total)

**Section Purpose**: Present the two major, distinct humanist/agency-centered revisions of base/superstructure causality — one hegemony/consciousness-centered (Lukács, Gramsci, Korsch), one critical-theoretic/normative (Frankfurt School, Habermas) — while noting they share a common target (economistic orthodoxy) but diverge in method.

**Subsection 2.1: Hegemony, Consciousness, and Totality (Lukács, Gramsci, Korsch)** — ~500 words
**Papers**: Lukács 1971 [1923]; Gramsci 1971; Korsch 1970 [1923]; Anderson 1976 (*Considerations on Western Marxism*); Anderson 1976 ("Antinomies of Antonio Gramsci"); Thomas 2009; Cospito 2016; Femia 1987; Devlin 2021; Feenberg 2014.
**Content**: Lukács's reification/totality and "imputed" class consciousness as the founding revision locating revolutionary agency in consciousness rather than economic mechanism; Gramsci's hegemony, "integral state," and "absolute historicism" as a reconception of structure/superstructure in which civil society becomes the terrain of struggle; Korsch's theory-practice unity as a reflexive critique of Second International scientism. Present Anderson's "Western Marxism" as a historiographic category (a turn from economics/politics to philosophy after 1917-23) alongside Devlin's (2021) revisionist challenge that Korsch does not fit this diagnosis — the category itself is contested. Cospito's diachronic reading shows Gramsci's structure/superstructure formulations evolved rather than settled.
**Connection**: Directly answers RQ2 (how the theory was revised) by showing consciousness/hegemony substituting for mechanical causation without (proponents argue) lapsing into idealism.

**Subsection 2.2: Critical Theory's Selective Inheritance (Frankfurt School, Habermas)** — ~450 words
**Papers**: Horkheimer 1972 ("Traditional and Critical Theory"); Benjamin 2020 [1940]; Tiedemann 1989; Habermas 1975 (*Reconstruction*); Habermas 1979 (on Benjamin); Rockmore 1979; Reichelt 2000; Vaki 2005; Osborne 2020; Kellner 1984.
**Content**: Horkheimer's traditional/critical theory distinction as rejecting "law-like" readings of historical materialism; Benjamin's messianic, discontinuous historiography as a challenge to progress-narratives (Tiedemann's "materialism or political messianism?" framing structures the reception); Habermas's explicit "reconstruction" replacing the labor-paradigm with a dual labor/interaction model and social evolution as institutionalized learning — with Rockmore's and Reichelt's skepticism that this amounts to abandonment rather than reconstruction; Adorno's negative dialectics (via Vaki's and Osborne's readings) as a rival, more consistently immanent critique. Marcuse's psychoanalytic supplementation (Kellner 1984) as a parallel response to the absent revolutionary proletariat.
**Connection**: Habermas's move away from a single-track, labor-primacy model of history anticipates themes (anti-essentialism, doubts about class as the sole explanatory anchor) that Section 6's post-Marxism will radicalize — flag this forward link for the writer of Section 6 rather than treating it twice.

**Section Summary**: Both currents relocate the explanatory weight of historical materialism away from a deterministic economic base — one toward political/cultural hegemony, one toward normative/communicative reconstruction — while disagreeing among themselves (e.g., Devlin vs. Anderson on Korsch; Habermas vs. Adorno on immanent critique) about how much of "orthodox" causal structure can be abandoned before the theory stops being historical materialism at all.
**Word Target**: 950 words

## Section 3: Anti-Humanist Structuralism and the Humanist Backlash (~600 words)

**Section Purpose**: Present the sharpest internal opposition in the review — Althusserian anti-humanism versus the humanist, agency/experience-centered tradition of Section 2 — as a direct methodological confrontation.

**Main Claims**:
1. Althusser's "epistemological break," overdetermination, and "structural causality" reconceive base/superstructure via "relative autonomy," explicitly rejecting both mechanistic and Hegelian-expressive causality.
2. Thompson's *The Poverty of Theory* mounts the century's most influential humanist rebuttal, and the controversy remains a live methodological choice (Fuchs 2019) rather than a settled dispute.

**Papers**: Althusser 2005 [*For Marx*]; Althusser and Balibar 2016 [*Reading Capital*]; Althusser 2002 [ISA essay]; Poulantzas 1973; Poulantzas 1978; Thompson 1978; Pippa 2016; Sotiris 2014; Rehmann 2013; Sorokin 2025 (Miliband-Poulantzas); Sotiris 2017 (Althusser/Poulantzas and Gramsci); Fuchs 2019.

**Content**: Structural causality as a "structure of structures without center," contrasted with mechanistic and expressive causality (Pippa 2016); the ISA essay's functionalist cast and the reproduction-without-struggle critique (Rehmann 2013); Poulantzas's state theory (relative autonomy of the capitalist state as "factor of cohesion") and its evolution toward Gramscian categories in his later work, complicating a clean structuralism/humanism binary (Sotiris 2017); the Miliband-Poulantzas instrumentalism/structuralism debate (Sorokin 2025); Thompson's "experience" as the counter-category to overdetermination, and Fuchs's (2019) reframing of the controversy as still active rather than resolved.

**Connection**: Note explicitly for the writer that Poulantzas's late convergence with Gramscian "integral state" language (Sotiris 2017) is the point of contact with Section 2.1 — a brief cross-reference suffices; do not re-narrate Gramsci's hegemony theory here.

**Section Summary**: Structuralism and Western Marxism represent genuinely opposed answers to the agency/structure problem within the same broad post-orthodox reaction, and Poulantzas's own trajectory shows the boundary between them is more permeable than Anderson's or Thompson's polemics suggest.
**Word Target**: 600 words

## Section 4: The Analytical Reconstruction — Functionalism, Individualism, and the Primacy Thesis (~850 words) [special emphasis]

**Section Purpose**: Present the review's most rigorous, best-documented defense-versus-critique exchange, organized dialectically rather than by author, per the domain's own synthesis guidance.

**Main Claims**:
1. Cohen's functional reconstruction of the Development and Primacy Theses is the reference point every subsequent position in this exchange responds to.
2. No successor position (Elster's individualism, Wright/Levine/Sober's anti-reductionism) has achieved consensus displacement of Cohen's functionalism, and the primacy thesis's textual grounding in Marx remains independently disputed.

**Subsection 4.1: Cohen's Functionalist Defense**
**Papers**: Cohen 1978; Cohen 1982 ("Functional explanation, consequence explanation, and Marxism"); Cohen 1983 ("Reply to Four Critics").
**Content**: The Development Thesis and Primacy Thesis, and why Cohen reads primacy as functional explanation (the "hollow bones" analogy) rather than direct causal stimulus, to reconcile it with the thesis that relations of production control the forces.

**Subsection 4.2: The Individualist and Anti-Reductionist Counter-Positions**
**Papers**: Elster 1985; Wood 1986 (defends functional explanation against Elster); Wright, Levine, and Sober 1992; Nolan 2002; Sober and Levine 2003.
**Content**: Elster's demand for individual-level microfoundations and his game-theoretic alternative; Wood's rebuttal that Elster's objection caricatures functional explanation; Wright/Levine/Sober's "anti-reductionist" third way, denying the dispute must be settled at the individual level; the Darwin analogy's persistence as a live point of contention into the 2000s (Nolan vs. Sober and Levine).

**Subsection 4.3: Contesting the Primacy Thesis on Textual and Coherence Grounds**
**Papers**: Fisk 1982; Suchting 1982; Van Parijs 1983; Ruben 1981; Miller 1981; Shaw 1986.
**Content**: The 1982-83 *Analyse & Kritik* exchange reasserting class struggle's centrality against a technological reading of primacy; independent doubts (Ruben, Miller) about whether the primacy thesis survives scrutiny even from sympathetic analytic readers; Shaw's examination of the Development Thesis's textual grounding.

**Subsection 4.4: The Political-Marxist Verdict from Outside the September Group**
**Papers**: Wood 1989/1995 ("Rational Choice Marxism: Is the Game Worth the Candle?").
**Content**: Wood's skeptical assessment that rational-choice Marxism's individualist microfoundations lose what is historically specific about Marxist categories — the direct bridge to Section 5's Political Marxism, which explicitly positions itself against this entire analytical program.

**Section Summary**: Whether the primacy thesis survives analytical scrutiny remains genuinely contested thirty-plus years on; no single successor framework has won, and the debate's own internal factions (functionalist, individualist, anti-reductionist) do not map neatly onto "defenders" versus "critics" of historical materialism as such.
**Word Target**: 850 words

## Section 5: Political Marxism — Class Relations and the Transition to Capitalism (~700 words)

**Section Purpose**: Present the class-relational alternative to both orthodox determinism and analytical Marxism's technological primacy thesis, and its own internal and external contestation.

**Main Claims**:
1. Brenner's "Brenner Debate" explains the divergent trajectories of English and French agrarian development through class struggle over property relations, not commercialization or demography — rejecting the premise shared by liberal and orthodox-Marxist historians alike.
2. Political Marxism's own coherence has been challenged from within (Knafo and Teschke 2020) for drifting toward the structural determinism it was meant to displace, and from without for Eurocentrism (Anievas and Nişancıoğlu 2013) — the same charge developed further in Section 7's world-systems material.

**Papers**: Brenner 1976; Brenner 1982; Wood 1995; Wood 2002; Comninel 1987; Knafo and Teschke 2020; Chibber 2011 (staging Political Marxism against analytical Marxism — cite once here; cross-referenced from Section 4 and Section 8); Teschke 2005; Post 2019; Anievas and Nişancıoğlu 2013; Moreno Zacarés 2018; Pal 2021.

**Content**: Brenner's founding thesis and Wood's generalization into a theory of capitalism-specific "social property relations" (the separation of "the economic" from "the political"); Comninel's extension of the class-relational method to the French Revolution, against both revisionist historians (Furet, Cobban) and orthodox "bourgeois revolution" narratives; the post-2019 internal reopening of the debate (Knafo and Teschke's "historicist vs. rules of reproduction" critique, and the Pal-edited symposium response); the Eurocentrism charge (Anievas and Nişancıoğlu) via "uneven and combined development"; Moreno Zacarés's empirical test case (Catalonia) finding the Brenner thesis wanting outside England.

**Cross-reference note for writer**: State the "neo-Smithian Marxism" charge against Wallerstein/Frank here in brief (it originates in Brenner's own 1977/1982 essay) but reserve the fuller two-sided adjudication of that specific dispute (Kvangraven, Song, Denemark and Thomas) for Section 7.1, to avoid duplicating the same debate twice.

**Section Summary**: Political Marxism supplies the review's clearest example of a corrective to determinism that has itself been charged with relapsing into a new structuralism, and its transition-to-capitalism thesis remains contested on both methodological and geographic-scope grounds.
**Word Target**: 700 words

## Section 6: External Critiques — Historicism, Multi-Causality, and Post-Marxism (~600 words)

**Section Purpose**: Supply the necessary adversarial counterweight from outside Marxism (and, with post-Marxism, at Marxism's dissolving edge), directly serving the project's priority against over-representing defenses.

**Main Claims**:
1. Popper's historicism charge and the Weberian multi-causal critique target the primacy-of-the-economic claim from external philosophical and sociological standpoints, with contested success against Marx's actual texts.
2. Laclau and Mouffe's post-Marxism dissolves base/superstructure and class essentialism from a position that retains some Marxist/Gramscian vocabulary while explicitly abandoning economic determination — raising the question of what, if anything, remains "Marxist."

**Subsection 6.1: Historicism and Multi-Causality**
**Papers**: Popper 1957; Suchting 1972; Hudelson 1980; Passmore 1975; Löwy 1989; Giddens 1981; Parkin 1979; Bimber 1990; Duquette 1992; Hindess and Hirst 1977; Bailey and Llobera 1979 (Asiatic mode of production controversy).
**Content**: Popper's charge that historicism converts observed trends into inexorable laws, and the sustained rebuttal literature (Suchting, Hudelson, Passmore) disputing whether Popper's target is even coherently specified; Weber's/Giddens's/Parkin's multi-causal and class-reductionism critiques; technological-determinism critiques from science-and-technology-studies (Bimber) and internal Marxist debate (Duquette); the mode-of-production concept's coherence questioned even by former structuralists themselves (Hindess and Hirst's auto-critique) and by the Asiatic-mode-of-production controversy.

**Subsection 6.2: Post-Marxism's Dissolution of Base/Superstructure**
**Papers**: Laclau and Mouffe 1985; Ives 2005; Jacobs 2018; Townshend 2017; Goldstein 2019.
**Content**: The anti-essentialist argument that classical Marxism's expectation of proletarian unity rests on an untenable assumption of objectively given class interests; the generalization of Gramscian hegemony into a discourse-theoretic category no longer anchored in class; Ives's Gramscian rebuttal that this strips away Gramsci's own materially grounded understanding of coercion/consent (linking back to Section 2.1); the Geras-Laclau exchange on whether Laclau and Mouffe misread classical texts; the later bifurcation of Laclau's and Mouffe's own projects.

**Section Summary**: External and post-Marxist critiques converge on doubting the theory's claim to identify a single privileged causal factor (the economic, or class), but disagree sharply on whether anything recognizably "materialist" or "Marxist" survives the resulting revisions.
**Word Target**: 600 words

## Section 7: Extending the Theory — Feminism, World-Systems, and Ecology (~950 words total)

**Section Purpose**: Show historical materialism's application to domains addressed unevenly or not at all by Marx, and the internal debates each extension generates.

**Subsection 7.1: Feminist Historical Materialism** — ~350 words
**Papers**: Hartmann 1979; Vogel 2013 [1983]; Vogel 1996; Bhattacharya 2017; Blackledge 2018; Portella and Busk 2024; Ferguson 2016; Falquet 2024; Federici 2021; Gabrielli 2024.
**Content**: The dual-systems (Hartmann: patriarchy as a distinct system with its own material base) versus unitary/social-reproduction-theory (Vogel, Bhattacharya: reproduction of labor power as internal to a single capitalist totality) debate; the adequacy of Engels's *Origin of the Family* as adjudicated from both camps (Vogel's critical retrieval versus Blackledge's sympathetic reconstruction); recent attempts (Portella and Busk) to resolve the dual/unitary split via formal/real subsumption; the tension between unitary theory's totalizing ontology and intersectionality (Ferguson); the recovery of francophone materialist feminism (Falquet) as an independent variant.

**Subsection 7.2: World-Systems and Dependency Theory** — ~350 words
**Papers**: Wallerstein 2004; Brenner 1982 ("neo-Smithian Marxism"); Denemark and Thomas 1988; Amin 1976; Weeks and Dore 1979; Kvangraven 2020; Song 2015; Chian 2025; de Oliveira and Kvangraven 2023; Pradella 2017.
**Content**: World-systems analysis's shift of unit of analysis to the capitalist world-economy (core-periphery-semi-periphery) and dependency theory's "development of underdevelopment"/unequal exchange; Brenner's neo-Smithian Marxism charge (introduced in Section 5) now adjudicated fully — Denemark and Thomas's and Song's reframing of the dispute as a disagreement over scale/unit of analysis rather than trade-versus-production per se, and Kvangraven's contemporary defense of dependency theory as a production-focused research programme against the caricature; the decolonial recovery of the tradition's anti-Eurocentric origins (Chian on Wallerstein's African fieldwork; de Oliveira and Kvangraven on the 1972 Dakar conference) and Pradella's textual case that Marx's own later writings already incorporate a non-Eurocentric analysis of colonialism.

**Subsection 7.3: Ecological Extensions** — ~350 words
**Papers**: Foster 1999; Burkett 1999; Saito 2017; Saito 2023; Moore 2017; Malm 2019; Gellert 2018; Foster and Burkett 2016.
**Content**: Foster's "metabolic rift" thesis (Marx's engagement with Liebig on capitalist agriculture's rupture of soil nutrient cycling) and Burkett's value-theoretic grounding (nature as "free gift"); Saito's MEGA2-based extension arguing Marx's ecological thought deepened after *Capital*, culminating in a late turn toward "degrowth communism" that directly revises Cohen's primacy thesis (Section 4) by suggesting Marx himself doubted that developing the productive forces is either inevitable or desirable; the internal metabolic-rift/world-ecology dispute — Moore's charge that rift-thinking reproduces a Cartesian nature/society dualism, Malm's defense of a "property dualism," and Gellert's adjudicating comparison of the two frameworks with Bunker's ecologically unequal exchange (a further point of contact with Section 7.2).

**Section Summary**: Each extension revises a specific historical-materialist category (production, unit of analysis, human/nature metabolism) rather than simply applying the theory unchanged, and each generates its own internal defender/critic structure mirroring the debates of Sections 4-6.
**Word Target**: 950 words

## Section 8: Contemporary Defenses and the Postcolonial Challenge (~500 words)

**Section Purpose**: Close the post-Marx narrative with the theory's most recent, explicitly post-1989 defenses and their live controversies.

**Papers**: Chibber 2011 (cross-referenced from Sections 4 and 5 — full treatment here); Chibber 2013; Lazarus 2016; Prew, Rotta, Smith, and Vidal 2018.

**Content**: Chibber's verdict that attempts to rescue Cohen's classical, technologically deterministic theory of history (by Wright and colleagues, and by Carling) are largely unsuccessful and, where they partly succeed, do so by converting the theory into a class-struggle account — read alongside Section 4's internal analytical-Marxist debate and Section 5's Political Marxism as three converging post-Cohen verdicts; Chibber's separate, more polemical defense of capital's "universalizing tendencies" against Subaltern Studies and postcolonial theory, and Lazarus's sympathetic-critical response arguing Chibber's diagnosis of postcolonial theory as "Eurocentric" mischaracterizes a "conjuncturally distinct" Third Worldism — a controversy that remains unsettled and connects back to Section 7.2's Eurocentrism debates; the *Oxford Handbook of Karl Marx* introduction's synthetic claim that historical materialism's core categories continue to be defended, elaborated, and applied across the social sciences.

**Section Summary**: The theory's post-1989 defenders converge on abandoning the classical technological-determinist core while disagreeing on what replaces it (class-struggle theory, universalizing capital, ecological metabolism), leaving the field genuinely open rather than settled.
**Word Target**: 500 words

## Conclusion (~500 words)

**Purpose**: Synthesize the arc and position the review's account of where live disagreement remains.

**Content**:
- Summarize the recurring pattern: nearly every post-Marx current defines itself by relocating the site of historical-materialist explanation (consciousness, structure, individual rational action, class-relational property forms, discourse, gender/reproduction, the world-system, nature) away from the compressed, quasi-lawlike causality of the 1859 Preface — yet no successor framework has achieved uncontested consensus, and several (Political Marxism via Knafo and Teschke; metabolic-rift theory via Moore; social reproduction theory via Goikoetxea) face significant internal critique on grounds structurally similar to those leveled at the original orthodoxy.
- Note the unresolved cross-cutting questions the review has traced across domains: whether "economic determinism" ever accurately described Marx himself or only his codifiers (Section 1, revisited by every later current's self-positioning); whether functional or class-relational or discourse-theoretic categories can do the explanatory work classical historical materialism claimed (Sections 4-6); and whether the theory's core categories generalize beyond their originally European frame of reference (Sections 5, 7.2, 8).
- State that live disagreement persists on all these axes as of the most recent scholarship surveyed (2021-2026 symposia and monographs), rather than converging toward a settled post-Marx consensus.
- Close by noting the review's acknowledged limitations (non-Anglophone reception, New Materialism/Actor-Network boundary) as directions the debate may yet extend.

**Key Papers**: Chibber 2011; Knafo and Teschke 2020; Moore 2017; Prew et al. 2018.
**Word Target**: 500 words
