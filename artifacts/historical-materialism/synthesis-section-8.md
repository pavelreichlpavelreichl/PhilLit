## Section 7: Extending the Theory — Feminism, World-Systems, and Ecology

### Subsection 7.1: Feminist Historical Materialism

Marxist-feminist scholarship divides over whether women's oppression requires a second explanatory system alongside historical materialism's categories of production or can be explained from within a single, unitary framework. Hartmann (1979) argues that Marxist categories are "sex-blind": they explain capital's drive to accumulate but not why women specifically perform unpaid domestic labor and occupy subordinate wage positions. Her "dual-systems theory" treats patriarchy as a distinct social structure, predating and interacting with capitalism, grounded materially in men's historical control over women's labor power. Vogel (2013 [1983]) rejects this bifurcation, locating women's subordination within a single capitalist totality: the generational and daily reproduction of labor power is unpaid work that capital does not remunerate but structurally requires. Bhattacharya (2017) extends this unitary position into contemporary social reproduction theory, treating the production of things and the production of the people who produce things as two vantage points on one process, thereby dissolving rather than merely qualifying the base/superstructure distinction between "real" production and derivative domestic labor.

The adequacy of Engels's *Origin of the Family* as a materialist account of gender oppression is adjudicated differently from within this same unitary camp. Vogel (1996) credits Engels with linking private property's rise to women's subordination but faults him for treating the household as a holdover from earlier modes of production rather than as integral to capitalism itself; Blackledge (2018) instead defends a sympathetic reconstruction of Engels as still furnishing the resources for a unitary theory, against critics who treat *Origin* as either reductionist or superseded by later kinship anthropology. Portella and Busk (2024) attempt to bridge the dual-systems/unitary split directly by applying Marx's categories of formal and real subsumption, explaining both capitalism's appropriation of pre-existing patriarchal forms and its production of new, specifically capitalist forms of gendered oppression without granting patriarchy transhistorical autonomy. Ferguson (2016) identifies a further tension internal to unitary theory: whether its totalizing ontology of a differentiated capitalist whole can accommodate intersectionality's insistence on the irreducibility of race, gender, and class as distinct axes, or competes with it. Falquet (2024) recovers a comparatively neglected variant, francophone materialist feminism's "domestic mode of production" thesis (Delphy), independently anticipating the sex/race/class interlocking that later Anglophone debates approached separately, while Gabrielli (2024) traces the Wages for Housework current (Dalla Costa, Fortunati) that historically preceded, and against which, both Hartmann's and Vogel's positions were formulated.

### Subsection 7.2: World-Systems and Dependency Theory

World-systems analysis (Wallerstein 2004) and dependency theory (Amin 1976) relocate historical materialism's unit of analysis from the single social formation to the capitalist world-economy as a whole, structured into core, periphery, and semi-periphery zones linked by unequal exchange. As Section 5 notes, Brenner's charge of "neo-Smithian Marxism" holds that this move substitutes a generic, Smithian logic of trade and profit-maximization for Marx's analysis of historically specific class relations of production. Denemark and Thomas (1988) reconstruct this dispute as one in which Brenner and Wallerstein largely talk past each other: Brenner addresses capitalism's causal origins in specific agrarian class relations, while Wallerstein addresses the systemic reproduction of an already-constituted world-economy — distinct explanatory projects rather than straightforwardly competing empirical claims. Song (2015) reframes the disagreement similarly, arguing that the "trade versus production" dichotomy obscures what is actually at stake: a dispute over the proper scale and boundary of the unit of analysis (a bounded social formation versus a globally totalized world-economy) rather than a genuine choice between exchange- and production-centered explanation. Kvangraven (2020) mounts a more direct rebuttal, reconstructing dependency theory as a research programme defined by four tenets — a global historical approach, theorization of capitalism's polarizing tendencies, an explicit focus on structures of production, and attention to peripheral economies' specific constraints — and arguing that Brenner-style critiques caricature a tradition that already theorizes production relations, only at a world scale.

A second, largely independent axis concerns the tradition's own relation to Eurocentrism. Chian (2025) traces Wallerstein's Marxist turn to his decade of engagement with African decolonization and thinkers including Fanon, Nkrumah, and Cabral, situating world-systems analysis's genesis in anti-colonial struggle rather than metropolitan theory subsequently applied to the periphery. De Oliveira and Kvangraven (2023) recover the 1972 Dakar conference, organized by Amin, as a suppressed founding moment of a Global South-centered political economy, arguing dependency theory's marginalization from mainstream disciplinary histories was itself a form of epistemic Eurocentrism. Pradella (2017) supplies a textual complement from within Marx's own corpus, arguing his later writings and notebooks already incorporate colonialism and imperialism into a global, anti-Eurocentric analysis of accumulation.

### Subsection 7.3: Ecological Extensions

Foster (1999) reconstructs Marx's late engagement with Justus von Liebig's soil chemistry to argue that Marx diagnosed a "metabolic rift": capitalism's town/country division of labor severs the material exchange between humanity and the soil, rupturing natural nutrient cycling. Burkett (1999) grounds the same claim in value theory, arguing nature's role as a "free gift" appropriated without cost is constitutive of, rather than incidental to, Marx's critique of political economy. Saito (2017; 2023) extends this reading philologically, using Marx's unpublished natural-science notebooks (published in the ongoing MEGA2) to argue that Marx's ecological thought deepened after *Capital*, culminating in a late turn toward "degrowth communism" — a vision of post-capitalist society organized around sustainable use-value production rather than continued expansion of exchange-value. This reading bears directly on Cohen's primacy thesis discussed in Section 4: if Saito is correct, Marx himself came to doubt that developing the productive forces is either historically inevitable or emancipatory, requiring reconstruction rather than mere defense of the classical theory's technological core.

The metabolic-rift program has itself become internally contested. Moore (2017) charges rift-thinking with reproducing a Cartesian nature/society dualism that treats nature as an external object acted upon by an internal society, proposing instead a "world-ecology" in which capital and nature are co-produced. Malm (2019) defends metabolic rift's dualism as a considered "property dualism" — nature and society are equally material but really distinct relations — and argues Moore's hybridism, traced to Latour, dissolves the distinction needed to identify capitalism's specific responsibility for ecological harm; Foster and Burkett (2016) mount a parallel self-defense against a decade of such criticism. Gellert (2018) compares metabolic rift, world-ecology, and Bunker's ecologically unequal exchange, asking whether the three name substantively different mechanisms or largely redescribe the same diagnosis in rival vocabularies — a comparison that connects this dispute back to Subsection 7.2's unequal-exchange tradition.

**Section Summary**: Each extension revises a specific historical-materialist category — production (feminism), unit of analysis (world-systems), and human/nature metabolism (ecology) — rather than simply applying the theory unchanged, and each generates its own internal defender/critic structure paralleling the debates of Sections 4–6.
