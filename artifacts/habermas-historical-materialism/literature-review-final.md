## Introduction

No post-war theorist has invested more systematic effort in retaining the critical ambitions of historical materialism while reconstructing its theoretical foundations than Jürgen Habermas. His project is neither a restoration of orthodox Marxism nor an abandonment of it, but what he describes as a "reconstruction" — taking the theory apart and reassembling it on sounder foundations so that it better achieves its own stated aims (Habermas 1975, 287). This distinguishing posture, at once critical of and indebted to the Marxist tradition, defines the intellectual terrain that the present review maps.

The reconstruction did not emerge fully formed. It spans roughly three decades of sustained theoretical work. Knowledge and Human Interests (Habermas 1972) laid the groundwork by distinguishing three knowledge-constitutive interests — technical, practical, and emancipatory — and arguing that Marx had collapsed the practical and technical into a single productive paradigm, suppressing the independent logic of communicative interaction. The 1975 Theory and Society article "Towards a Reconstruction of Historical Materialism" translated that diagnosis into a positive programmatic proposal: social evolution follows a developmental logic grounded in communicative competence and moral-practical learning, not in the growth of productive forces alone (Habermas 1975, 287). Communication and the Evolution of Society (Habermas 1979) assembled the transitional essays that bridged the earlier critique of Marx to the fully elaborated theory, supplying the universal-pragmatic foundations for validity claims. The two volumes of The Theory of Communicative Action (Habermas 1984; 1987) then consolidated the reconstruction into a systematic social theory, announcing what Habermas characterized as a paradigm shift from production to communication as the basis of critical social inquiry.

Whether that trajectory represents completion or supersession is one of the central questions in the secondary literature. Rockmore (1989) offers the most detailed developmental account, tracing Habermas's engagement with Marx across four stages — interpretation, critique, reconstruction, and ultimate rejection — and arguing that The Theory of Communicative Action represents a replacement of historical materialism rather than its reconstruction. Against this reading, more recent scholarship has argued for a stronger line of continuity: Shoikhedbrod (2024) contends that two threads — the labor/interaction distinction and the emancipatory potential of the democratic constitutional state — run through Habermas's career from the 1970s essays to his 2019 philosophical history, and that the reconstruction reveals genuine lacunae in Marx while also leaving unacknowledged Marxian insights inadequately addressed. The tension between these readings — displacement versus continuation — structures much of the critical reception.

This review examines Habermas's reconstruction across four dimensions. It begins with Habermas's diagnosis of orthodox historical materialism: what he takes HM to claim, why he regards those claims as both indispensable and defective, and whether his portrait of the target is accurate. It then turns to the reconstruction program itself — the methodological concept of "rational reconstruction" and the positive theoretical content it produces, including the dual framework of communicative action and moral-practical learning that replaces the labor paradigm. The third dimension is Habermas's theory of social evolution, the substantive core of the reconstructed HM, and its contested developmental-logical thesis. Finally, the review assesses the critical reception: the charges of idealism, depoliticization, and methodological illegitimacy that have shaped the debate, and the more recent reassessments that seek a dialectical engagement with the reconstruction's gains and losses rather than wholesale acceptance or rejection.

## Section 1: The Target: Habermas's Reading of Orthodox Historical Materialism

Habermas's reconstruction of historical materialism begins with a diagnosis: the tradition he inherits is at once indispensable and theoretically defective. He does not reject the Marxist project of explaining social development through the dynamics of social reproduction; he argues, rather, that the project has been built on a philosophically inadequate foundation. That foundation is what he calls productivism — the thesis that labor, understood as purposive-rational action oriented toward the technical mastery of nature, is the primary medium through which the human species constitutes itself and through which historical change unfolds. The normative structures that Marx assigns to the superstructure — law, morality, culture — are on this reading derivative of the development of productive forces, unable to generate an independent logic of their own. Habermas's reconstruction requires dismantling this monistic framework before it can be reassembled on sounder foundations.

### 1.1 The Productivist Thesis

The conceptual lever for Habermas's diagnosis is the distinction between labor and interaction — or, in the vocabulary of *Knowledge and Human Interests* (Habermas 1972), between the technical and the practical knowledge-constitutive interests. Habermas argues there that the human species orients itself toward nature through an interest in technical control, toward the social world through an interest in mutual understanding, and toward the conditions of its own self-understanding through an interest in emancipation. These three interests are irreducibly distinct: they generate incommensurable forms of knowledge governed by different logics. Marx, on Habermas's reading, suppressed this distinction by modeling the species' entire self-formative process (*Bildungsprozess*) on the paradigm of labor. Communicative interaction — the domain of language, normative agreement, and intersubjective recognition — is thus collapsed into a derivative function of productive activity rather than acknowledged as a constitutively independent dimension of social life.

The 1975 article "Towards a Reconstruction of Historical Materialism" restates the diagnosis in explicitly historical-theoretical terms. Habermas (1975) argues that Marx's base-superstructure model makes normative progress a mere epiphenomenon of economic development, resulting in what he calls a "latent Hegelian philosophy of history in materialist guise": the progressive unfolding of reason is retained, but assigned to the growth of productive forces rather than to the self-movement of Spirit. Social evolution, on this reading, is driven by a single developmental engine — the expansion of technical productive capacity — while moral and legal learning are treated as its by-products rather than as dimensions with their own generative logic. For Habermas, this conflation is not incidental but structural: it follows from the reduction of species-formation to labor.

This reading of Marx has not gone uncontested. Bidet (2008) argues that Habermas, across works from *The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere* through *Legitimation Crisis*, consistently operates with a simplified Marx who has been stripped of his most distinctive analytical achievements. On Bidet's account, Habermas restores the ideological unity of the category of "productive force" that Marx's own value-form analysis had dissolved, and the conceptual separation of economics and politics that follows reverses Marx's anti-positivist intervention. Reichelt (2000) presses the same point at a more technical level: Habermas's reconceptualization of labor as purely purposive-rational action — an operation of technical mastery upon an objective world — distorts the categorial structure of *Capital*, where labor is not merely an instrumental activity but the site of capitalist domination mediated through the value-form. Guan Gui-qin (2008) similarly contends that Habermas misinterprets the basic theoretical commitments of historical materialism and therefore cannot claim to have achieved a genuine reconstruction of his target. Keane (1975) raises an earlier version of this objection, questioning whether the labor/interaction distinction adequately captures the dialectical relationship between labor and language in Marx's own texts.

### 1.2 The Normative Deficit

Habermas's second, related diagnosis concerns what the literature has come to call the normative deficit of historical materialism. If normative structures are merely superstructural — if law, morality, and culture possess no logic independent of the productive base — then critique of capitalism cannot draw on those structures as an independent normative standpoint from which to evaluate existing social arrangements. The critique would be measuring capitalism against standards that capitalism itself has already produced, leaving critical theory without a foothold outside the totality it wishes to interrogate. Finlayson (2009) situates this diagnosis within the broader arc of Frankfurt School critical theory, arguing that the normative problem — the difficulty of grounding social criticism without appealing to a substantive moral theory that the critical project tends to undermine — runs from Horkheimer through Adorno to Habermas, with Habermas's communicative ethics representing his distinctive, if not fully resolved, response to a shared predicament.

Habermas's 1975 article frames the normative deficit as internal to historical materialism's own commitments: the theory purports to offer emancipatory critique yet undermines the normative foundations such critique requires by treating norms as derivatives of productive development. The reconstruction is thus not merely a theoretical refinement but a response to a genuine contradiction at the heart of the tradition. By establishing that normative structures — and specifically the moral-practical learning embedded in communicative interaction — follow a developmental logic of their own, Habermas aims to provide the critical standpoint that orthodox historical materialism could not supply.

Árnason (1979), in one of the earliest scholarly responses to Habermas's reconstruction project, raises questions about whether this diagnosis is accurate or whether it reflects a selective portrayal of the Marxist tradition. Árnason's review examines whether Habermas's characterization of Marx as committed to a reductive, monistic productivism holds up against a more careful reading of the relevant texts, and argues that the developmental-logical framework Habermas proposes as a corrective carries its own teleological residues — raising the question of whether the normative deficit has been genuinely resolved or merely relocated.

Habermas's portrait of historical materialism is thus deliberate and stylized: it identifies productivism and the normative deficit as the tradition's constitutive weaknesses and positions the reconstruction as their remedy. Whether this portrait accurately represents what Marx's theory claims — or whether it is a polemically simplified foil constructed to motivate the communicative turn — is a question that divides critics (Bidet, Reichelt, Guan, Árnason) from those who accept the diagnosis as theoretically warranted, and it remains a contested premise on which the entire reconstruction rests.

## Section 2: The Reconstruction Program: Method and Positive Content

### 2.1 Rational Reconstruction as Method

The concept of "reconstruction" (*Rekonstruktion*) carries a precise methodological meaning in Habermas's work that distinguishes it from restoration, renaissance, or external critique. As Habermas (1975) formulates the procedure in his programmatic article, reconstruction takes a theory apart and reassembles it on new foundations in order to better achieve the theory's own stated goals. The target theory is not simply rejected or endorsed but is subjected to an internal transformation: its defensible commitments are retained while its defective foundations are replaced. Applied to historical materialism, this means preserving the materialist insight that social reproduction underlies social order and the evolutionary ambition that history exhibits directional change, while abandoning the labor paradigm that Habermas takes to be the source of HM's theoretical liabilities.

Jørgen Pedersen (2008) provides the most sustained methodological analysis of what rational reconstruction consists in as a philosophical procedure. On his account, the method occupies an unstable but productive position between the transcendental and the empirical: it reconstructs the implicit know-how of competent speakers and actors into explicit, generalizable rules, yet it does not claim transcendental necessity for its results, remaining in principle open to empirical disconfirmation. Pedersen traces this procedure through two of Habermas's research programs — universal pragmatics and discourse ethics — and in both cases the method generates reconstructive hypotheses that are, in his analysis, empirically underdetermined. The key unresolved problem he identifies is testability: it is not clear how to falsify a reconstructive hypothesis, since the implicit competences reconstructed are not directly observable and the criteria for adequate reconstruction are themselves contested. This problem bears directly on the historical-materialist reconstruction, where species-level learning capacities are inferred from sequences of historical formations rather than directly measured.

The normative dimension of the method is clarified by Titus Stahl (2013), who argues that rational reconstruction is the procedure that enables a specifically immanent form of critique. Rather than importing normative standards from outside the social practices under scrutiny — as traditional ideology critique imported standards from a philosophy of history or from class interests — Habermas locates the normative criteria within the communicative practices that reproduce the lifeworld. The validity presuppositions implicit in everyday speech acts — that assertions aim at truth, that normative claims aim at rightness, that expressive claims aim at sincerity — constitute an immanent normative resource that can be reconstructed and deployed against social arrangements that systematically violate them. Stahl's reading has an important consequence for how the replacement of ideology critique should be understood: it is not that Habermas abandons the critical ambition but that he relocates its normative ground from production relations to the communicative infrastructure of social life. *Communication and the Evolution of Society* (Habermas 1979) is the transitional text in which universal pragmatics — the formal analysis of the validity conditions of speech acts — provides the communicative foundation for the reconstruction, anticipating the full replacement of the labor paradigm that *The Theory of Communicative Action* will carry out.

### 2.2 Species-Competences and the Two-Level Theory

The positive content of the reconstruction is organized around two interlocking theses. The first concerns the evolutionary mechanism: Habermas (1979) replaces the growth of productive forces, which orthodox HM treats as the primary driver of social change, with a set of species-level competences — communicative, moral-practical, and cognitive — that develop according to their own reconstructible logic. These are not reducible to the technical capacities mobilized in production. The species constitutes itself, on this account, through communicative and normative learning as much as through labor, and historical transitions between social formations are triggered by the exhaustion of existing normative frameworks rather than by contradictions in the forces and relations of production. *Knowledge and Human Interests* (Habermas 1972) had already established the conceptual ground for this move by distinguishing the knowledge-constitutive interest in technical control from the practical interest in communicative understanding, demonstrating that these operate by incommensurable logics and cannot be collapsed into a single productive paradigm.

The second thesis concerns social ontology: the base/superstructure model is replaced by a two-level theory of society in which social integration — the communicatively reproduced lifeworld — and system integration — the functionally coordinated reproduction of society through the steering media of money and power — constitute analytically distinct but empirically intertwined dimensions of social order. Craig Browne (2019) identifies this reconceptualization of relations of production as forms of social integration mediated by systematically distorted communication as the central innovation of Habermas's reconstruction. What had appeared in orthodox Marxism as the causal primacy of an economic base over a normative superstructure is recast as a relation between two modes of social coordination, neither of which can be derived from the other. For Browne, however, this innovation carries an internal limitation: the developmental logic that governs transitions between social formations is too rigid and schematic, and by disengaging from the conflicts internal to material production, the reconstruction loses its capacity to theorize the social creativity through which historical change actually occurs.

Diester Misgeld (1981) raises a structurally different objection: that the turn to systems theory — specifically, the incorporation of Parsonian and Luhmannian functionalism to handle system integration — represents a retreat from the hermeneutic commitments of Habermas's earlier project. In the earlier work, the self-understanding of social actors retained explanatory and normative weight; the turn to functional systems analysis introduces a perspective from which actors' interpretations are rendered irrelevant to the reproduction of social structures. Social integration through communicative understanding is supplemented by, but risks being subordinated to, a functionalist account of systemic reproduction that is opaque from the inside. J. Domingues (1995) extends this diagnosis, arguing that the lifeworld/system duality, while it provides a workable replacement for base/superstructure, generates its own unresolved tension between the action-theoretic vocabulary — in which social life is constituted by the meaningful practices of agents — and the systems-theoretic vocabulary — in which society reproduces itself through functional mechanisms that bypass actors' intentions. This tension between the two vocabularies is not a marginal difficulty but runs through the reconstruction's social ontology, and it generates the central axis around which subsequent critical reception will turn.

## Section 3: Social Evolution: The Developmental Logic Thesis

### 3.1 The Piaget-Kohlberg Appropriation

The substantive core of Habermas's reconstructed historical materialism is a theory of social evolution grounded in developmental psychology. In both the 1975 programmatic article and *Communication and the Evolution of Society* (Habermas 1979), Habermas transposes Jean Piaget's model of cognitive-developmental stages and Lawrence Kohlberg's theory of moral stage development from the domain of individual ontogenesis to the level of collective social formations. The result is a phylogenetic account of history: social formations — mythical, religious-metaphysical, and modern-rational — can be arranged in a reconstructible sequence of increasing moral-practical learning, and transitions between them are triggered by systemic crises that cannot be resolved within the normative framework of the existing social order.

Central to this account is a distinction Habermas introduces in 1975 between the *logic* of development and the *dynamics* of development (Habermas 1975). The developmental logic designates the reconstructible sequence of stages itself: this sequence is rationally necessary in the sense that later stages presuppose competences acquired at earlier ones, and cannot be skipped without incoherence. The developmental dynamics, by contrast, designates the contingent historical processes — conflicts, crises, institutional breakdowns — that actually move a society from one stage to the next. This distinction is intended to preserve evolutionary directionality without collapsing into a deterministic philosophy of history: the sequence is rationally ordered, but its actualization remains historically contingent.

Oh (2023) provides a focused recent analysis of this distinction and identifies a systematic weakness in the logic/dynamics separation. Habermas's argument assumes that social learning is cumulative in the way that individual cognitive development is cumulative — that each stage deposits competences that cannot be reversed. Oh contests this assumption directly: individual learning may be cumulative within a single biography, but social learning is not similarly non-reversible at the collective level. Social formations can regress, competences can be lost, and institutional contexts that sustain moral-practical learning can collapse. Because Habermas underestimates developmental dynamics and overestimates the learning-accumulation model, the evolutionary mechanism underdetermines the historical transitions it is meant to explain. Mejía Fernández and Romero (2022) approach the same problem from a different angle, situating Habermas's appropriation of developmental psychology within the broader philosophical debate between Kantian normativism and Darwinian naturalism. They reconstruct Habermas's position as a form of "weak anthropological naturalism": drawing on evolutionary biology and developmental psychology while resisting the reduction of normative structures to natural facts. This mediating position is philosophically coherent but leaves open the question of how robustly the Piaget-Kohlberg framework can be transposed without distortion to the level of collective social history.

A related difficulty is raised by Arnason (1982), who argues that universal pragmatics — Habermas's theory of communicative competence grounded in speech-act validity claims — cannot straightforwardly serve as the normative foundation for a theory of social evolution without confronting the problem of cultural diversity. The validity claims that Habermas reconstructs from competent communicative practice may themselves reflect a particular cultural horizon rather than a genuinely universal pragmatic structure, so that the evolutionary sequence they are meant to ground inherits an unexamined cultural partiality.

### 3.2 The Ontogenetic Fallacy and Its Implications

The most systematic objection to Habermas's social evolution theory takes the form of an immanent critique developed from within the Frankfurt School's own intellectual orbit. Strydom (1992) identifies and names this objection as the "ontogenetic fallacy": the illegitimate transfer of the developmental logic of individual cognitive ontogenesis to the level of collective social evolution. The move is illegitimate, on this account, because the conditions that make cognitive development sequential and cumulative in an individual — a unified learning subject with continuous memory and increasing reflective capacity — do not obtain at the level of social formations. Collectives are not unified cognitive subjects; their learning processes are plural, conflictual, and structured by relations of power and institutional constraint that have no counterpart in individual developmental psychology. By modeling social evolution on individual ontogenesis, Habermas suppresses precisely what is socially specific about collective learning: its contested, power-laden, and intersubjectively fragmented character.

Strydom documents this objection as it was developed across a circle of Frankfurt School-adjacent theorists — Honneth, Joas, Eder, Arnason — each targeting a different dimension of the ontogenetic analogy. Habermas's response to these critics, examined in an earlier paper by Strydom (1987), involved significant concessions regarding the concept of collective learning. These concessions, however, generated new theoretical difficulties: the refinements introduced to accommodate the critique destabilized the broader framework rather than securing it. The trajectory of Habermas's revisions thus reveals an internal instability in the developmental logic thesis rather than a stable research program that can absorb objections without cost.

Antonio (1989) raises a further dimension of this instability. He argues that the evolutionary normative framework that Habermas imports from developmental psychology — grounding critical theory in a teleological account of species-level learning — contradicts the pragmatist historicism that Habermas simultaneously draws upon. Pragmatist approaches to normativity treat values as historically situated achievements that cannot be detached from their particular social contexts and projected onto a universal developmental sequence. By grounding critical theory in an evolutionary account of moral progress, Habermas reintroduces, in a reconstructed form, precisely the latent philosophy of history he claimed to have overcome in Marx. The evolutionary framework does not escape the teleological residue of orthodox Marxism; it displaces it to a different level.

Owen (1998) adds a further critical dimension by interrogating the universalist pretensions of the developmental logic thesis. The sequence of worldview formations — from mythical through religious-metaphysical to modern-rational — is presented as a universal, rationally reconstructible progression. Owen argues that this sequence in fact projects a specifically Western developmental trajectory onto world history, treating the rationalization trajectory of one civilization as the universal standard against which all social formations are measured. The stage model of worldview rationalization thereby carries Eurocentric implications that are internal to the framework rather than incidental to it: the very claim to rational universality in the stage sequence encodes a culturally particular standpoint.

Together, these objections constitute a sustained challenge not merely to a peripheral feature of Habermas's reconstruction but to its central mechanism. The ontogenetic fallacy objection strikes at the core of the reconstruction's evolutionary claims — if it succeeds, the reconstruction cannot escape the philosophy of history it sought to replace, but merely recasts it in the vocabulary of communicative learning.

## Section 4: The Theory of Communicative Action: Supersession or Completion?

### 4.1 The HM-to-TCA Transition

The question of whether *The Theory of Communicative Action* completes the reconstruction of historical materialism or replaces it with a fundamentally different theoretical framework turns on how one reads the intellectual trajectory from the 1970s reconstruction essays to the two-volume synthesis of 1984–1987. Rockmore (1989) offers the starkest answer: the engagement with Marx passes through four stages — interpretation, critique, reconstruction, and ultimate rejection — and TCA represents the final stage, in which the communicative paradigm displaces historical materialism rather than renewing it. On Rockmore's reading, TCA restores a quasi-transcendental subject in the form of communicative rationality, effectively returning to idealist ground under materialist cover.

The texts themselves support a more ambivalent verdict. TCA Volume 1 (Habermas 1984) announces the paradigm shift from production to communication by reconstructing the classical social-theoretical tradition — Marx, Weber, Mead, Durkheim — as a body of work converging, however incompletely, on the problematic of communicative rationality. Marx's identification of labor as the sole medium of social constitution is here treated not merely as a target for critique but as a theoretically deficient solution to a genuine problem: how are normative structures reproduced across historical time? The shift to communication reframes that problem rather than abandoning it. TCA Volume 2 (Habermas 1987) develops the system/lifeworld distinction and advances the colonization thesis, formally recasting the Marxian critique of capitalism as a communicative-theoretic account of reification: capitalist pathologies are misdiagnosed by Marxism as contradictions located in the sphere of production, whereas TCA relocates them as one-sided rationalization of the lifeworld under systemic pressure.

Stahl (2013) reads this transition as a methodological advance rather than a retreat. Where the HM reconstruction essays appealed to species-level evolutionary learning capacities as normative benchmarks — a residually evolutionary framework that Antonio (1989) identifies as sitting uneasily with Habermas's simultaneous pragmatist commitments — TCA grounds critique in the validity claims implicit in everyday communicative practice. This shift from phylogenetic learning to formal pragmatics constitutes, for Stahl, a form of immanent critique: the normative standards invoked against existing social arrangements are drawn from within the lifeworld practices they are used to evaluate, not imported from a developmental scheme external to actors. Honneth and Joas (1991), in their edited reception volume, map the main lines of debate that followed: whether the system/lifeworld dualism adequately replaces class analysis, and whether the colonization thesis captures what was most critical in the Marxian diagnosis of capitalism.

### 4.2 The Colonization Thesis: Gains and Losses

The colonization thesis is the site where what the HM-to-TCA transition gains and loses is most visible. On that thesis, systemic imperatives steered by money and power penetrate and distort the communicatively structured lifeworld, generating social pathologies — juridification, the monetization of welfare relations, the erosion of solidary forms of life — that TCA diagnoses as reification reconceived. The thesis inherits the critical ambition of the Marxian account of capitalism while displacing its theoretical foundations.

Jütten (2011) offers the most analytically precise assessment of what this displacement costs. The colonization thesis, he argues, contains a structural gap: Habermas treats juridification as a form of colonization — the penetration of legal-administrative steering media into domains of communicative action — but excludes the commodification of labor-power from the same diagnosis. This asymmetry is not incidental. By failing to treat labor-power as a domain subject to colonization, Habermas effectively removes the wage relation from critical scrutiny, depoliticizing class relations in the very framework that purports to reconstruct their critique. The result is a normative inconsistency: the thesis condemns systemic intrusion in some domains (welfare state, family) while immunizing it in others (labor markets).

Brand (1986) develops a related objection from a different angle. By recasting capitalist domination as lifeworld pathology — a disruption of communicative integration rather than a structured form of power — the colonization thesis obscures the specifically political dimension of emancipation. Brand reads this as a theoretical consequence of displacing Arendt's action-theoretic account of politics: where Arendt preserves a domain of political action irreducible to either labor or communication, Habermas's system/lifeworld architecture tends to absorb political conflict into a therapeutic model of lifeworld defense.

More recent work has attempted to extend the colonization thesis to contemporary capitalism while preserving its critical core. Condon (2020) proposes a reconstruction that distinguishes between delinguistified monetary communication and ordinary linguistic communication, reframing neoliberalism not as colonization from without but as "relinguistification" — the translation of monetary coding into the idiom of everyday speech that distorts communicative action from within. This revision moves the framework closer to ideology critique, potentially reconnecting what the HM-to-TCA transition separated. Prasai (2024) presses in the opposite direction, arguing that Habermas's own account of emancipation implies a "lifeworldization" — the progressive subordination of systemic logics to communicative reason across all spheres — that undermines the system/lifeworld boundary his diagnosis depends on. If the emancipatory telos tends toward the elimination of the boundary, the thesis that pathology consists in its transgression becomes internally unstable.

The trade-off that results from the HM-to-TCA transition can be stated precisely. TCA gains a normative foundation that the HM reconstruction essays lacked: immanent critique grounded in the validity presuppositions of communicative practice is not subject to the evolutionary teleology objection in the same form, and it does not require the contested analogy between individual ontogenesis and collective social learning. But the shift achieves this gain at the cost of critical purchase on class, production, and political economy. By relocating the pathology of capitalism from the sphere of production to the system/lifeworld boundary, TCA forfeits the resources for a systematic critique of the wage relation and the accumulation dynamic — resources that critics from Brand through Jütten have argued are not recoverable within the communicative framework.

## Section 5: Critical Reception: Idealism, Depoliticization, and Ongoing Debates

The critical reception of Habermas's reconstruction of historical materialism does not form a unified verdict. It fractures along distinct lines of argument, and the sharpest disagreements concern not whether Habermas succeeded in his stated aims but whether those aims were worth pursuing. Three main strands of critique can be identified: internal Frankfurt School objections to the systems-theoretic framework, feminist and political-economy charges that the communicative paradigm abandons critical purchase on capitalist social relations, and more recent reassessments that argue for productive engagement with the reconstruction rather than rejection.

### 5.1 Internal Frankfurt School Critiques

The most sustained internal critique originates with Axel Honneth. In *The Critique of Power* (Honneth 1991), Honneth accepts the communicative turn — the claim that social reproduction involves normatively structured interaction irreducible to purposive-rational action — but rejects the systems-theoretic dualism that Habermas erects around it. By treating the economy and the state as norm-free systems governed solely by money and administrative power, Habermas renders invisible the social struggles and moral conflicts that traverse those institutions. Workers contesting workplace authority, citizens challenging administrative decisions, and subordinate groups resisting domination all deploy communicative and moral resources within the very domains Habermas classifies as systemic. The dualism, for Honneth, forecloses precisely what a critical theory of society most needs to theorize. Ivkovic (2015) reconstructs this objection in detail, showing that Honneth's alternative program — theorizing social reproduction in action-theoretic terms shaped by inter-group dynamics of conflict and domination — was the direct motivation for his subsequent move to recognition theory. The trajectory from Habermas's systems-theoretic dualism to Honneth's recognition framework is, on this reading, one of immanent critique driving theoretical innovation.

Antonio (1989) identifies a distinct but related tension internal to Habermas's evolutionary framework, one that is traceable to the historical materialism reconstruction period itself. The quasi-biological theory of species-level learning stages — appropriated from Piaget and Kohlberg — that Habermas deploys to ground emancipatory claims contradicts the historicist approach of the American pragmatists he simultaneously draws upon. Pragmatist historicism holds that normative standards are embedded in particular forms of life and cannot be grounded in a transhistorical developmental sequence; the evolutionary normative framework imposes precisely such a sequence. Antonio argues the residual evolutionism in *The Theory of Communicative Action* is an unresolved inheritance from the historical materialism reconstruction, not a resolved feature of it.

### 5.2 Political Economy and the Idealism Charge

A more radical line of critique holds that the communicative paradigm does not merely distort the analysis of capitalism but abandons the theoretical tools adequate to understanding it as a social totality. Lotz (2015) formulates this objection with particular force: the normative turn in critical theory — the substitution of communicative and ethical criteria for the Marxian critique of political economy — entails the loss of a dialectical concept of society capable of grasping capitalist social relations in their totality, constituted as these are by money and labor rather than by communicative norms. The charge is not that communication is unimportant but that grounding critique in it displaces the analysis of capitalism as a structural formation. Friberg (2022) extends a closely related argument, drawing on Sørensen's defense of classical critical theory: the paradigm shift from production to communication entails abandonment of uncompromising critique of political economy, capitalism, and ideology.

Fraser's feminist political-economy critique (Fraser 1989) targets the system/lifeworld split from a different angle. The domestic sphere — the site of gender-differentiated care labor — is classified by Habermas as lifeworld, as communicatively integrated. But domestic labor is simultaneously symbolic and economic: it produces value, it is materially constrained, and it is organized through power relations that cannot be captured by the vocabulary of communicative distortion alone. The public/private distinction that structures Habermas's critical theory thus reproduces, rather than interrogates, the ideological separation that disadvantages women. Fraser argues that the needs interpretation framework fails to account for political contestation over what counts as a need in the first place — a contestation that crosses the system/lifeworld boundary Habermas treats as theoretically foundational.

Reichelt (2000) pursues the idealism charge at the level of Marx exegesis. The labor/interaction distinction treats labor as merely purposive-rational action, thereby reading Marx's concept of labor as simple technical production. This misses what Reichelt regards as the critical heart of Marxian political economy: the value-form analysis, in which labor under capitalism is the site of a specific form of social domination — not merely an instrument of production but a constitutively social relation through which surplus-value is extracted. Habermas's instrumental reading of labor loses this dimension, and with it the resources for a critique of capitalism that does not reduce to a critique of communicative distortion. Bohman (1999) situates this trajectory within the broader history of post-war Marxist theory: like the analytical Marxists who updated Marxism with rational-choice theory, Habermas progressively talked himself out of recognizable Marxism, arriving at a position better described as liberal social theory than as critical Marxism. Bohman does not call for a return to orthodoxy but advocates theoretical pluralism rather than a unified meta-theory of either communicative action or historical materialism.

### 5.3 Contemporary Reassessments

More recent scholarship resists the binary between defending and dismissing the reconstruction. Shoikhedbrod (2024) argues for a productive tension rather than a clean break: two threads in Habermas's project — the labor/interaction distinction and the democratic constitutional state as bearer of emancipatory potential — remain theoretically productive even in Habermas's later work. The distinction reveals genuine lacunae in Marx's reduction of social relations to productive forces, and the constitutional state takes over where Marx's revolutionary proletariat fails as a vehicle of emancipation. Shoikhedbrod contends that Habermas has yet to fully address insights in Marx that his own reconstruction points toward, making the reconstruction an unfinished resource rather than a superseded program. Pinzani (2022) traces Habermas's engagement with capitalism across four decades, from the crisis diagnoses of the 1970s through the debate with Wolfgang Streeck over whether capitalism has come to dominate democracy structurally. Where Streeck argues that Habermas systematically underestimates capitalism's role in shaping the global order by fixating on legality and bureaucratic procedure, Pinzani situates this as a continuing tension in Habermas's evolving theoretical program rather than a simple failure.

Browne (2019) identifies the antinomies structuring the reconstruction without endorsing either Habermas or his critics wholesale. The genuine innovation, for Browne, is the reconceptualization of relations of production as forms of social integration mediated by systematically distorted communication — a move that supplements rather than simply displaces political economy. But this innovation is constrained by an inflexible developmental logic that cannot accommodate the conflicts internal to material production or the forms of social creativity that drive historical change. The antinomy is structural: what makes the reconstruction theoretically innovative (the integration of communication into the analysis of social reproduction) also installs the rigidity that limits it (the developmental logic that abstracts from concrete social conflict).

The critical reception thus fractures along two axes. The first concerns the communicative turn itself: Honneth accepts it while rejecting its systems-theoretic consequences, whereas Lotz, Reichelt, and Fraser reject it as the source of the reconstruction's critical failures. The second concerns whether the reconstruction de-radicalizes Marxism: critics from Reichelt to Bohman argue that the shift from labor to communication abandons critical purchase on capitalism as a social totality, while Shoikhedbrod and Browne maintain that the reconstruction contains resources — acknowledged and unacknowledged — for a dialectical engagement with Marx that neither restoration nor dismissal can achieve.

## Conclusion

Habermas's reconstruction of historical materialism is best understood as a sustained attempt to preserve HM's emancipatory ambitions while eliminating what he diagnosed as its foundational defects: the reduction of social reproduction to labor, the epiphenomenal treatment of normative structures, and the inability to ground its own critical claims. Against these liabilities, the reconstruction introduces a dual social ontology — communicative action and system integration — that supports both a richer theory of social evolution and a non-reductive account of capitalist pathologies. The program, from the 1975 programmatic essay (Habermas 1975) through *The Theory of Communicative Action*, achieves what orthodox Marxism could not: a normatively grounded account of social development that derives its critical standards from within social practice itself rather than from a teleology of productive forces.

The costs of this achievement, however, are substantial and run through each of the reconstruction's major components. The colonization thesis, which recasts Marxian reification as systemic encroachment on communicative lifeworlds, systematically excludes the commodification of labor-power from its critical purview — treating it as a structural given rather than a site of political contestation — and thereby depoliticizes the class relations that remain central to any account of capitalist domination (Jütten 2011). The developmental logic thesis, which reconstructs the sequence of social formations on analogy with individual moral-cognitive development, carries residues of both teleology and Eurocentrism: the stage model presupposes a unidirectional trajectory whose endpoint is the rationalized lifeworld of Western modernity, and Antonio (1989) argues that this covert evolutionary normativity reproduces the philosophy-of-history deficiency the reconstruction was designed to overcome. The systems-theoretic turn that enables the colonization thesis simultaneously renders power and social struggle invisible within the economy and the state, precisely the lacuna that generated Honneth's alternative framework of recognition.

The deepest tension in the reconstruction concerns the theoretical status of the shift from labor to communication. For sympathetic readers, this shift makes theoretically explicit the communicative and normative dimensions that orthodox HM always suppressed, enabling a more adequate account of modern social integration than production relations alone can provide. For critics within the political-economy tradition, the same shift constitutes a de-radicalization — an abandonment of the dialectical concept of social totality adequate to capitalism in favor of a discourse-theoretic framework that can describe systemic distortions but cannot grasp their structural determinants. Browne (2019) captures this antinomy precisely: the reconstruction's innovation — treating relations of production as forms of communicatively mediated social integration — simultaneously disengages the framework from the conflicts internal to production that any critical theory of capitalism must confront.

Shoikhedbrod (2024) poses what remains the most productive framing for the reconstruction's contested legacy: is Habermas still, in any meaningful sense, the "last Marxist"? The question is not merely biographical but theoretical. Two threads that Shoikhedbrod identifies — the labor/interaction distinction as a diagnostic tool for HM's lacunae, and the democratic constitutional state as the site where emancipatory potentials are institutionalized — suggest that the reconstruction's resources are not exhausted by the critiques leveled against them, and that a productive tension between Habermas and Marx remains available to critical theory rather than a clean break. Whether one reads the reconstruction as achievement or retreat depends substantially on what one takes critical theory's primary task to be: normative grounding and democratic legitimation, or the analysis of capitalist social totality. That this question remains genuinely open — that the reconstruction is neither vindicated by its critics nor simply superseded by them — is precisely what makes it the unavoidable reference point for any post-Marxist critical theory that refuses both the productivist residues of orthodox HM and the normative voluntarism of its alternatives.
