## Section 4: The Theory of Communicative Action — Supersession or Completion?

### 4.1: The HM-to-TCA Transition

The question of whether *The Theory of Communicative Action* completes the reconstruction of historical materialism or replaces it with a fundamentally different theoretical framework turns on how one reads the intellectual trajectory from the 1970s reconstruction essays to the two-volume synthesis of 1984–1987. Rockmore (1989) offers the starkest answer: the engagement with Marx passes through four stages — interpretation, critique, reconstruction, and ultimate rejection — and TCA represents the final stage, in which the communicative paradigm displaces historical materialism rather than renewing it. On Rockmore's reading, TCA restores a quasi-transcendental subject in the form of communicative rationality, effectively returning to idealist ground under materialist cover.

The texts themselves support a more ambivalent verdict. TCA Volume 1 (Habermas 1984) announces the paradigm shift from production to communication by reconstructing the classical social-theoretical tradition — Marx, Weber, Mead, Durkheim — as a body of work converging, however incompletely, on the problematic of communicative rationality. Marx's identification of labor as the sole medium of social constitution is here treated not merely as a target for critique but as a theoretically deficient solution to a genuine problem: how are normative structures reproduced across historical time? The shift to communication reframes that problem rather than abandoning it. TCA Volume 2 (Habermas 1987) develops the system/lifeworld distinction and advances the colonization thesis, formally recasting the Marxian critique of capitalism as a communicative-theoretic account of reification: capitalist pathologies are misdiagnosed by Marxism as contradictions located in the sphere of production, whereas TCA relocates them as one-sided rationalization of the lifeworld under systemic pressure.

Stahl (2013) reads this transition as a methodological advance rather than a retreat. Where the HM reconstruction essays appealed to species-level evolutionary learning capacities as normative benchmarks — a residually evolutionary framework that Antonio (1989) identifies as sitting uneasily with Habermas's simultaneous pragmatist commitments — TCA grounds critique in the validity claims implicit in everyday communicative practice. This shift from phylogenetic learning to formal pragmatics constitutes, for Stahl, a form of immanent critique: the normative standards invoked against existing social arrangements are drawn from within the lifeworld practices they are used to evaluate, not imported from a developmental scheme external to actors. Honneth and Joas (1991), in their edited reception volume, map the main lines of debate that followed: whether the system/lifeworld dualism adequately replaces class analysis, and whether the colonization thesis captures what was most critical in the Marxian diagnosis of capitalism.

### 4.2: The Colonization Thesis — Gains and Losses

The colonization thesis is the site where what the HM-to-TCA transition gains and loses is most visible. On that thesis, systemic imperatives steered by money and power penetrate and distort the communicatively structured lifeworld, generating social pathologies — juridification, the monetization of welfare relations, the erosion of solidary forms of life — that TCA diagnoses as reification reconceived. The thesis inherits the critical ambition of the Marxian account of capitalism while displacing its theoretical foundations.

Jütten (2011) offers the most analytically precise assessment of what this displacement costs. The colonization thesis, he argues, contains a structural gap: Habermas treats juridification as a form of colonization — the penetration of legal-administrative steering media into domains of communicative action — but excludes the commodification of labor-power from the same diagnosis. This asymmetry is not incidental. By failing to treat labor-power as a domain subject to colonization, Habermas effectively removes the wage relation from critical scrutiny, depoliticizing class relations in the very framework that purports to reconstruct their critique. The result is a normative inconsistency: the thesis condemns systemic intrusion in some domains (welfare state, family) while immunizing it in others (labor markets).

Brand (1986) develops a related objection from a different angle. By recasting capitalist domination as lifeworld pathology — a disruption of communicative integration rather than a structured form of power — the colonization thesis obscures the specifically political dimension of emancipation. Brand reads this as a theoretical consequence of displacing Arendt's action-theoretic account of politics: where Arendt preserves a domain of political action irreducible to either labor or communication, Habermas's system/lifeworld architecture tends to absorb political conflict into a therapeutic model of lifeworld defense.

More recent work has attempted to extend the colonization thesis to contemporary capitalism while preserving its critical core. Condon (2020) proposes a reconstruction that distinguishes between delinguistified monetary communication and ordinary linguistic communication, reframing neoliberalism not as colonization from without but as "relinguistification" — the translation of monetary coding into the idiom of everyday speech that distorts communicative action from within. This revision moves the framework closer to ideology critique, potentially reconnecting what the HM-to-TCA transition separated. Prasai (2024) presses in the opposite direction, arguing that Habermas's own account of emancipation implies a "lifeworldization" — the progressive subordination of systemic logics to communicative reason across all spheres — that undermines the system/lifeworld boundary his diagnosis depends on. If the emancipatory telos tends toward the elimination of the boundary, the thesis that pathology consists in its transgression becomes internally unstable.

The trade-off that results from the HM-to-TCA transition can be stated precisely. TCA gains a normative foundation that the HM reconstruction essays lacked: immanent critique grounded in the validity presuppositions of communicative practice is not subject to the evolutionary teleology objection in the same form, and it does not require the contested analogy between individual ontogenesis and collective social learning. But the shift achieves this gain at the cost of critical purchase on class, production, and political economy. By relocating the pathology of capitalism from the sphere of production to the system/lifeworld boundary, TCA forfeits the resources for a systematic critique of the wage relation and the accumulation dynamic — resources that critics from Brand through Jütten have argued are not recoverable within the communicative framework.
