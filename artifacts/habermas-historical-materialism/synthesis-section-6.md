## Section 5: Critical Reception — Idealism, Depoliticization, and Ongoing Debates

The critical reception of Habermas's reconstruction of historical materialism does not form a unified verdict. It fractures along distinct lines of argument, and the sharpest disagreements concern not whether Habermas succeeded in his stated aims but whether those aims were worth pursuing. Three main strands of critique can be identified: internal Frankfurt School objections to the systems-theoretic framework, feminist and political-economy charges that the communicative paradigm abandons critical purchase on capitalist social relations, and more recent reassessments that argue for productive engagement with the reconstruction rather than rejection.

### 5.1: Internal Frankfurt School Critiques

The most sustained internal critique originates with Axel Honneth. In *The Critique of Power* (Honneth 1991), Honneth accepts the communicative turn — the claim that social reproduction involves normatively structured interaction irreducible to purposive-rational action — but rejects the systems-theoretic dualism that Habermas erects around it. By treating the economy and the state as norm-free systems governed solely by money and administrative power, Habermas renders invisible the social struggles and moral conflicts that traverse those institutions. Workers contesting workplace authority, citizens challenging administrative decisions, and subordinate groups resisting domination all deploy communicative and moral resources within the very domains Habermas classifies as systemic. The dualism, for Honneth, forecloses precisely what a critical theory of society most needs to theorize. Ivkovic (2015) reconstructs this objection in detail, showing that Honneth's alternative program — theorizing social reproduction in action-theoretic terms shaped by inter-group dynamics of conflict and domination — was the direct motivation for his subsequent move to recognition theory. The trajectory from Habermas's systems-theoretic dualism to Honneth's recognition framework is, on this reading, one of immanent critique driving theoretical innovation.

Antonio (1989) identifies a distinct but related tension internal to Habermas's evolutionary framework, one that is traceable to the historical materialism reconstruction period itself. The quasi-biological theory of species-level learning stages — appropriated from Piaget and Kohlberg — that Habermas deploys to ground emancipatory claims contradicts the historicist approach of the American pragmatists he simultaneously draws upon. Pragmatist historicism holds that normative standards are embedded in particular forms of life and cannot be grounded in a transhistorical developmental sequence; the evolutionary normative framework imposes precisely such a sequence. Antonio argues the residual evolutionism in *The Theory of Communicative Action* is an unresolved inheritance from the historical materialism reconstruction, not a resolved feature of it.

### 5.2: Political Economy and the Idealism Charge

A more radical line of critique holds that the communicative paradigm does not merely distort the analysis of capitalism but abandons the theoretical tools adequate to understanding it as a social totality. Lotz (2015) formulates this objection with particular force: the normative turn in critical theory — the substitution of communicative and ethical criteria for the Marxian critique of political economy — entails the loss of a dialectical concept of society capable of grasping capitalist social relations in their totality, constituted as these are by money and labor rather than by communicative norms. The charge is not that communication is unimportant but that grounding critique in it displaces the analysis of capitalism as a structural formation. Friberg (2022) extends a closely related argument, drawing on Sørensen's defense of classical critical theory: the paradigm shift from production to communication entails abandonment of uncompromising critique of political economy, capitalism, and ideology.

Fraser's feminist political-economy critique (Fraser 1989) targets the system/lifeworld split from a different angle. The domestic sphere — the site of gender-differentiated care labor — is classified by Habermas as lifeworld, as communicatively integrated. But domestic labor is simultaneously symbolic and economic: it produces value, it is materially constrained, and it is organized through power relations that cannot be captured by the vocabulary of communicative distortion alone. The public/private distinction that structures Habermas's critical theory thus reproduces, rather than interrogates, the ideological separation that disadvantages women. Fraser argues that the needs interpretation framework fails to account for political contestation over what counts as a need in the first place — a contestation that crosses the system/lifeworld boundary Habermas treats as theoretically foundational.

Reichelt (2000) pursues the idealism charge at the level of Marx exegesis. The labor/interaction distinction treats labor as merely purposive-rational action, thereby reading Marx's concept of labor as simple technical production. This misses what Reichelt regards as the critical heart of Marxian political economy: the value-form analysis, in which labor under capitalism is the site of a specific form of social domination — not merely an instrument of production but a constitutively social relation through which surplus-value is extracted. Habermas's instrumental reading of labor loses this dimension, and with it the resources for a critique of capitalism that does not reduce to a critique of communicative distortion. Bohman (1999) situates this trajectory within the broader history of post-war Marxist theory: like the analytical Marxists who updated Marxism with rational-choice theory, Habermas progressively talked himself out of recognizable Marxism, arriving at a position better described as liberal social theory than as critical Marxism. Bohman does not call for a return to orthodoxy but advocates theoretical pluralism rather than a unified meta-theory of either communicative action or historical materialism.

### 5.3: Contemporary Reassessments

More recent scholarship resists the binary between defending and dismissing the reconstruction. Shoikhedbrod (2024) argues for a productive tension rather than a clean break: two threads in Habermas's project — the labor/interaction distinction and the democratic constitutional state as bearer of emancipatory potential — remain theoretically productive even in Habermas's later work. The distinction reveals genuine lacunae in Marx's reduction of social relations to productive forces, and the constitutional state takes over where Marx's revolutionary proletariat fails as a vehicle of emancipation. Shoikhedbrod contends that Habermas has yet to fully address insights in Marx that his own reconstruction points toward, making the reconstruction an unfinished resource rather than a superseded program. Pinzani (2022) traces Habermas's engagement with capitalism across four decades, from the crisis diagnoses of the 1970s through the debate with Wolfgang Streeck over whether capitalism has come to dominate democracy structurally. Where Streeck argues that Habermas systematically underestimates capitalism's role in shaping the global order by fixating on legality and bureaucratic procedure, Pinzani situates this as a continuing tension in Habermas's evolving theoretical program rather than a simple failure.

Browne (2019) identifies the antinomies structuring the reconstruction without endorsing either Habermas or his critics wholesale. The genuine innovation, for Browne, is the reconceptualization of relations of production as forms of social integration mediated by systematically distorted communication — a move that supplements rather than simply displaces political economy. But this innovation is constrained by an inflexible developmental logic that cannot accommodate the conflicts internal to material production or the forms of social creativity that drive historical change. The antinomy is structural: what makes the reconstruction theoretically innovative (the integration of communication into the analysis of social reproduction) also installs the rigidity that limits it (the developmental logic that abstracts from concrete social conflict).

The critical reception thus fractures along two axes. The first concerns the communicative turn itself: Honneth accepts it while rejecting its systems-theoretic consequences, whereas Lotz, Reichelt, and Fraser reject it as the source of the reconstruction's critical failures. The second concerns whether the reconstruction de-radicalizes Marxism: critics from Reichelt to Bohman argue that the shift from labor to communication abandons critical purchase on capitalism as a social totality, while Shoikhedbrod and Browne maintain that the reconstruction contains resources — acknowledged and unacknowledged — for a dialectical engagement with Marx that neither restoration nor dismissal can achieve.
