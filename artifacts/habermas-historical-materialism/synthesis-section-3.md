## Section 2: The Reconstruction Program — Method and Positive Content

### 2.1: Rational Reconstruction as Method

The concept of "reconstruction" (*Rekonstruktion*) carries a precise methodological meaning in Habermas's work that distinguishes it from restoration, renaissance, or external critique. As Habermas (1975) formulates the procedure in his programmatic article, reconstruction takes a theory apart and reassembles it on new foundations in order to better achieve the theory's own stated goals. The target theory is not simply rejected or endorsed but is subjected to an internal transformation: its defensible commitments are retained while its defective foundations are replaced. Applied to historical materialism, this means preserving the materialist insight that social reproduction underlies social order and the evolutionary ambition that history exhibits directional change, while abandoning the labor paradigm that Habermas takes to be the source of HM's theoretical liabilities.

Jørgen Pedersen (2008) provides the most sustained methodological analysis of what rational reconstruction consists in as a philosophical procedure. On his account, the method occupies an unstable but productive position between the transcendental and the empirical: it reconstructs the implicit know-how of competent speakers and actors into explicit, generalizable rules, yet it does not claim transcendental necessity for its results, remaining in principle open to empirical disconfirmation. Pedersen traces this procedure through two of Habermas's research programs — universal pragmatics and discourse ethics — and in both cases the method generates reconstructive hypotheses that are, in his analysis, empirically underdetermined. The key unresolved problem he identifies is testability: it is not clear how to falsify a reconstructive hypothesis, since the implicit competences reconstructed are not directly observable and the criteria for adequate reconstruction are themselves contested. This problem bears directly on the historical-materialist reconstruction, where species-level learning capacities are inferred from sequences of historical formations rather than directly measured.

The normative dimension of the method is clarified by Titus Stahl (2013), who argues that rational reconstruction is the procedure that enables a specifically immanent form of critique. Rather than importing normative standards from outside the social practices under scrutiny — as traditional ideology critique imported standards from a philosophy of history or from class interests — Habermas locates the normative criteria within the communicative practices that reproduce the lifeworld. The validity presuppositions implicit in everyday speech acts — that assertions aim at truth, that normative claims aim at rightness, that expressive claims aim at sincerity — constitute an immanent normative resource that can be reconstructed and deployed against social arrangements that systematically violate them. Stahl's reading has an important consequence for how the replacement of ideology critique should be understood: it is not that Habermas abandons the critical ambition but that he relocates its normative ground from production relations to the communicative infrastructure of social life. *Communication and the Evolution of Society* (Habermas 1979) is the transitional text in which universal pragmatics — the formal analysis of the validity conditions of speech acts — provides the communicative foundation for the reconstruction, anticipating the full replacement of the labor paradigm that *The Theory of Communicative Action* will carry out.

### 2.2: Species-Competences and the Two-Level Theory

The positive content of the reconstruction is organized around two interlocking theses. The first concerns the evolutionary mechanism: Habermas (1979) replaces the growth of productive forces, which orthodox HM treats as the primary driver of social change, with a set of species-level competences — communicative, moral-practical, and cognitive — that develop according to their own reconstructible logic. These are not reducible to the technical capacities mobilized in production. The species constitutes itself, on this account, through communicative and normative learning as much as through labor, and historical transitions between social formations are triggered by the exhaustion of existing normative frameworks rather than by contradictions in the forces and relations of production. *Knowledge and Human Interests* (Habermas 1972) had already established the conceptual ground for this move by distinguishing the knowledge-constitutive interest in technical control from the practical interest in communicative understanding, demonstrating that these operate by incommensurable logics and cannot be collapsed into a single productive paradigm.

The second thesis concerns social ontology: the base/superstructure model is replaced by a two-level theory of society in which social integration — the communicatively reproduced lifeworld — and system integration — the functionally coordinated reproduction of society through the steering media of money and power — constitute analytically distinct but empirically intertwined dimensions of social order. Craig Browne (2019) identifies this reconceptualization of relations of production as forms of social integration mediated by systematically distorted communication as the central innovation of Habermas's reconstruction. What had appeared in orthodox Marxism as the causal primacy of an economic base over a normative superstructure is recast as a relation between two modes of social coordination, neither of which can be derived from the other. For Browne, however, this innovation carries an internal limitation: the developmental logic that governs transitions between social formations is too rigid and schematic, and by disengaging from the conflicts internal to material production, the reconstruction loses its capacity to theorize the social creativity through which historical change actually occurs.

Diester Misgeld (1981) raises a structurally different objection: that the turn to systems theory — specifically, the incorporation of Parsonian and Luhmannian functionalism to handle system integration — represents a retreat from the hermeneutic commitments of Habermas's earlier project. In the earlier work, the self-understanding of social actors retained explanatory and normative weight; the turn to functional systems analysis introduces a perspective from which actors' interpretations are rendered irrelevant to the reproduction of social structures. Social integration through communicative understanding is supplemented by, but risks being subordinated to, a functionalist account of systemic reproduction that is opaque from the inside. J. Domingues (1995) extends this diagnosis, arguing that the lifeworld/system duality, while it provides a workable replacement for base/superstructure, generates its own unresolved tension between the action-theoretic vocabulary — in which social life is constituted by the meaningful practices of agents — and the systems-theoretic vocabulary — in which society reproduces itself through functional mechanisms that bypass actors' intentions. This tension between the two vocabularies is not a marginal difficulty but runs through the reconstruction's social ontology, and it generates the central axis around which subsequent critical reception will turn.
