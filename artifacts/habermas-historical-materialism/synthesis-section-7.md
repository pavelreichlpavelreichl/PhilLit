## Conclusion

Habermas's reconstruction of historical materialism is best understood as a sustained attempt to preserve HM's emancipatory ambitions while eliminating what he diagnosed as its foundational defects: the reduction of social reproduction to labor, the epiphenomenal treatment of normative structures, and the inability to ground its own critical claims. Against these liabilities, the reconstruction introduces a dual social ontology — communicative action and system integration — that supports both a richer theory of social evolution and a non-reductive account of capitalist pathologies. The program, from the 1975 programmatic essay (Habermas 1975) through *The Theory of Communicative Action*, achieves what orthodox Marxism could not: a normatively grounded account of social development that derives its critical standards from within social practice itself rather than from a teleology of productive forces.

The costs of this achievement, however, are substantial and run through each of the reconstruction's major components. The colonization thesis, which recasts Marxian reification as systemic encroachment on communicative lifeworlds, systematically excludes the commodification of labor-power from its critical purview — treating it as a structural given rather than a site of political contestation — and thereby depoliticizes the class relations that remain central to any account of capitalist domination (Jütten 2011). The developmental logic thesis, which reconstructs the sequence of social formations on analogy with individual moral-cognitive development, carries residues of both teleology and Eurocentrism: the stage model presupposes a unidirectional trajectory whose endpoint is the rationalized lifeworld of Western modernity, and Antonio (1989) argues that this covert evolutionary normativity reproduces the philosophy-of-history deficiency the reconstruction was designed to overcome. The systems-theoretic turn that enables the colonization thesis simultaneously renders power and social struggle invisible within the economy and the state, precisely the lacuna that generated Honneth's alternative framework of recognition.

The deepest tension in the reconstruction concerns the theoretical status of the shift from labor to communication. For sympathetic readers, this shift makes theoretically explicit the communicative and normative dimensions that orthodox HM always suppressed, enabling a more adequate account of modern social integration than production relations alone can provide. For critics within the political-economy tradition, the same shift constitutes a de-radicalization — an abandonment of the dialectical concept of social totality adequate to capitalism in favor of a discourse-theoretic framework that can describe systemic distortions but cannot grasp their structural determinants. Browne (2019) captures this antinomy precisely: the reconstruction's innovation — treating relations of production as forms of communicatively mediated social integration — simultaneously disengages the framework from the conflicts internal to production that any critical theory of capitalism must confront.

Shoikhedbrod (2024) poses what remains the most productive framing for the reconstruction's contested legacy: is Habermas still, in any meaningful sense, the "last Marxist"? The question is not merely biographical but theoretical. Two threads that Shoikhedbrod identifies — the labor/interaction distinction as a diagnostic tool for HM's lacunae, and the democratic constitutional state as the site where emancipatory potentials are institutionalized — suggest that the reconstruction's resources are not exhausted by the critiques leveled against them, and that a productive tension between Habermas and Marx remains available to critical theory rather than a clean break. Whether one reads the reconstruction as achievement or retreat depends substantially on what one takes critical theory's primary task to be: normative grounding and democratic legitimation, or the analysis of capitalist social totality. That this question remains genuinely open — that the reconstruction is neither vindicated by its critics nor simply superseded by them — is precisely what makes it the unavoidable reference point for any post-Marxist critical theory that refuses both the productivist residues of orthodox HM and the normative voluntarism of its alternatives.
