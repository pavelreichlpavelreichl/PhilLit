## Conclusion

Across the seven sections of this review, one asymmetry recurs: Lukács's negative diagnosis of reification has proven more durable than the constructive apparatus built to overcome it. Reification's core insight — that capitalist social form imposes a "contemplative" stance on both everyday consciousness and classical German philosophy — is extended by Adorno, transformed by Honneth, and defended in its capitalism-specific form by Feenberg and Kavoulakos against Honneth's de-ontologizing generalization (Honneth 2008; Feenberg 2014). By contrast, the constructive doctrines meant to resolve reification — the identical subject-object, imputed class consciousness, and historical teleology — are the literature's persistent target, criticized from within (López, Jaffe, Menozzi) and from without (Althusser, Merleau-Ponty, analytic Marxism, Postone).

The central synthetic question this review has tracked section by section — whether the subject-object antinomy is resolved, relocated, or dissolved — admits, on the evidence assembled here, a single descriptive answer: displacement rather than resolution. *Theory of the Novel*'s "transcendental homelessness" already names the antinomy; HCC relocates it onto the proletariat's practical self-recognition as "identical subject-object of history"; and the *Ontology* relocates it again, from collective class consciousness to the individual labor-act's teleological positing within a causal nexus (Section 5). At no point does the secondary literature converge on a text where the antinomy is simply solved. This is precisely why continuity theorists (Löwy, Arató and Breines) and rupture theorists (the Budapest School, Tosel) can each marshal genuine textual support: both are tracking the same recurring problem under different names at different stages, and each stage supplies enough apparent novelty to support a rupture reading and enough persistence of the underlying question to support a continuity reading. Jay's (2018) question — whether HCC's singular-subject historical narrative can survive without an identifiable subject to replace the proletariat — sharpens the point: post-Lukácsian substitutes (Badiou's and Žižek's "Event") relocate the problem again rather than dispensing with it.

Four fault lines recur across every section examined here and organize the debate independently of any single text: the status of a dialectics of nature (Section 3.4, Section 5.2); party authority versus proletarian self-activity in securing imputed consciousness (Section 3.3–3.4); the open-versus-closed structure of historical teleology (Section 3.5, Section 5.1); and the genealogical question of how far Lukács's categories are Hegelian, neo-Kantian, or phenomenological in origin (Section 2.2, Section 4.1).

These fault lines mark the review's synthesis gap: existing scholarship has not produced a unified assessment, across the full young-to-late arc, of whether the teleology/subject-object problem is genuinely dissolved by the *Ontology*'s labor-model or merely displaced onto it once more, nor has it settled whether reification can retain critical force once detached from the Hegelian-teleological apparatus in which Lukács embedded it — the live disagreement between Thompson's and Ivanova's detachability projects and Honneth's, Kavoulakos's, and Feenberg's insistence on capitalism-specific anchoring (Dorahy 2023). Several areas remain comparatively under-researched in the Anglophone literature surveyed here: the untranslated *Prolegomena* and Moscow-period writings; Rickert and Windelband as direct rather than inferred targets of the reification critique; the early essay "Towards a Methodology of the Problem of Organisation"; and the Benjamin–Lukács and early Horkheimer–Lukács relations, each gestured at but not systematically treated across the domains surveyed.
