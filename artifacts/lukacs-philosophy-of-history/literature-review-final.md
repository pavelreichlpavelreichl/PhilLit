## Introduction

Georg Lukács (1885–1971) occupies a position in twentieth-century Marxist thought that is difficult to summarize without already taking a side in the debates his work provoked. *History and Class Consciousness* (1923; hereafter HCC) is routinely treated as the inaugural text of what Perry Anderson calls "Western Marxism" — a tradition defined, on Anderson's account, by its turn away from the political economy and strategic questions of the Second International and toward philosophical foundations: method, consciousness, and the relationship between subject and history (Anderson 1976). Stahl's overview traces a set of recurring concepts — totality, reification, anti-positivism — across Lukács's pre-Marxist, HCC-era, self-critical, and late-ontological periods without asserting where, if anywhere, a decisive break occurs (Stahl 2013). That question of periodization is not incidental to Lukács scholarship; it is one of the field's organizing disputes, and this review returns to it throughout.

HCC itself advances two closely joined projects. The first is critical and negative: Lukács diagnoses reification as the general form taken by Marx's commodity fetishism under developed capitalism, and traces its effects through the "antinomies of bourgeois thought," the "contemplative" stance of classical German philosophy, neo-Kantian and Weberian historicism, and the scientistic economism of Second-International Marxism (Lukács 1971). The second project is constructive and positive: against the contemplative stance, Lukács proposes totality as method, the standpoint of the proletariat as a privileged epistemic position, "imputed" (*zugerechnetes*) class consciousness as opposed to merely empirical consciousness, and praxis as the activity through which the proletariat becomes, in Lukács's phrase, the "identical subject-object of history." Jay's intellectual history of the category of totality shows how this Hegelian inheritance was repurposed by Lukács to grasp capitalist society as an internally mediated whole rather than a collection of isolated, reified facts, and how the category was subsequently contested, transformed, and in some cases abandoned by later Western Marxists, Althusser prominent among them (Jay 1984). Feenberg situates the antinomies critique and the theory of reification as the pivotal moment in a continuous "philosophy of praxis" running from Marx through the Frankfurt School, underscoring that Lukács's critique and construction are not separable projects but two faces of one argument (Feenberg 2014).

A meta-question runs beneath nearly every debate this review surveys: how to periodize Lukács's oeuvre — the early, pre-Marxist writings; HCC itself; the 1967 self-critical preface; and the late *Ontology of Social Being* — and whether that sequence exhibits rupture or continuity of problem. The answer one gives conditions how much weight falls on the "young" versus the "old" Lukács, and whether the 1967 preface should be read as genuine self-correction or retrospective repositioning.

The review also tracks Lukács's critical afterlife across four broad receptions: the structuralist and anti-humanist critique associated with Althusser; the phenomenological engagement associated with Merleau-Ponty; analytic-Marxist scrutiny of holistic and teleological explanation; and the Frankfurt School and Western Marxist tradition more broadly (Adorno, Honneth, Korsch, Gramsci, Goldmann, Jameson, Postone), which variously inherits, transforms, or rejects the reification thesis and its constructive counterpart.

The review proceeds in seven sections. Section 1 addresses the periodization debate; Section 2 reconstructs the critique of the tradition; Section 3 turns to the constructive doctrines; Section 4 examines Lukács's Hegelian and Marxian sources; Section 5 presents the late *Ontology*; Sections 6 and 7 assess the structuralist/phenomenological/analytic objections and the Frankfurt School/Western Marxist reception. Throughout, the review returns to a single synthetic question: whether the subject-object antinomy Lukács's critics diagnose is resolved, relocated, or dissolved across his trajectory.

## Section 1: The Shape of a Career: Development and the Periodization Debate

Any account of Lukács's philosophy of history inherits a prior, unresolved question about the shape of his career. The secondary literature organizes itself around three recurring interpretive models — a rupture or epistemological-break model, a continuity-of-problem model, and a biographical-contingency model — to which a fourth, self-periodization-as-tactics reading of Lukács's own retrospective framings must be added (Stahl 2013). These models do not simply describe different scholarly emphases; they generate different weightings of early versus late texts as evidence for what "Lukács's" theory of history actually is. The 1967 preface to *History and Class Consciousness* functions in this literature as the hinge document on which the break-versus-continuity question is adjudicated, because it is Lukács's own retrospective self-periodization, and how one reads it constrains how one reconstructs the mature theory of history that follows.

### 1.1 The continuity thesis and its origins

The founding statements of the continuity model treat Lukács's 1918 turn to Bolshevism not as an abandonment of his pre-Marxist sensibility but as its dialectical extension. Löwy (2023 [1979]) reads the trajectory from Sunday Circle romanticism through *The Theory of the Novel* to the Communist Party as "romantic anti-capitalism" redirected from aesthetic and ethical withdrawal toward revolutionary politics. Arato and Breines (1979) similarly argue that Western Marxism as a tradition originates in problems inherited from Simmel, Weber, and neo-Kantianism — above all a proto-concept of reification already latent in the earlier problem of "soul and form" — such that *History and Class Consciousness* transposes pre-Marxist categories onto the standpoint of the proletariat rather than replacing them. Later contributions specify the persistent problem differently: Markus (2011) locates it in "the problem of culture," Gilbert (2020) in a "crisis of subjectivism" that positing the proletariat as "subject-object" of history brings under conceptual control, and Westerman (2017) in the transposed function of Dostoevsky reception across the ethical and political phases. Lendvai (2008) supplies fine-grained internal periodization of the pre-1918 stages themselves. Perica (2020) proposes a *tertium datur* — a third way between simple rupture and simple continuity, organized around the dialectic of form and content rather than either term's supposed dominance in a given phase. Together these readings license treating *Theory of the Novel*'s diagnosis of "transcendental homelessness" as a precursor to the theory of reification, and they locate the antinomy of subject and object already within the pre-Marxist problematic, as something the 1918 conversion redirects rather than newly creates.

### 1.2 Contingency, conversion, and accommodation

A second cluster offers an archival and biographical counterweight to philosophically deterministic continuity narratives. Congdon (1983) and Gluck (1985), drawing on materials from the 1972 Lukács Archive discovery, stress personal crisis and the specific Sunday Circle milieu (Mannheim, Hauser, Balázs) over any internally necessary philosophical evolution, with Gluck treating revolutionary socialism as only one live option within a fragmented postwar ideological field rather than a predetermined outcome. Kadarkay (1991) supplies the comprehensive biographical synthesis on which both continuity and rupture theorists draw for dating specific turning points. Nakhimovsky (2022) reframes the 1918–19 conversion essays themselves, arguing against reading "Bolshevism as a Moral Problem" and "Tactics and Ethics" as autobiographical way-stations and proposing instead a Weberian-Fichtean *Realpolitik* of ethically responsible revolutionary action — a reframing that unsettles what counts as evidence for either continuity or rupture. A further sub-debate addresses the Moscow period: Tihanov (2022) situates Lukács's exile alongside Béla Balázs under the category of "exilic Marxism," while Congdon (2007) reads the unpublished 1925–26 manuscript "Tailism and the Dialectic" as evidence of a growing "apotheosizing of the party" that anticipates the 1933 retraction. This cluster introduces the question, decisive for the 1967 preface, of whether Lukács's self-criticism reflects genuine philosophical revision or structural pressure exerted by party and exile.

### 1.3 The 1967 preface as hinge

Baehrens (2023a, 2023b) reads the 1922 and 1967 prefaces to *History and Class Consciousness* against one another as two competing "agenda-setting" texts rather than treating the later preface as transparent autobiographical testimony, a framing that reopens rather than settles the repudiation/accommodation/revision trichotomy. The "how many Lukácses" problem recurs even within Lukács's final, unfinished project: Forczek (2023) shows that the Budapest School's charge that the late *Ontology of Social Being* mixes "two incompatible ontologies" (Fehér, Heller, Márkus, and Vajda 1976; see also Tosel 2008) has itself become a periodization dispute internal to the late work, one Forczek argues dissolves once Lukács's thesis of the *post festum*-rationality of history is properly understood. Macciò (1972) shows this framing device already active in Anglophone reception immediately after Lukács's death.

**Section Summary**: No consensus exists on periodization. The review adopts the three-models frame — together with the self-periodization-as-tactics reading — as an analytic device for tracking how the antinomy of subject and object is treated across the oeuvre, rather than endorsing any one model, and hands the continuity/rupture question forward to Sections 3 and 5.

## Section 2: The Critique of the Tradition: Reification, the Antinomies, and the Contemplative Stance

The negative half of Lukács's philosophy of history begins from Marx's analysis of the commodity-form and extends it into a general diagnosis of bourgeois thought. In "Reification and the Consciousness of the Proletariat," the central essay of *History and Class Consciousness*, Lukács argues that the same structure Marx identified in commodity fetishism — a social relation between persons appearing as a relation between things — recurs as the "contemplative" stance of a subject confronting a world of lawlike, quantified facts from which its own practical activity has been evacuated (Arato 1972). The concept's explanatory reach is considerable: it is meant to unify the critique of classical German philosophy's antinomies, of neo-Kantian and Weberian sociology, and of Second-International Marxism's scientism within a single social-structural diagnosis. But the secondary literature divides sharply over two questions this section takes in turn: what conceptual sources the diagnosis actually draws on, and whether Lukács's own resolution of the antinomies he identifies is secure.

### 2.1 The targets: classical German philosophy, neo-Kantian/Weberian sociology, Second-International Marxism

Lukács's first target is the sequence of antinomies — freedom and necessity, form and content, subject and object — that López (2020a) reconstructs as recurring, in successively refined form, across Kant, Fichte, Schelling, and Hegel; on this reconstruction, each philosopher's attempted resolution reproduces the dualism it sought to overcome, a pattern Lukács reads as symptomatic of the same reified social structure rather than as a sequence of purely internal philosophical failures. Parker (1981) offers a sustained study of the companion essay "The Antinomies of Bourgeois Thought," treating Lukács's diagnosis of the Kantian "thing-in-itself" as ideology critique embedded within his broader anti-revisionist polemic. A second target is neo-Kantian-inflected sociology: Bueno and Teixeira (2017) trace how Lukács absorbs and historicizes Weber's account of formal-calculative rationalization and Simmel's diagnosis, in *The Philosophy of Money*, of a "tragedy of culture" in which objectified cultural forms become estranged from subjective life, arguing that Lukács converts what Weber and Simmel treat as a transhistorical fate of modernity into a critique specific to generalized commodity exchange. López (2020b) extends this line by situating Lukács's early 1920s philosophy as a metacritique of Simmel's "absolute relativism." The third target is Second-International "orthodox" Marxism. Colletti (1971) characterizes Kautsky- and Plekhanov-era Marxism as a positivist, evolutionary "scientific socialism" that collapses Marx's dialectical method into a deterministic philosophy of history; Soares de Souza (2024) reconstructs Lukács's own methodological essay, "What Is Orthodox Marxism?," as grounding social critique not in economic doctrine but in the categories of totality, historicity, and mediation, while Tripodi (2024) traces the afterlife of this totality-versus-positivism fault line into the 1937 Horkheimer-Neurath debate, showing Lukács's "point of view of totality" already dividing Hegelian-Marxist from logical-empiricist currents within Critical Theory's own prehistory.

### 2.2 The genealogy dispute: neo-Kantian, Hegelian, or phenomenological?

Where these targets are agreed upon, the theoretical apparatus used to unify them is not. Kavoulakos (2019, 2018a) argues that reification depends on a specifically neo-Kantian mechanism — the "form of objectivity" inherited from Emil Lask — through which Marx's commodity form becomes the "archetype" of capitalist objectivity by converting qualitative content into quantitative, calculable categories. Kinzel (2026) sharpens this by distinguishing two idealist vocabularies operating simultaneously in the essay: a (neo-)Kantian form/content vocabulary that she argues performs genuine critical work, diagnosing capitalism as the domination of form over content, and a Hegelian vocabulary that she argues instead constrains Lukács's own insights into the contingent character of political practice and history. Vervoort (2025) situates this neo-Kantian inheritance within a longer lineage, arguing that Lukács and Foucault share a common problematic concerning historically constituted, non-natural social "forms." Westerman (2019) supplies a rival genealogy, tracing the essay's philosophical scaffolding to Lukács's unfinished pre-Marxist "Heidelberg Aesthetics" project and its Husserlian, Laskian, and Rieglian sources, on the view that this phenomenological account of subjectivity constituted within social relations avoids problems attaching to Hegelian-teleological readings. Complementing this dispute, modality studies by Miller (2024) and Rametta (2024) examine Lukács's category of "objective possibility," tracing its Weberian methodological origin while showing how Lukács redirects it, via Hegelian categories of possibility, actuality, and necessity, into a non-deterministic social ontology distinct from Hegel's own Logic. Whether reification is read as fundamentally neo-Kantian, Hegelian, or phenomenological in derivation thus bears directly on how "materialized" or "re-idealized" the resulting theory of history should be judged to be.

### 2.3 Is the resolution secure? Immanent and value-form critiques

A further and more consequential dispute concerns whether Lukács's own resolution of the antinomies survives scrutiny. Jaffe (2020) argues that the "standpoint of the proletariat," posited as the singular, necessary solution to the antinomies, illegitimately transposes logical necessity proper to philosophical determinations into socio-historical possibilities, so that the standpoint is "abstractly derived" and reproduces at a higher level the very antinomic structure it claims to overcome. López (2020a) reaches a structurally similar verdict from a different angle, contending that Lukács's concept of praxis is built to bear the weight of, and overcome, Hegel's Absolute, yet remains "unconsciously" within speculative philosophy — a "conceptual mythology" rather than its supersession. A parallel challenge comes from value-form theory. Rose (1981), as reconstructed by Lazarus (2020), argues that Lukács conflates the specific Marxian category of commodity fetishism with the broader and more diffuse category of reification, a flattening that loses the speculative character of Marx's value-form analysis; Lazarus judges Rose's own proposed alternative unfulfilled but treats the tension she identifies as a genuine methodological fork for value-form-theoretic engagements with Lukács. Ivanova (2026) responds to this pressure from within the reification tradition itself, arguing for a narrower, economically anchored respecification of the concept against looser extensions — a corrective aimed in part at treatments like Bewes (2002), which generalizes reification into a diffuse existential category of "anxiety" characteristic of late capitalism. Feenberg (2014) offers a further framing of the stakes: he reconstructs Lukács's claim that the antinomies cannot be resolved either critically (Kant) or speculatively (Hegel) but only "metacritically," through a sociological desublimation showing that seemingly philosophical problems are rooted in social reality and resolvable only by social change — a formulation that anticipates the Frankfurt School's later negative-dialectical treatment of the same antinomies (discussed further in Section 7).

Taken together, this literature establishes reification as a diagnostic capable of unifying the critique of philosophy, science, and social form, but leaves two questions open before the constructive theory of history can be assessed on its own terms: which genealogy (neo-Kantian, Hegelian, or phenomenological) best explains the diagnosis's critical power, and whether the "standpoint of the proletariat" resolves the antinomies it identifies or merely relocates them at a higher level — a tension the next section's constructive doctrines must be read against.

## Section 3: The Constructive Theory: Totality, the Identical Subject-Object, Class Consciousness, and Praxis

Where Section 2 traced Lukács's critique of the "contemplative" bourgeois stance, the constructive half of *History and Class Consciousness* must supply an alternative capable of grounding action rather than mere diagnosis. Four doctrines carry this weight jointly: totality as method, the proletariat as "identical subject-object of history," imputed (*zugerechnetes*) class consciousness, and praxis as the vehicle converting the first three into revolutionary activity. Each, however, imports its own unresolved tension — over residual Hegelianism, party authority, the dialectics of nature, and the teleological shape of history — such that the constructive theory does not so much close the antinomy diagnosed in Section 2 as relocate it onto new terrain.

### 3.1 Totality as method

Lukács's claim, in "What Is Orthodox Marxism?," that totality is "the bearer of the principle of revolution in science" makes methodological holism the decisive Marxist commitment, prior to any economic thesis: grasping capitalist society as an internally mediated whole rather than isolated, reified facts. Jay (1984) traces this category's career through Western Marxism, showing that totality migrated from an ontological claim about society into a contested methodological posture that later thinkers (Althusser prominently, per Section 6) would treat as a liability rather than a resource. Schmidt (1975) narrows the focus to totality's pedagogical function, arguing that the "concrete totality" underwrites proletarian *Bildung* through immersion in, rather than abstraction from, the social whole. Alzu (2020) tracks how the concept changes across Lukács's oeuvre, arguing that a substantial shift occurs between HCC and the later *Ontology*, driven by the need to make room for a subjective practice not ineluctably alienated by totality — a developmental claim anticipating Section 5's treatment of the *Ontology*'s altered concept. Thompson (2011) presses detachability furthest, reconstructing totality as a resource for critical theory while bracketing the historical-teleological apparatus — the proletariat as revolutionary subject — in which Lukács originally embedded it, leaving open whether totality can survive detachment from the subject-object claim examined next.

### 3.2 The identical subject-object and praxis

Lukács's most contested claim is that the proletariat, becoming conscious of itself as a commodity, simultaneously becomes conscious of the totality of capitalist relations that produced it, occupying at once the position of object determined by history and subject transforming it (Lukács 1971) — an explicitly materialist inheritance of Hegelian Spirit that the secondary literature divides over how literally to take. Westerman (2010) argues that the technical mechanics of the claim — how self-consciousness and consciousness-of-totality could be a single act — are best reconstructed via Husserlian phenomenology's account of constitution rather than via the *Phenomenology of Spirit* alone, complicating readings that derive the concept from Hegel exclusively. López (2019), by contrast, reconstructs praxis as the concept designed to bear the weight of Hegel's Absolute and overcome it, but concludes on immanent grounds that Lukács does not succeed: praxis's insight into the historical totality is won only retrospectively, after the event, in the manner of Hegelian reflection, so that the subject-object remains "unconsciously" within the speculative structure it claims to supersede — sharpening, rather than resolving, the question raised in Section 2 by Jaffe and López's antinomies work, of whether the standpoint of the proletariat relocates rather than dissolves the subject-object problem it was meant to answer. López (2017), reviewing Feenberg's philosophy-of-praxis reconstruction, maps this interpretive terrain and proposes a rival, mediation-centered Hegelian reading, while Rockmore (1992b) situates the standpoint-of-the-proletariat resolution within a historicized, contextualist conception of reason that explains why a specific social position could yield superior insight into the whole.

### 3.3 Imputed (*zugerechnetes*) class consciousness

The doctrine that makes the subject-object claim politically actionable is Lukács's distinction between the empirical, psychological consciousness actual workers possess and the class consciousness "imputed" to the proletariat as the rational response its objective class position would produce if fully thought through. Lanning (2009, 2002) supplies the most sustained Anglophone treatment of this distinction, tracing its operation across historical necessity as self-activity, the gap between class experience and false consciousness, and — in the later study — its extension into Lukács's realist aesthetics, where realist form is held to represent precisely the gap between empirical and imputed consciousness that modernism cannot register. Outside Lanning's work, imputed consciousness is treated mainly as a corollary of the antinomies rather than as a freestanding concept, leaving it comparatively under-studied relative to totality and the subject-object claim.

### 3.4 The Tailism debate: party authority and the dialectics of nature

Lukács's unpublished 1925–26 manuscript defending HCC against party critics Rudas and Deborin (Lukács 2000) supplies primary evidence for how far the "imputed" consciousness of 3.3 depends on party mediation. Fracchia (2013) argues that Lukács's purely conceptual derivation of reification as the necessary form of working-class consciousness commits him to a "philosophical Leninism," in which consciousness must be attributed to the party rather than achieved through proletarian self-activity — a considerably more authoritarian reading than Lanning's. Burkett (2013) identifies a related tension: Lukács's Tailism-era concession of an objective dialectic in nature sits uneasily with his continued practical restriction of dialectical method to the social-historical realm. Feenberg (2015) responds to both, defending alternative, less concessive readings of Lukács's positions on class consciousness and the social construction of nature. Moir's (2020) translation of Bloch's contemporaneous 1923 review situates this dispute historically, showing that Bloch, unlike Lukács, never abandoned a dialectics of nature, even as both thinkers converged on the present as the privileged "Archimedean point" of revolutionary praxis.

### 3.5 Historical teleology

The constructive theory's temporal structure is its most contested feature. Menozzi (2022) argues that Lukács's teleology of labor, developed in *The Young Hegel* and reworked in the *Ontology*, oscillates unresolved between a genuinely materialist, Blochian "anticipatory" openness and a residual, closed determinism inherited from the Hegelian dialectic Lukács otherwise criticizes. Jay (2018) presses the same problem at the level of narrative form, arguing that HCC presupposes history as a unified meta-narrative with a single destined subject, and asking whether Badiou's and Žižek's non-teleological "fidelity to the Event" can salvage the revolutionary charge of HCC without this apparatus — concluding that later substitutes trade one figural device for another rather than escaping teleology as such.

**Section Summary**: Totality, the identical subject-object, imputed consciousness, and praxis form a systematic alternative to the contemplative stance, but each doctrine leaves the subject-object antinomy relocated rather than resolved — as unresolved Hegelian residue (López), as party authority substituting for proletarian self-activity (Fracchia), as an unstable nature/society dialectical boundary (Burkett), and as an oscillating teleology (Menozzi, Jay) — questions that structure the sources debate (Section 4) and the late *Ontology* (Section 5).

## Section 4: Sources: Hegel's Dialectic, Marx's Method, and the "Materialization" Problem

Lukács's constructive theory of history rests on a genealogical claim: that HCC and his later work "materialize" Hegel's dialectic by recovering, through Marx, the rational kernel latent within Hegel's idealist categories of totality, mediation, and subject-object identity. Whether this materialization succeeds, or instead re-imports idealism under a materialist name, divides the secondary literature into two camps. A second, historically specific puzzle compounds the question: HCC (1923) anticipates themes from Marx's 1844 Manuscripts, published nine years later, forcing scholars to ask whether Lukács's reification and Marx's alienation are the same category reached independently or two distinct diagnoses that merely resemble one another.

### 4.1 The Hegelianization thesis vs. legitimate reconstruction

Rockmore's studies (Rockmore 1980, 1992a, 2023) argue that Lukács's Marxism is not a neutral recovery of Marx's own texts but a philosophically overdetermined construction transmitted via Fichte's subject-object identity philosophy and Emil Lask's neo-Kantian theory of categorial forms (Rockmore 1980; Rockmore 1992a). On this account, the identical subject-object of HCC owes its structure less to Hegel directly than to this Fichtean-Laskian detour, making Lukács's Marxism "Kantian, Fichtean, and neo-Kantian as well" as Hegelian (Rockmore 1992a); Rockmore (2023) extends the thesis comparatively, treating Lukács's synthesis as one contingent national variant of "Hegel's Marx" among several rather than the uniquely correct reconstruction. Against this, Mészáros reconstructs totality and mediation as categories genuinely inherited from Hegel's Logic and Phenomenology and redeployed by Marx himself against positivist Second-International Marxism, treating Lukács's achievement as the recovery rather than the imposition of this apparatus (Mészáros 1972). Day traces a specific structural continuity—freedom as the teleological outcome of necessity—running from Hegel's philosophy of history through Marx's realm-of-freedom/realm-of-necessity distinction into Lukács's theory of class consciousness as historical agency (Day 1989), while Norman and Sayers stage the underlying methodological dispute directly, with Sayers defending continuity between Hegel's logic and Marx's method in *Capital* and Norman insisting on a decisive materialist break (Norman and Sayers 1980). Two further studies locate specific Hegelian transmission points inside HCC itself: Potamias identifies the Kojève-mediated master-slave dialectic, alongside totality, as an underemphasized input shaping reification, praxis, and proletarian self-consciousness (Potamias 2024), and Jütten documents Lukács's explicit claim that overcoming reification realizes the identity-philosophies of Fichte and Hegel, defending this claim's continued philosophical plausibility as a diagnosis of the erosion of freedom through commodification and juridification (Jütten 2011).

### 4.2 *The Young Hegel* and Marx's method in *Capital*

Lukács's own *The Young Hegel*, composed in Moscow and defended as a doctoral thesis in 1942, argues across four phases of Hegel's development that the dialectic emerged from sustained engagement with English and Scottish political economy rather than from speculative theology alone—the direct textual basis for his materialization claim (Lukács 1975). Arthur's "new dialectic" reading of *Capital* as a systematic-dialectical exposition modeled on the categorial movement of Hegel's *Science of Logic* supplies a benchmark of methodological rigor against which Lukács's earlier and comparatively unsystematic invocation of totality and mediation can be measured (Arthur 2004). Finelli complicates any clean materialization narrative from the other direction, arguing that even the young Marx's own 1843–44 break from Hegel reproduced a Hegelian logic of subjectivity rather than achieving the decisive rupture it claimed—a "failed parricide" that, if correct, limits how fully any later reader, including Lukács, could complete what Marx himself left unfinished (Finelli 2016). Maidansky's account of the fluctuating Soviet reception of Hegel situates *The Young Hegel*'s composition and defense within specific, shifting ideological constraints on Hegel scholarship, context bearing on how the book's economic-materialist reading of Hegel should be read (Maidansky 2025).

### 4.3 The 1844-Manuscripts puzzle: alienation vs. reification

Márkus argues that Marx's alienation—rooted in a Feuerbachian-humanist account of species-being—and Lukács's reification—derived from commodity fetishism read through Weber and Simmel—are conceptually distinct despite their frequent conflation in the humanist-Marxism literature, cutting against readings of HCC as an unwitting restatement of the Manuscripts (Márkus 1982). Kain instead treats Lukács's 1923 anticipation as a genuine convergence, describing HCC as the first work to "discover" the humanist Marx later vindicated by the Manuscripts' 1932 publication (Kain 1986); Sayers's account of the Hegelian content of Marx's own alienation—continuous with Hegel's theory of self-realization through externalization—provides a further benchmark for testing whether reification captures the same inheritance (Sayers 2011). The dialectics-of-nature dispute forms a related fault line: Azeri defends Engels against the HCC-era charge that his dialectics of nature rests on a non-philosophical misreading of the Kantian thing-in-itself, arguing Engels' account of praxis anticipates later "human activity" theories (Azeri 2022). Ninos extends the Hegel-Lukács relation into the late *Ontology*, arguing that Lukács's analysis of labor as teleological positing is shaped by his reading of the doctrine of essence in Hegel's *Logic*, and identifying unresolved methodological tensions in that appropriation—a connection that bridges directly into Section 5's treatment of the *Ontology* (Ninos 2025). Whether Lukács's philosophy of history counts as authentically materialist, rather than idealism relocated, turns substantially on how these source questions are resolved.

The legitimacy of Lukács's Hegel-Marx synthesis remains genuinely contested between the Hegelianization and reconstructionist camps; the distinction between alienation and reification, by contrast, is broadly—though not universally—accepted, with Kain's convergence reading standing as the principal dissent from Márkus and Sayers.

## Section 5: The Late *Ontology of Social Being* and the Continuity Question

Lukács's unfinished last project, *Zur Ontologie des gesellschaftlichen Seins* (written in the 1960s, published posthumously from 1971), relocates the philosophy of history from the terrain of proletarian self-consciousness onto a more basic terrain: the structure of labor itself. Where HCC had sought to overcome the subject-object antinomy through the revolutionary class's practical self-recognition as "identical subject-object of history," the *Ontology* argues that every act of labor already contains, in miniature, a resolution of the antinomy between teleology and causality, and proposes to build outward from this "model" (*Modell*) to explain the genesis of value, ideology, alienation, and historical development generally (Lukács 1980). This section asks whether that relocation resolves, merely displaces, or altogether abandons the subject-object problem tracked through Sections 1 and 3, and finds the secondary literature sharply divided on the answer.

### 5.1 The labor/teleology/causality model

The *Ontology*'s central claim is that labor is neither purely teleological, as idealism holds, nor purely causal, as mechanistic materialism holds, but a distinctive third structure: an act of positing a goal (teleological positing, *teleologische Setzung*) that can be realized only by manipulating an objectively existing causal nexus (Lukács 1970; Lukács 1980). Lukács develops the Hegelian point of departure for this model in the first *Ontology* volume, distinguishing a "false," idealist ontology — in which categories such as teleology are illegitimately extended across nature and history as a whole — from a "genuine" ontology he finds latent in Hegel's *Logic* and proposes to extract and rework materialistically (Lukács 1978a). The second volume then reconstructs Marx's method itself as an implicit social ontology centered on historicity and the priority of material reproduction (Lukács 1978b). Correa Castañeda's recent reconstruction of the full categorial architecture makes explicit a tension already latent within the text: between a "historical" first part tracing the genesis of labor, ideology, and alienation, and a more "systematic" second part, and situates Lukács's categories against the rival ontological systems of Hartmann, Hegel, Marx, and existentialism (Correa Castañeda 2024). This historical/systematic split becomes, in the following subsection, the seam along which readers divide over whether the *Ontology* continues or breaks with HCC.

### 5.2 Rupture vs. fulfillment: the Budapest School and its rebuttals

The reception of the *Ontology* was shaped decisively by an internal critique from Lukács's own students. Fehér, Heller, Márkus, and Vajda — the "Budapest School" — charged in 1976 that the unfinished manuscript oscillates between two incompatible projects: a genuinely dialectical, historically specific "ontology of social being" and a residual, quasi-Engelsian "general ontology" that illegitimately extends causality and teleology across nature, biology, and society as a single evolutionary continuum, reproducing precisely the problems of orthodox Soviet dialectical materialism that HCC's emphasis on historical specificity had been meant to avoid (Fehér et al. 1976). Tosel's account of the same episode goes further, arguing that Lukács does not merely risk incoherence but explicitly *abandons* the subject-object dialectic of HCC — proletarian class consciousness overcoming the bourgeois separation of subject and object through the teleology of history — replacing it with the labor-based social ontology as a new theoretical basis for a democratic revival of socialism after 1956 (Tosel 2008). On this reading the *Ontology* does not resolve or relocate the antinomy diagnosed in Section 2; it drops the categorial apparatus (totality-as-revolutionary-standpoint, imputed consciousness) that had been meant to resolve it.

Against this, a "fulfillment/deepening" position denies that the tension amounts to rupture. Tertulian, across several essays, presents the *Ontology* as Lukács's most philosophically serious and original achievement rather than a Stalinist-era decline, situating it within twentieth-century ontology alongside Heidegger and Nicolai Hartmann and arguing that Lukács's genetic, dialectical method — grounded in labor and the causal nexus — offers a materialist alternative to Heideggerian hermeneutic ontology and its treatment of thrownness (*Geworfenheit*) (Tertulian 1988; Tertulian 2009). Tertulian further argues that alienation — HCC's signature theme — receives a distinct and arguably more developed treatment in the *Ontology* and in the *Aesthetics*, in critical dialogue with Sartre and Heidegger, which complicates any simple rupture narrative between the two texts (Tertulian 2006). Joós's early monograph frames the *Ontology* not as an externally imposed political retraction but as Lukács's own considered "autocriticism" of HCC's identical-subject-object claim and early messianic utopianism (Joós 1983; Joós 1977). Duayer and Medeiros extend the fulfillment reading comparatively, arguing that the *Ontology* is an indispensable contribution to social ontology in general, in productive convergence with the Bhaskarian critical-realist tradition (Duayer and Medeiros 2005); Browne similarly defends the internal coherence of the labor-teleology-causality model against charges that it fails to escape the antinomies it diagnoses (Browne 1990). A cross-domain rebuttal bears directly on this dispute: Forczek argues that the Budapest School's "two ontologies" charge is misleading, and that the apparent tension dissolves once Lukács's thesis of the "post festum-rationality" of history — that historical rationality becomes visible only retrospectively — is properly understood, so that the "how many Lukácses" problem recurs even inside a single late, unfinished text (Forczek 2023). Where Tosel and the Budapest School read rupture and Tertulian, Joós, and Forczek read fulfillment or defensible unity, both sides agree that the site of the dispute is precisely the fate of HCC's subject-object identity claim — confirming that the antinomy motif from Sections 1 through 3 does not disappear in the late work so much as migrate into a dispute over how to read it.

### 5.3 Contemporary reconstruction and its limits

A more recent, largely English-language wave treats the *Ontology*'s categorial apparatus as extractable from the continuity debate altogether. Thompson's 2020 edited collection reassembles the labor-teleology model as a resource for contemporary critical social ontology, and a related article traces the model's German Idealist roots — self-creation and social relatedness — as themes that could reorient critical theory away from post-metaphysical and neo-Idealist currents (Thompson 2020; Thompson 2024). Alzu's tracking of the totality concept across Lukács's career supplies an internal-development account of why the HCC-era totality — bound up with proletarian practice as ineluctably alienated — needed revision by the time of the *Ontology*, concluding that the later concept enables a non-dogmatic emancipatory practice distinct from party-centered dogmatism (Alzu 2020). Fuchs and Blanton extend the categorial apparatus further still, applying teleological positing to digital and cultural labor and to a labor-grounded theory of judgment not dependent on a unified knowing subject, respectively (Fuchs 2015; Blanton 2020); Vaisman and Fortes trace the political categories embedded in Lukács's ostensibly general late ontology (Vaisman and Fortes 2014); and Szécsi situates the *Ontology*'s realism against neopositivist rejections of ontology (Szécsi 2015). Against this reconstructive current, Dorahy's review of Thompson's volume concludes that whatever social ontology's promise for critical theory generally, the later Lukács represents an "untenable and antiquated" theoretical resource, citing specific barriers rooted in his paradigm of labor and philosophy of history (Dorahy 2023). A parallel, Hegel-focused verdict reinforces this skepticism: Ninos's critical reappraisal of Lukács's use of Hegel's *Logic* — specifically the doctrine of essence — in the labor-teleology analysis finds persistent methodological inconsistencies and the absence of the more dynamic, speculative-dialectical presentation that a fuller engagement with Hegel might have supplied, suggesting that the Hegelian sources mobilized in the 1920s–30s remain active and unresolved rather than superseded in the final work (Ninos 2025).

**Section Summary**: Whether the *Ontology* dissolves or displaces the HCC subject-object problem depends on the interpreter. The Budapest School and Tosel read outright abandonment of the identical-subject-object thesis; Tertulian, Joós, Browne, Duayer and Medeiros, and Forczek read fulfillment or defensible unity; and the contemporary reconstructive current (Thompson, Alzu, Fuchs, Blanton) treats the labor-teleology model as usable independently of that dispute, against Dorahy's and Ninos's judgment that the model remains untenable or internally inconsistent on its own Hegelian terms. What the debate confirms is that the antinomy first diagnosed in *Theory of the Novel* and reworked in HCC is not so much answered by the *Ontology* as relocated once more — from the collective subject's self-consciousness to the individual labor-act's teleological positing — leaving open, at the very endpoint of Lukács's career, the same question of resolution, relocation, or dissolution that structures the review as a whole. Anglophone access to the full argument remains partial, since the *Prolegomena* — the introductory volume distinct from the three translated substantive volumes — has no complete English translation, a gap noted independently by Fuchs.

## Section 6: The Critical Front: Structuralist, Phenomenological, and Analytic Objections

Where Sections 2–5 traced internal tensions within Lukács's own project, this section turns to three external critical traditions that converge, from strikingly different starting points, on the same target: the category of totality and the identical subject-object of history. The structuralist, phenomenological, and analytic-Marxist objections rarely cite one another, and only the first two explicitly engage Lukács by name, yet each concludes that the Lukácsian attempt to ground historical knowledge in the standpoint of a privileged subject is either theoretically illegitimate or practically unsustainable.

### 6.1 Althusser and structuralist anti-humanism

Althusser's *For Marx* diagnoses "historicism" — the reduction of a social totality to the "internal essence" of an epoch, knowable through a subject's lived experience — and "expressive totality" — a whole whose parts merely express one central essence — as the defining errors of Hegelian Marxism (Althusser 1969). Although Lukács is not named, later commentary treats *History and Class Consciousness*'s category of totality and its identical subject-object as the paradigm case of both errors. Against this, *Reading Capital* (Althusser and Balibar 2016) proposes "structural causality": a decentered structure in which the economic determines "in the last instance" which instance is dominant, with no instance, and no historical subject, functioning as the whole's expressive ground — a "process without a subject." This alternative supplies the standard against which the charge of expressive totality must be measured, and it raises a reflexive difficulty that Callinicos (1976) and Elliott (1987) each press: does Althusser's own "determination in the last instance" avoid collapsing back into the expressive totality he condemns, and is the reading of Lukács itself fair, given that Lukács is never Althusser's direct interlocutor? Anderson (1976) situates this dispute historically, identifying HCC as the inaugural text of "Western Marxism" and tracing the tradition's later structuralist reaction against its own Hegelian, subject-centered wing; his subsequent reassessment of the Thompson–Althusser debate over agency and structure (Anderson 1980) extends the same humanism/structuralism dispute into British historiography on more empirical grounds.

### 6.2 Merleau-Ponty and the impasse of Western Marxism

Merleau-Ponty's chapter "'Western' Marxism" in *Adventures of the Dialectic* offers an internal rather than external diagnosis: HCC is read as the most rigorous attempt to preserve a dialectical Marxism in which truth is internal to a historical perspective — the proletariat's — rather than external and "objectivist" (Merleau-Ponty 1973). He argues this position is structurally unstable, since the proletariat's "true" consciousness must be certified by something external to workers' actual, empirical consciousness — the Party — so that the dialectic collapses either into an ungrounded wager on revolutionary spontaneity or into a dogmatic objectivism in which the Party's verdict simply replaces experience. Merleau-Ponty takes Bolshevism-Stalinism to have realized the second, degenerating outcome. Flynn (2007) traces how Merleau-Ponty's qualified sympathy for the proletariat as "universal class" progressively dissolves across these texts as he comes to doubt any social group can occupy the epistemically privileged position Lukács's theory requires, while Rockmore (1996) reconstructs Merleau-Ponty's insistence that the identical-subject-object thesis and imputed consciousness be judged against whether revolutionary practice actually vindicated them — a test he concludes history failed. Kruks (1988) supplies a skeptical check on this declinist frame, questioning whether the "tale of woe" reading shared by Anderson and Merleau-Ponty accurately captures the tradition's philosophical achievements or merely registers its distance from an orthodox model of proletarian practice.

### 6.3 Analytic Marxism and holistic/teleological explanation

A third front, developed independently of Lukács, concerns method rather than politics. Elster (1985) rejects functional and teleological explanation — historical development "requiring" certain relations of production, productive forces "needing" to develop — as illegitimate unless reducible to individualist, rational-choice micro-foundations. Cohen (1979, 1982) defends functional explanation as legitimate when an "elaborating" causal mechanism can be specified, even without full micro-foundations, while Wood (1986) argues that Elster's critique caricatures both functional explanation and the kind of teleological claims Marx actually makes. Elster and Cohen do not discuss Lukács directly; applying this debate to imputed class consciousness and totality-as-standpoint is therefore a construction performed in this synthesis, not a critique found in the sources. So construed, the question is whether Lukács's holistic categories — a class "standpoint" that explains historical direction without specifying individual mechanisms — can meet Elster's individualist bar or instead require the kind of non-reductive defense Cohen and Wood offer for historical materialism generally.

### 6.4 A pivot figure

Postone (2009) evaluates both critiques together, arguing that Lukács's identification of the proletariat as identical subject-object, whatever its interpretive power over Hegel, remains bound within a "traditional" Marxism affirming rather than critiquing labor and the standpoint of totality — but that the structuralist rejection of totality and the historical Subject as inherently dominating discards Marx's own critical appropriation of Hegel along with Lukács's. Neither position, on this reading, is adequate, which transitions the review into Section 7's account of later reconstruction.

Across these three fronts, the subject-object problem reappears from directions that share little else: Althusser's charge targets totality's logical form, Merleau-Ponty's targets its practical certification, and the analytic-Marxist debate targets its explanatory legitimacy. Yet each critique carries its own unresolved difficulty — Althusser's last instance, the constructed nature of the Elster application, Kruks's challenge to declinism — suggesting the critical front is convergent but not uniform.

## Section 7: Inheritance and Transformation: Frankfurt School, Western Marxism, and Later Reception

Lukács's philosophy of history did not end with the *Ontology*; it was absorbed, contested, and redirected by successors who treated reification, totality, and the subject-object problem as an inheritance to rework rather than a system to adopt whole. This section traces that afterlife along three axes: the Frankfurt School's simultaneous retention and rejection of Lukács's categories, the comparative test case of "Western Marxism" as a shared position, and later reconstructions and critiques that carry the labor-based philosophy of history into contemporary debate.

### 7.1 Negative-dialectical inheritance (Adorno) and recognition-theoretic transformation (Honneth)

Adorno's *Negative Dialectics* retains Lukács's diagnosis of reification as the pathology of bourgeois society while rejecting the "identical subject-object" as its cure: all identity-thinking is a form of domination, so any claimed reconciliation of subject and object is itself ideological, and totality survives only as a negative, diagnostic horizon rather than an achieved standpoint (Adorno 1973). The *History and Freedom* lectures work the same conclusion through Kant's and Hegel's philosophies of history, proposing a "natural-historical" alternative in which freedom appears only fragmentarily against a reified, administered totality (Adorno 2006). Bueno (2024) specifies the relationship more precisely: Lukács's proletarian subject-object identity and Adorno's diagnosis of the "end of psychology" in the authoritarian masses converge, through opposite routes, on the elimination of the subject-object distinction, producing a shared political impasse; Bueno reconstructs both thinkers to recover a reading in which emancipatory subjectivity is driven by, rather than opposed to, the material limits of reification — Adorno's non-identical.

Honneth's *Reification* pushes the inheritance further by "de-ontologizing" the concept into a "forgetfulness of recognition" — a suspension of a prior existential recognition of self and other — detached from Lukács's anchoring in the commodity form and generalized beyond capitalism as such (Honneth 2008). Foster (2011) and Kavoulakos (2018b) respond that this move re-reifies reification itself, trading Lukács's structural critique of capitalist social form for a quasi-anthropological claim that loses critical purchase on its own object. Kavoulakos (2017) compares Feenberg's and Honneth's rival reconstructions, arguing Feenberg's practice-centered reading — treated as a primary position in Sections 2 and 3 — better preserves Lukács's own intentions. Feenberg's programmatic claim that Frankfurt School categories are unintelligible without HCC as their point of departure supplies the thesis this material substantiates (Feenberg 2017).

### 7.2 Comparative Western Marxism: Korsch and Gramsci

Korsch's *Marxism and Philosophy*, published the same year as HCC, argues in close parallel that Second International Marxism wrongly treated theory as a neutral science standing outside class struggle, when Marxist theory is itself a historically specific, partisan moment continuous with the Hegelian dialectic (Korsch 1970). Devlin (2021) cautions against reading Korsch as a mere philosophical twin of Lukács: recontextualizing the text within interwar labor-movement politics, he shows Korsch's historicization of Marx's own writing produced a tension distinct from Lukács's ontological concerns, oriented more toward the sociology of Marxist theory than toward HCC's epistemological questions. Skidmore-Hess (2022), marking the text's centenary, affirms the continuing relevance of Korsch's historicist thesis alongside Lukács's parallel project. Gramsci's *Prison Notebooks*, composed independently and largely without access to HCC, develop hegemony, the philosophy of praxis, and the organic intellectual as tools oriented toward political strategy and cultural leadership rather than the epistemological and ontological questions central to Lukács (Gramsci 1971). Read together, Korsch and Gramsci — canonically grouped with Lukács as founders of Western Marxism — supply the comparative test of whether that label names one position or a family resemblance; Devlin's recontextualization suggests the latter.

### 7.3 Later reconstruction and critique: Goldmann, Jameson, Postone

Goldmann argues that Heidegger's *Being and Time* is deeply, if unacknowledged, indebted to HCC's diagnosis of reified, inauthentic existence, using the rapprochement to stake out his own genetic-structuralist departure from both Lukácsian totality and Heideggerian ontology (Goldmann 1977). Lotz (2021) argues a more adequate comparison should ground itself in Lukács's and Heidegger's respective appropriations of the concept of categories rather than in Goldmann's account of everyday, ready-to-hand dealings, so that Goldmann's framework both illuminates and distorts the philosophical stakes. Jameson's *Marxism and Form* transmits Lukácsian totality and mediation into Anglophone literary theory against then-dominant formalism (Jameson 1971), while *The Antinomies of Realism* reworks the realism/totality problem by locating an unresolved antinomy — between scenic *récit* and the totalizing machinery of plot — inside realism itself, revising rather than simply applying Lukács's earlier realism/modernism opposition (Jameson 2013). Schubertová (2025) traces four reconfigurations of the realism/utopia relationship across both careers, reading Jameson's apparent departure from the Lukácsian model as continuity rather than rupture; Homer (1998) supplies a synoptic account of how Lukácsian categories are transmitted through and transformed within Jameson's oeuvre.

Postone's *Time, Labor, and Social Domination* mounts what its own reception (Jay 2020) describes as the most thorough critique of Lukács's constructive philosophy of history: "traditional Marxism," including Lukács's, mistakes capitalism's historically specific abstract domination — the duality of concrete and abstract labor, value as reified labor — for a transhistorical struggle between labor and its alienated products, and so cannot ground a critique of value and abstract labor themselves (Postone 1993). Postone and Brick (1982) trace this diagnosis earlier to the pessimistic turn of Horkheimer and Adorno's *Dialectic of Enlightenment*, itself a consequence of retaining a traditional-Marxist standpoint of labor after historical developments undermined its plausibility. Jay (2020) restates the argument sympathetically, while Devyver (2025) situates Postone's critique within, rather than simply against, the Frankfurt School's negative-dialectical tradition, reading his account of abstract domination alongside Adorno's critique of identity-thinking — closing a triangulation among Postone, Adorno, and Lukács running through this section.

Across these three axes, Lukács's philosophy of history survives largely as a *problematic* that others extend, invert, or reject rather than as a settled doctrine: Adorno and Honneth retain reification while dismantling the identical subject-object in different ways; Korsch and Gramsci show that "Western Marxism" names, at most, a family resemblance rather than one shared position; and Goldmann, Jameson, and Postone each redeploy Lukácsian categories toward ends — existentialist ontology, literary interpretation, the critique of abstract labor — that measure how far the original framework can travel. Reification proves more durable across this reception than the subject-object identity it was meant to secure.

## Conclusion

Across the seven sections of this review, one asymmetry recurs: Lukács's negative diagnosis of reification has proven more durable than the constructive apparatus built to overcome it. Reification's core insight — that capitalist social form imposes a "contemplative" stance on both everyday consciousness and classical German philosophy — is extended by Adorno, transformed by Honneth, and defended in its capitalism-specific form by Feenberg and Kavoulakos against Honneth's de-ontologizing generalization (Honneth 2008; Feenberg 2014). By contrast, the constructive doctrines meant to resolve reification — the identical subject-object, imputed class consciousness, and historical teleology — are the literature's persistent target, criticized from within (López, Jaffe, Menozzi) and from without (Althusser, Merleau-Ponty, analytic Marxism, Postone).

The central synthetic question this review has tracked section by section — whether the subject-object antinomy is resolved, relocated, or dissolved — admits, on the evidence assembled here, a single descriptive answer: displacement rather than resolution. *Theory of the Novel*'s "transcendental homelessness" already names the antinomy; HCC relocates it onto the proletariat's practical self-recognition as "identical subject-object of history"; and the *Ontology* relocates it again, from collective class consciousness to the individual labor-act's teleological positing within a causal nexus (Section 5). At no point does the secondary literature converge on a text where the antinomy is simply solved. This is precisely why continuity theorists (Löwy, Arató and Breines) and rupture theorists (the Budapest School, Tosel) can each marshal genuine textual support: both are tracking the same recurring problem under different names at different stages, and each stage supplies enough apparent novelty to support a rupture reading and enough persistence of the underlying question to support a continuity reading. Jay's (2018) question — whether HCC's singular-subject historical narrative can survive without an identifiable subject to replace the proletariat — sharpens the point: post-Lukácsian substitutes (Badiou's and Žižek's "Event") relocate the problem again rather than dispensing with it.

Four fault lines recur across every section examined here and organize the debate independently of any single text: the status of a dialectics of nature (Section 3.4, Section 5.2); party authority versus proletarian self-activity in securing imputed consciousness (Section 3.3–3.4); the open-versus-closed structure of historical teleology (Section 3.5, Section 5.1); and the genealogical question of how far Lukács's categories are Hegelian, neo-Kantian, or phenomenological in origin (Section 2.2, Section 4.1).

These fault lines mark the review's synthesis gap: existing scholarship has not produced a unified assessment, across the full young-to-late arc, of whether the teleology/subject-object problem is genuinely dissolved by the *Ontology*'s labor-model or merely displaced onto it once more, nor has it settled whether reification can retain critical force once detached from the Hegelian-teleological apparatus in which Lukács embedded it — the live disagreement between Thompson's and Ivanova's detachability projects and Honneth's, Kavoulakos's, and Feenberg's insistence on capitalism-specific anchoring (Dorahy 2023). Several areas remain comparatively under-researched in the Anglophone literature surveyed here: the untranslated *Prolegomena* and Moscow-period writings; Rickert and Windelband as direct rather than inferred targets of the reification critique; the early essay "Towards a Methodology of the Problem of Organisation"; and the Benjamin–Lukács and early Horkheimer–Lukács relations, each gestured at but not systematically treated across the domains surveyed.
