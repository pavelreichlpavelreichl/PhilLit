## Section 5: The Late *Ontology of Social Being* and the Continuity Question

Lukács's unfinished last project, *Zur Ontologie des gesellschaftlichen Seins* (written in the 1960s, published posthumously from 1971), relocates the philosophy of history from the terrain of proletarian self-consciousness onto a more basic terrain: the structure of labor itself. Where HCC had sought to overcome the subject-object antinomy through the revolutionary class's practical self-recognition as "identical subject-object of history," the *Ontology* argues that every act of labor already contains, in miniature, a resolution of the antinomy between teleology and causality, and proposes to build outward from this "model" (*Modell*) to explain the genesis of value, ideology, alienation, and historical development generally (Lukács 1980). This section asks whether that relocation resolves, merely displaces, or altogether abandons the subject-object problem tracked through Sections 1 and 3, and finds the secondary literature sharply divided on the answer.

### 5.1 The labor/teleology/causality model

The *Ontology*'s central claim is that labor is neither purely teleological, as idealism holds, nor purely causal, as mechanistic materialism holds, but a distinctive third structure: an act of positing a goal (teleological positing, *teleologische Setzung*) that can be realized only by manipulating an objectively existing causal nexus (Lukács 1970; Lukács 1980). Lukács develops the Hegelian point of departure for this model in the first *Ontology* volume, distinguishing a "false," idealist ontology — in which categories such as teleology are illegitimately extended across nature and history as a whole — from a "genuine" ontology he finds latent in Hegel's *Logic* and proposes to extract and rework materialistically (Lukács 1978a). The second volume then reconstructs Marx's method itself as an implicit social ontology centered on historicity and the priority of material reproduction (Lukács 1978b). Correa Castañeda's recent reconstruction of the full categorial architecture makes explicit a tension already latent within the text: between a "historical" first part tracing the genesis of labor, ideology, and alienation, and a more "systematic" second part, and situates Lukács's categories against the rival ontological systems of Hartmann, Hegel, Marx, and existentialism (Correa Castañeda 2024). This historical/systematic split becomes, in the following subsection, the seam along which readers divide over whether the *Ontology* continues or breaks with HCC.

### 5.2 Rupture vs. fulfillment — the Budapest School and its rebuttals

The reception of the *Ontology* was shaped decisively by an internal critique from Lukács's own students. Fehér, Heller, Márkus, and Vajda — the "Budapest School" — charged in 1976 that the unfinished manuscript oscillates between two incompatible projects: a genuinely dialectical, historically specific "ontology of social being" and a residual, quasi-Engelsian "general ontology" that illegitimately extends causality and teleology across nature, biology, and society as a single evolutionary continuum, reproducing precisely the problems of orthodox Soviet dialectical materialism that HCC's emphasis on historical specificity had been meant to avoid (Fehér et al. 1976). Tosel's account of the same episode goes further, arguing that Lukács does not merely risk incoherence but explicitly *abandons* the subject-object dialectic of HCC — proletarian class consciousness overcoming the bourgeois separation of subject and object through the teleology of history — replacing it with the labor-based social ontology as a new theoretical basis for a democratic revival of socialism after 1956 (Tosel 2008). On this reading the *Ontology* does not resolve or relocate the antinomy diagnosed in Section 2; it drops the categorial apparatus (totality-as-revolutionary-standpoint, imputed consciousness) that had been meant to resolve it.

Against this, a "fulfillment/deepening" position denies that the tension amounts to rupture. Tertulian, across several essays, presents the *Ontology* as Lukács's most philosophically serious and original achievement rather than a Stalinist-era decline, situating it within twentieth-century ontology alongside Heidegger and Nicolai Hartmann and arguing that Lukács's genetic, dialectical method — grounded in labor and the causal nexus — offers a materialist alternative to Heideggerian hermeneutic ontology and its treatment of thrownness (*Geworfenheit*) (Tertulian 1988; Tertulian 2009). Tertulian further argues that alienation — HCC's signature theme — receives a distinct and arguably more developed treatment in the *Ontology* and in the *Aesthetics*, in critical dialogue with Sartre and Heidegger, which complicates any simple rupture narrative between the two texts (Tertulian 2006). Joós's early monograph frames the *Ontology* not as an externally imposed political retraction but as Lukács's own considered "autocriticism" of HCC's identical-subject-object claim and early messianic utopianism (Joós 1983; Joós 1977). Duayer and Medeiros extend the fulfillment reading comparatively, arguing that the *Ontology* is an indispensable contribution to social ontology in general, in productive convergence with the Bhaskarian critical-realist tradition (Duayer and Medeiros 2005); Browne similarly defends the internal coherence of the labor-teleology-causality model against charges that it fails to escape the antinomies it diagnoses (Browne 1990). A cross-domain rebuttal bears directly on this dispute: Forczek argues that the Budapest School's "two ontologies" charge is misleading, and that the apparent tension dissolves once Lukács's thesis of the "post festum-rationality" of history — that historical rationality becomes visible only retrospectively — is properly understood, so that the "how many Lukácses" problem recurs even inside a single late, unfinished text (Forczek 2023). Where Tosel and the Budapest School read rupture and Tertulian, Joós, and Forczek read fulfillment or defensible unity, both sides agree that the site of the dispute is precisely the fate of HCC's subject-object identity claim — confirming that the antinomy motif from Sections 1 through 3 does not disappear in the late work so much as migrate into a dispute over how to read it.

### 5.3 Contemporary reconstruction and its limits

A more recent, largely English-language wave treats the *Ontology*'s categorial apparatus as extractable from the continuity debate altogether. Thompson's 2020 edited collection reassembles the labor-teleology model as a resource for contemporary critical social ontology, and a related article traces the model's German Idealist roots — self-creation and social relatedness — as themes that could reorient critical theory away from post-metaphysical and neo-Idealist currents (Thompson 2020; Thompson 2024). Alzu's tracking of the totality concept across Lukács's career supplies an internal-development account of why the HCC-era totality — bound up with proletarian practice as ineluctably alienated — needed revision by the time of the *Ontology*, concluding that the later concept enables a non-dogmatic emancipatory practice distinct from party-centered dogmatism (Alzu 2020). Fuchs and Blanton extend the categorial apparatus further still, applying teleological positing to digital and cultural labor and to a labor-grounded theory of judgment not dependent on a unified knowing subject, respectively (Fuchs 2015; Blanton 2020); Vaisman and Fortes trace the political categories embedded in Lukács's ostensibly general late ontology (Vaisman and Fortes 2014); and Szécsi situates the *Ontology*'s realism against neopositivist rejections of ontology (Szécsi 2015). Against this reconstructive current, Dorahy's review of Thompson's volume concludes that whatever social ontology's promise for critical theory generally, the later Lukács represents an "untenable and antiquated" theoretical resource, citing specific barriers rooted in his paradigm of labor and philosophy of history (Dorahy 2023). A parallel, Hegel-focused verdict reinforces this skepticism: Ninos's critical reappraisal of Lukács's use of Hegel's *Logic* — specifically the doctrine of essence — in the labor-teleology analysis finds persistent methodological inconsistencies and the absence of the more dynamic, speculative-dialectical presentation that a fuller engagement with Hegel might have supplied, suggesting that the Hegelian sources mobilized in the 1920s–30s remain active and unresolved rather than superseded in the final work (Ninos 2025).

**Section Summary**: Whether the *Ontology* dissolves or displaces the HCC subject-object problem depends on the interpreter. The Budapest School and Tosel read outright abandonment of the identical-subject-object thesis; Tertulian, Joós, Browne, Duayer and Medeiros, and Forczek read fulfillment or defensible unity; and the contemporary reconstructive current (Thompson, Alzu, Fuchs, Blanton) treats the labor-teleology model as usable independently of that dispute, against Dorahy's and Ninos's judgment that the model remains untenable or internally inconsistent on its own Hegelian terms. What the debate confirms is that the antinomy first diagnosed in *Theory of the Novel* and reworked in HCC is not so much answered by the *Ontology* as relocated once more — from the collective subject's self-consciousness to the individual labor-act's teleological positing — leaving open, at the very endpoint of Lukács's career, the same question of resolution, relocation, or dissolution that structures the review as a whole. Anglophone access to the full argument remains partial, since the *Prolegomena* — the introductory volume distinct from the three translated substantive volumes — has no complete English translation, a gap noted independently by Fuchs.
