## Section 4: Sources — Hegel's Dialectic, Marx's Method, and the "Materialization" Problem

Lukács's constructive theory of history rests on a genealogical claim: that HCC and his later work "materialize" Hegel's dialectic by recovering, through Marx, the rational kernel latent within Hegel's idealist categories of totality, mediation, and subject-object identity. Whether this materialization succeeds, or instead re-imports idealism under a materialist name, divides the secondary literature into two camps. A second, historically specific puzzle compounds the question: HCC (1923) anticipates themes from Marx's 1844 Manuscripts, published nine years later, forcing scholars to ask whether Lukács's reification and Marx's alienation are the same category reached independently or two distinct diagnoses that merely resemble one another.

### 4.1 The Hegelianization thesis vs. legitimate reconstruction

Rockmore's studies (1980, 1992, 2023) argue that Lukács's Marxism is not a neutral recovery of Marx's own texts but a philosophically overdetermined construction transmitted via Fichte's subject-object identity philosophy and Emil Lask's neo-Kantian theory of categorial forms (`rockmore1980lukacsclassicalgerman`; `rockmore1992fichtelask`). On this account, the identical subject-object of HCC owes its structure less to Hegel directly than to this Fichtean-Laskian detour, making Lukács's Marxism "Kantian, Fichtean, and neo-Kantian as well" as Hegelian (`rockmore1992fichtelask`); `rockmore2023hegeloriginsmarxism` extends the thesis comparatively, treating Lukács's synthesis as one contingent national variant of "Hegel's Marx" among several rather than the uniquely correct reconstruction. Against this, Mészáros reconstructs totality and mediation as categories genuinely inherited from Hegel's Logic and Phenomenology and redeployed by Marx himself against positivist Second-International Marxism, treating Lukács's achievement as the recovery rather than the imposition of this apparatus (`meszaros1972lukacsdialectic`). Day traces a specific structural continuity—freedom as the teleological outcome of necessity—running from Hegel's philosophy of history through Marx's realm-of-freedom/realm-of-necessity distinction into Lukács's theory of class consciousness as historical agency (`day1989dialecticfreedom`), while Norman and Sayers stage the underlying methodological dispute directly, with Sayers defending continuity between Hegel's logic and Marx's method in *Capital* and Norman insisting on a decisive materialist break (`normansayers1980debate`). Two further studies locate specific Hegelian transmission points inside HCC itself: Potamias identifies the Kojève-mediated master-slave dialectic, alongside totality, as an underemphasized input shaping reification, praxis, and proletarian self-consciousness (`potamias2024masterslave`), and Jütten documents Lukács's explicit claim that overcoming reification realizes the identity-philosophies of Fichte and Hegel, defending this claim's continued philosophical plausibility as a diagnosis of the erosion of freedom through commodification and juridification (`jutten2011verdinglichung`).

### 4.2 *The Young Hegel* and Marx's method in *Capital*

Lukács's own *The Young Hegel*, composed in Moscow and defended as a doctoral thesis in 1942, argues across four phases of Hegel's development that the dialectic emerged from sustained engagement with English and Scottish political economy rather than from speculative theology alone—the direct textual basis for his materialization claim (`lukacs1975younghegel`). Arthur's "new dialectic" reading of *Capital* as a systematic-dialectical exposition modeled on the categorial movement of Hegel's *Science of Logic* supplies a benchmark of methodological rigor against which Lukács's earlier and comparatively unsystematic invocation of totality and mediation can be measured (`arthur2004newdialectic`). Finelli complicates any clean materialization narrative from the other direction, arguing that even the young Marx's own 1843–44 break from Hegel reproduced a Hegelian logic of subjectivity rather than achieving the decisive rupture it claimed—a "failed parricide" that, if correct, limits how fully any later reader, including Lukács, could complete what Marx himself left unfinished (`finelli2016failedparricide`). Maidansky's account of the fluctuating Soviet reception of Hegel situates *The Young Hegel*'s composition and defense within specific, shifting ideological constraints on Hegel scholarship, context bearing on how the book's economic-materialist reading of Hegel should be read (`maidansky2025hegelmirrors`).

### 4.3 The 1844-Manuscripts puzzle — alienation vs. reification

Márkus argues that Marx's alienation—rooted in a Feuerbachian-humanist account of species-being—and Lukács's reification—derived from commodity fetishism read through Weber and Simmel—are conceptually distinct despite their frequent conflation in the humanist-Marxism literature, cutting against readings of HCC as an unwitting restatement of the Manuscripts (`markus1982alienationreification`). Kain instead treats Lukács's 1923 anticipation as a genuine convergence, describing HCC as the first work to "discover" the humanist Marx later vindicated by the Manuscripts' 1932 publication (`kain1986marxmethod`); Sayers's account of the Hegelian content of Marx's own alienation—continuous with Hegel's theory of self-realization through externalization—provides a further benchmark for testing whether reification captures the same inheritance (`sayers2011marxalienation`). The dialectics-of-nature dispute forms a related fault line: Azeri defends Engels against the HCC-era charge that his dialectics of nature rests on a non-philosophical misreading of the Kantian thing-in-itself, arguing Engels' account of praxis anticipates later "human activity" theories (`azeri2022engelsdialectics`). Ninos extends the Hegel-Lukács relation into the late *Ontology*, arguing that Lukács's analysis of labor as teleological positing is shaped by his reading of the doctrine of essence in Hegel's *Logic*, and identifying unresolved methodological tensions in that appropriation—a connection that bridges directly into Section 5's treatment of the *Ontology* (`ninos2025labourteleological`). Whether Lukács's philosophy of history counts as authentically materialist, rather than idealism relocated, turns substantially on how these source questions are resolved.

The legitimacy of Lukács's Hegel-Marx synthesis remains genuinely contested between the Hegelianization and reconstructionist camps; the distinction between alienation and reification, by contrast, is broadly—though not universally—accepted, with Kain's convergence reading standing as the principal dissent from Márkus and Sayers.
