## Section 2: System, Freedom, and the State — Continuity or Rupture?

The question of whether Fichte's philosophy of history coheres with the *Wissenschaftslehre* and his practical philosophy runs along two distinct but related axes. The first concerns systematic grounding: is history already embedded in the early practical *Wissenschaftslehre*, or does it become possible only after the post-1800 turn to the Absolute? The second concerns political development: do the early republican Fichte and the late statist and national Fichte form a coherent trajectory, or does the transition represent a genuine rupture? These axes intersect, but they can come apart: one can hold that the later system is metaphysically discontinuous while maintaining that the political philosophy develops coherently, or vice versa. The section maps the principal positions across both dimensions.

### 2.1 History and the *Wissenschaftslehre* (Jena/Berlin)

The most influential argument for systematic continuity between the early *Wissenschaftslehre* and the philosophy of history proceeds through the concept of "pragmatic history of the mind." Breazeale (2001) traces Fichte's use of this term to a distinctive philosophical method already operative in the Jena period: the philosopher does not report empirical history but constructs the necessary stages of the mind's self-constitution, in a procedure neither purely a priori nor empirically descriptive. In his comprehensive study of the Jena system, Breazeale (2013) shows that the early *Wissenschaftslehre* harbors a genetic-historical structure internally — the finite I's infinite striving (*Streben*) toward an Absolute it can asymptotically approach but never reach generates a temporal teleology before any explicit philosophy of history is formulated. Alessiato (2021) reinforces this by tracing the conceptual arc from the *Tathandlung* through *Anstoss*, *Streben*, and intersubjective *Wechselwirkung* to freedom as the goal of historical action, arguing that the unity of Fichte's system means the transcendental and moral-historical dimensions are internally connected, not externally juxtaposed.

Against this reading, Neumann (2023) argues that the 1804-II *Wissenschaftslehre* cannot plausibly present itself as a continuation and correction of Kant's transcendental philosophy, because its central demand — for an Absolute unity undifferentiated in itself — departs fundamentally from Kant's premises. On Neumann's reading, the turn to the Absolute reflects a shift into a different metaphysical register, making the 1804 system something closer to a form of speculative monism than a development of critical idealism. If Neumann is right, the grounding of history in the later system — as the Absolute's self-manifestation through finite consciousness — rests on premises unavailable to the Jena Fichte.

Bruno (2021) and Nini (2024) occupy an intermediate position that has become increasingly influential. Bruno argues for what might be called methodological continuity alongside doctrinal transformation: both the Jena and Berlin periods deploy a genetic method aimed at deriving necessarily what Kant left as factically given, but in the Berlin period Fichte directs this critique against his own earlier doctrine of intellectual intuition, eliminating residual factical terms. Nini argues along similar lines that the WL-1804-II exhibits structural continuity with the Jena method — it remains a transcendental argument operating through the agreement of what is said and what is enacted — while its fivefold expansion of this structure represents genuine doctrinal innovation rather than abandonment. On this reading, there is neither straightforward continuity nor clean rupture, but a self-radicalizing refinement. Rametta (2026) takes the late synthesis furthest, arguing for a deep structural unity between the *Wissenschaftslehre* and the *Staatslehre* (1813): the multiplicity of *Wissenschaftslehre* presentations reflects the dynamic self-manifestation of the Absolute through consciousness, and the *Staatslehre* reconfigures political sovereignty as the unfolding of transcendental intelligence within a community, replacing theological legitimacy with rational self-determination.

### 2.2 Freedom, Right, and the State as History's Medium

The practical-philosophical basis for the historical teleology rests on the *Grundlage des Naturrechts* (1796/97) and its account of mutual recognition (*Anerkennung*) as a transcendental condition of self-consciousness. Nance (2012) reconstructs Fichte's argument that a finite rational agent can be self-conscious only if another free being has summoned it (*Aufforderung*), establishing intersubjectivity as a condition of the possibility of individual agency rather than its product. Nance identifies what he calls a "modal dilemma": the necessity Fichte derives is either metaphysically too strong or normatively too weak. McNulty (2016) responds by distinguishing the logical from the practical necessity at work in the argument, contending that recognizing others is required not as a bare logical condition but as a condition of the rational activity of self-positing — a form of practical necessity that can sustain normative conclusions about right. Ramsauer (2023) complements this by arguing that Fichte's deduction of the concept of right generates a genuinely non-moral normativity: the obligation to limit one's freedom in light of others' agency arises from the conditions of rational agency itself, independently of the moral law. Together, these readings establish that the intersubjective structure of right in the *Grundlage* already possesses a temporal-developmental dimension — it defines conditions that must be progressively actualized through legal and political institutions.

Arrese Igor (2019) presses the most pointed version of the tension-thesis: the synchronic Jena theory cannot by itself guarantee the stability of a rights-based order. The *Grundlage*'s mechanism of coercive enforcement requires a functioning ephorate to oversee executive power, but the ephorate itself demands civic virtue that the theory's own foundational assumptions — citizens as rational egoists — cannot generate. This inconsistency is not incidental but structural, and Arrese Igor argues that the philosophy of history in the *Grundzüge des gegenwärtigen Zeitalters* (1806) is therefore *required* by the Jena theory rather than merely supplementing it: the diachronic perspective projects the resolution of the ephorate problem into a future epoch in which citizens transcend egoism through rational solidarity. History, on this reading, is not a contingent addition to the system but the medium in which the synchronic theory's unresolved problems find their only possible resolution.

Bradley (2025) characterizes the overall trajectory as an "incomplete republicanism": Fichte commits to the republican principles of collective self-governance and the rule of law but lacks the theoretical resources to show why coercion is a necessary part of right rather than merely a practically useful supplement. James (2011) offers the most sustained continuity reading, arguing that the property theory developed in the *Grundlage* — property as a politically constructed entitlement grounded in intersubjective recognition rather than a pre-political natural right — organizes both the early and the late political philosophy, with the state's transformative role in shaping virtuous citizens already implicit in the Jena framework.

### 2.3 Political Economy and the Closed Commercial State

The *Der geschlossene Handelsstaat* (1800) has been read as either aberrant or exemplary of Fichte's broader project. Nakhimovsky (2011) makes the strongest case for exemplarity: he traces the intellectual genealogy from Rousseau's critique of commercial society through Kant's perpetual peace project to Fichte's argument that economic autarky is the condition under which a republican state can preserve genuine political independence. The closed commercial state is not an eccentric departure but the logical terminus of a tradition that identified asymmetric commercial dependence as a structural threat to republican self-governance. James (2010) reinforces this by showing that Fichte's departure from Kant's cosmopolitan right rests on a specific diagnosis: Kant's hospitality provision fails to recognize that free trade generates indirect forms of coercion through unequal economic dependence, and the closed state is Fichte's solution to this problem within his own theory of right. Nance (2019) connects the political economy directly to the *Grundlage*'s property theory, arguing that Fichte's contractualism commits the state to a planned economy: since every citizen's access to the property required for free agency must be guaranteed, the state is required to mandate the production and transfer of goods rather than leaving this to market mechanisms. Economic planning is thus not an authoritarian imposition but a consequence of the normative foundations of right-based freedom.

The three axes surveyed in this section converge on a common finding. Continuity readings (James, Bruno, Nini, Rametta) and rupture or tension readings (Neumann, Arrese Igor, Bradley) remain genuinely in dispute rather than one side having clearly prevailed. The Berlin turn to the Absolute is the pivot for the systematic version of the dispute; the ephorate/coercion problem is the pivot for the practical-philosophical version. Whether these two axes ultimately coincide — whether the systematic transformation and the political tensions are symptoms of the same underlying difficulty in Fichte's project — is a question the existing literature poses but does not fully answer.
