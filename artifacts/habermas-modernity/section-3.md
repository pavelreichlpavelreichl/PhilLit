## Section 3: Internal Debates and the Poststructuralist Challenge

### 3.1 The Frankfurt School Inheritance — Adorno, Marcuse, and Honneth

The communicative turn that defines Habermas's mature project cannot be understood apart from its founding polemical gesture: the diagnosis that the first generation of Frankfurt School critical theory had trapped itself in a performative contradiction. In "The Entwinement of Myth and Enlightenment," Habermas (1982) accepts the central thesis of Horkheimer and Adorno's *Dialectic of Enlightenment* — that the demythologizing drive of Enlightenment reason reproduces the very domination it sought to overcome — but contests its theoretical conclusion. By collapsing all rationality into the calculation of means, Horkheimer and Adorno (1972) eliminated the normative standpoint from which their own critique derived its force. The critique of reason deployed reason; the condemnation of totalizing thought was itself a totalizing claim. Habermas's alternative is to rescue rationality from this self-undermining dynamic by disaggregating it: communicative reason, embedded in the pragmatic presuppositions of ordinary speech, provides the normative resources that instrumental reason cannot supply. This is not merely a methodological revision but a fundamental reconception of what critical theory is for.

The secondary literature has, however, challenged both the diagnosis and the proposed remedy. Freyenhagen (2023) argues that Habermas's critique rests on a misreading of Horkheimer and Adorno's actual position on language. Reconstructing Horkheimer's *Eclipse of Reason*, Freyenhagen shows that the first generation shared with the later Wittgenstein a picture of language as constitutively tied to forms of life — making language itself an inexhaustible "reservoir of objective reason" that resists full instrumental colonization. On this reading, the first generation was not stranded without normative resources; they already had a linguistic-normative foundation, one that Habermas's communicative pragmatics partially recapitulates without acknowledging the debt. Müller-Doohm (2023) draws a parallel conclusion from a different angle: despite their substantive differences — Adorno's negative dialectics treats the "fall of metaphysics" as an irresolvable aporia, while Habermas reconstitutes practical reason as a post-metaphysical placeholder — both thinkers share a commitment to post-metaphysical philosophy that renounces strong ontological claims while preserving critique's claims to relevance. What Habermas presented as a paradigm shift appears, on Müller-Doohm's account, as a differentiated continuation of a shared project.

A more fine-grained challenge to Habermas's reading is mounted by Kämpfer (2025), who identifies a deliberate and productive ambiguity at the heart of the *Dialectic of Enlightenment*: whether the reversal of domination over nature into domination by nature is a contingent feature of specifically modern capitalist societies or a structural feature of human history as such. Habermas's appropriation of the text requires resolving this ambiguity in the contingent direction, since his account of communicative reason as a historically available corrective depends on the pathologies of modernity being corrigible rather than constitutive. Honneth's reception, Kämpfer argues, makes a symmetrically distorting move in the opposite direction. Horkheimer and Adorno, however, deliberately leave the tension unresolved — and this irresolution, rather than being a flaw, preserves the text's critical force against both appropriations.

Marcuse's place in this genealogy is distinctive and somewhat underexplored relative to the Adorno axis. Habermas's conceptual innovation of distinguishing communicative action from purposive-rational (instrumental and strategic) action was developed partly in response to Marcuse's (1964) diagnosis of one-dimensional society: the thesis that advanced industrial societies have eliminated the "second dimension" of transcendence and negation through a technological rationality that integrates all consciousness into the existing order. Habermas accepts Marcuse's diagnosis of technology as a mechanism of domination but rejects the theoretical grounding: the error, for Habermas, lies in treating all technological rationality as inherently ideological rather than recognizing that purposive-rational action is a legitimate domain of human practice distinct from the communicative action governed by norms. This revision allows Habermas to preserve Marcuse's critique of one-dimensional culture while avoiding the theoretical impasse of a total critique that cannot identify an emancipatory alternative. Verovšek (2025) argues more broadly that the narrative framing Habermas's communicative turn as a "domestication" of the radical critical project misrepresents what Habermas actually accomplished: rather than severing critical theory from emancipatory practice, he rebuilt — on different foundations — the bridge between theory and praxis that the first generation's pessimism had itself rendered theoretically unavailable.

The most sustained internal challenge to Habermas within the Frankfurt School tradition, however, has come from Axel Honneth. In *The Critique of Power*, Honneth (1991) surveys the tradition from Horkheimer and Adorno through Habermas and Foucault, arguing that each stage of critical theory has failed to adequately ground social analysis in the action-theoretic dynamics of actual social conflict. Habermas's specific failure, on Honneth's account, is his dependence on a systems-theoretic framework borrowed from Luhmann: by theorizing money and power as steering media that operate beneath the threshold of conscious social action, Habermas occludes the intersubjective dynamics through which domination is reproduced and contested. The "colonization of the lifeworld" thesis captures a real pathology but remains unable to explain how that colonization is experienced, resisted, and potentially reversed by concrete social actors.

In *The Struggle for Recognition*, Honneth (1995) offers the positive alternative: drawing on Hegel's early Jena writings and G.H. Mead's social psychology, he argues that social conflicts are fundamentally driven by experiences of disrespect and misrecognition across three spheres of social life — love, law, and social esteem. This "moral grammar of conflict" provides both a motivational theory of social struggle and a normative framework for critical theory, grounding normativity in the phenomenology of subjects rather than in abstract communicative procedures. Where Habermas locates the normative criterion of critique in the counterfactual presuppositions of ideal speech, Honneth locates it in the structural needs for recognition that existing social arrangements systematically fail to meet.

The stakes of this shift have themselves become contested in the secondary literature. Repa (2023) defends Honneth's reconstructive critique as a genuine form of immanent critique rather than merely internal criticism: the concept of a "surplus of validity" in recognition norms — normative content exceeding what existing institutions actualize — provides the transcendent moment within social reality that motivates critique and grounds the possibility of transformation. Crucially, Repa argues that Honneth's approach resolves a methodological division that Habermas himself introduced: the split between grounding normative criteria and identifying immanently available elements for social transformation, a split that discourse ethics reproduces without resolving. Thompson (2014), however, argues that the recognition-theoretic turn represents a neo-Idealist domestication of critical theory: by grounding critique in intersubjective experiences of disrespect, Honneth abstracts from the structural conditions of administrative-capitalist societies that the first and second generations kept in view. The result is a theory that addresses the normative grammar of social suffering while losing the systemic critique of the material conditions that produce it. The debate between Thompson and Repa tracks a real tension between the explanatory resources of recognition theory and its capacity to sustain a genuinely radical critique of existing social structures.

Fleming and Sebastian (2025), writing on the centennial of the Institute for Social Research, frame this internal debate within a broader contemporary demand for reorientation. Assessments of Habermas's inheritance now range from calls to recover political economy and anti-colonial perspectives to arguments that the tradition's strength lies in its diffuse reception through feminist, abolitionist, and activist contexts that Habermas's own framework did not anticipate. Whether the communicative paradigm can be extended to meet these demands, or whether its conceptual architecture makes such extension structurally difficult, remains genuinely open.

### 3.2 The Poststructuralist and Postmodernist Debate

If the Frankfurt School inheritance represents a debate largely conducted within shared premises about the emancipatory purpose of critical theory, the poststructuralist and postmodernist debate takes place across a more fundamental disagreement about the status of modernity, reason, and the Enlightenment legacy. The debate was inaugurated by Habermas's 1980 Adorno Prize address, published as "Modernity versus Postmodernity" (Habermas 1981), in which he declared modernity an "incomplete project" — not exhausted, as neoconservative and postmodern critics alleged, but still harboring unrealized emancipatory potential. The address introduced a polemical taxonomy: "old conservatives" who reject modern culture in favor of pre-modern traditions; "neoconservatives" who accept modernity's economic and scientific achievements while resisting its cultural and normative implications; and "young conservatives" — poststructuralists — who appropriate aesthetic modernism's negativism as a critique of Enlightenment rationality without providing any alternative normative basis. This last charge, directed principally at Foucault, became the axis of a decade-long dispute prosecuted most systematically in *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity* (Habermas 1987a), which developed extended critiques of Nietzsche, Heidegger, Bataille, Foucault, and Derrida under the rubric of a "performative contradiction": each thinker employs the resources of reason to undermine reason without acknowledging that their normative commitments presuppose what they deny.

The Lyotard axis of the debate concerns the status of communicative consensus itself. Lyotard (1984) argued that the grand narratives that had legitimated modern science and politics — dialectics of Spirit, narratives of emancipation, the accumulation of wealth — had lost credibility. Against Habermas's consensus-oriented model of communicative reason, Lyotard proposed "paralogy" — the legitimation of knowledge through dissensus and the invention of new language games — on the grounds that consensus imposes a homogenizing violence on the plurality of incommensurable language games. Watson (1984) provided one of the earliest systematic comparisons of the two positions in Anglophone philosophy, arguing that Habermas and Lyotard represent genuinely opposed responses to the same diagnosis of modernity's crisis rather than a merely terminological disagreement: Habermas insists on the rehabilitative capacity of communicative reason while Lyotard endorses the irreducibility of heterogeneity. The most recent major reassessment, by Verovšek and Burdman (2023), challenges this standard framing of mutual opposition. Their close reading argues that Habermas is not a strong foundationalist committed to universal rules for language but rather develops a social pragmatics in which communicative reason is a presupposition of meaningful interaction rather than a transcendental foundation; and that Lyotard does not endorse mere relativism but affirms a universal element of dissensus that cannot be subordinated to consensus. Neither, they argue, defends the Enlightenment philosophy of history or historical relativism: both seek alternatives to these positions that their polemical framing against one another obscured. The Habermas-Lyotard dispute was, on this reading, less about irresolvable philosophical disagreement than about the conceptual imprecision with which each characterized the other's project.

The Foucault axis has generated a distinct and philosophically richer literature. Fraser's (1985) analysis remains the foundational intervention: accepting Habermas's charge that Foucault's genealogical critique relies on implicit normative commitments it refuses to make explicit — a condition she names "cryptonormativism" — Fraser argues that this is a methodological problem, not a political one, and that Habermas's "young conservative" label fundamentally misidentifies Foucault's critical orientation. Foucault's implicit normative commitments are those of a radical critic of domination, not a political conservative; the problem is that he refuses to make these commitments theoretically explicit in a way that would allow the critique to be evaluated on its own terms. Biebricher (2005) carries this reading further, arguing that Habermas's critique of Foucault in *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity* rests on a "double misunderstanding": Habermas misreads Foucaultian genealogy as aspiring to a strictly scientific account of history when in fact genealogy is a hybrid of science and rhetoric — a form of critique that stands between philosophy and literature. This misreading is itself traceable to Habermas's reconstruction of Nietzsche, which strips the latter's thought of its rhetorical dimensions. Counter-intuitively, Biebricher concludes, a genealogy understood as including rhetorical elements is actually compatible with Habermas's own framework, given his remarks on Derrida's use of literary strategies in *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity*. Koopman (2010) extends this revisionary argument further, drawing on Foucault's late Kantian essays — particularly "What Is Enlightenment?" — to show that Foucault develops a "philosophical ethos" of permanent critique of our historical present that shares key features with the Habermasian project of immanent critique while explicitly rejecting the universalist ambitions of communicative rationality. Foucault, on Koopman's reading, belongs within the tradition of critical engagement with modernity rather than against it; the "counter-Enlightenment" label misidentifies the structure of his thought.

A third axis is provided by Visker (1992), who argues that Habermas's critiques of both Heidegger and Foucault in *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity* rest on a conflation of two distinct issues: whether post-metaphysical thought collapses meaning into validity, and whether it can provide normative criteria for critique. Visker contends that neither Heidegger nor Foucault is guilty of both charges simultaneously, and that Habermas's argument misapplies to each thinker a critique calibrated for the other. Fleming (1996) makes a related mediating argument: all three of Habermas's major poststructuralist interlocutors — Habermas himself, Foucault, and Derrida — are committed to immanent critique of modernity and differ primarily in their conception of what internal critique requires. The performative contradiction charge, on this reading, begs the question against Foucault and Derrida by assuming that the communicative standpoint is the only legitimate mode of critique.

The Derrida axis evolved most dramatically over time. Thomassen's (2006) edited collection traces the full arc of the Habermas-Derrida relationship from the polemics of the 1980s — in which Habermas charged deconstruction with an illegitimate aestheticization of philosophical discourse — to the late-career rapprochement symbolized by their joint declaration after September 11, 2001, and their shared opposition to the Iraq War. What began as a confrontation between the paradigmatic representative of Frankfurt School universalism and the premier theorist of différance ended with significant common ground — in particular, a shared commitment to the Kantian cosmopolitan tradition and a shared critique of the unilateralism of American foreign policy. Whether this convergence reveals a deeper philosophical compatibility or merely a contingent political alignment remains disputed, but the trajectory itself challenges any reading of the modernity/postmodernity debate as defined by irresolvable philosophical opposition.

The Rorty axis presents a different form of challenge — one from within the broadly liberal tradition rather than from Continental anti-humanism. Rorty (1985) argued, in his contribution to the Bernstein volume, that the dispute between Habermas and Lyotard is less about substantive philosophical disagreements regarding rationality than about which vocabulary is politically most useful for sustaining liberal democratic hope. In *Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity*, Rorty (1989) developed this position into a systematic critique of Habermasian universalism: liberal society has no need of philosophical foundations derived from a theory of communicative rationality; solidarity is a matter of imaginative identification with others rather than rational consensus grounded in context-transcending validity claims. The vocabularies of self-creation and social solidarity are, for Rorty, incommensurable and should be kept separate. McCarthy (1990) responded by arguing that Rorty's ironist liberalism cannot escape the performative contradiction it attributes to universalism: to make the evaluative claim that liberal society is better than its alternatives requires some standards of comparison that transcend local context, which Rorty's thoroughgoing contingency cannot supply. Dispensing with validity claims generates exactly the kind of contradiction Habermas diagnosed in poststructuralism.

Wellmer (1991) offers the most theoretically refined mediating position. As a student of Habermas who takes the postmodern challenge seriously, Wellmer argues that both parties overshoot: Habermas's communicative rationality retains too much of the Enlightenment's teleological self-understanding, projecting a developmental narrative of reason whose metaphysical residue the postmetaphysical framework was supposed to eliminate; while postmodernism correctly identifies the plurality and incommensurability of reason's domains without drawing adequate emancipatory conclusions from this insight. Wellmer's proposed "post-Enlightenment modernity" preserves the critical impulse of the Enlightenment while acknowledging that systematic rationality cannot provide the unified normative foundation Habermas requires.

Taken together, the secondary literature on the poststructuralist debate reveals a complex picture. The debate was less polarized than it appeared at its most polemical moments — neither Habermas's universalism nor Lyotard's paralogy was as dogmatic as the mutual caricatures suggested, and Habermas's readings of both Foucault and Derrida were systematically contested as distortions. Yet the structural challenge that poststructuralism leveled at the universalist aspirations of communicative reason — the challenge of whether any account of modernity can sustain normative claims without reintroducing a covert grand narrative — has not been definitively answered. The question of whether Habermas's social pragmatics avoids the "grand narrative" charge or merely displaces it remains productive precisely because it requires a more precise account of what distinguishing communicative rationality from foundationalism actually entails.
