## Introduction

Jürgen Habermas inherited a theoretical catastrophe. The *Dialectic of Enlightenment*, published by Max Horkheimer and Theodor W. Adorno in 1944, delivered a diagnosis of modernity so encompassing that it threatened to consume the normative ground on which any critique could stand (Horkheimer and Adorno 1972). Their argument was that the Enlightenment drive to demythologize the world through reason had always already been in the service of domination: the logic of means-ends calculation — of mastering nature and managing human beings as objects of technical control — was not an aberration of reason but its inmost tendency. The culture industry, the administered society, the eclipse of objective reason: these were not failures of the Enlightenment project but its fulfillment. What the first generation of the Frankfurt School produced was, in the formulation Habermas later made canonical, a total critique — one that left no normative standpoint intact from which that critique could itself be justified. If all rationality is instrumental rationality, the critical judgment that instrumental rationality is pathological cannot appeal to any other kind of rationality to support it. The project of emancipatory social theory had argued itself into a corner.

Habermas's career, spanning more than six decades of philosophical and sociological work, is best understood as a single extended attempt to answer this problem. His aim was to show that a defensible, non-foundationalist account of rationality remained available — one that could ground emancipatory critique without retreating either to the naive progressivism that Adorno and Horkheimer had dismantled or to a pessimism so total that it disabled critical theory as a practical enterprise. The vehicle for this attempt was the theory of communicative rationality developed over the decade culminating in *The Theory of Communicative Action*, first published in German in 1981 (Habermas 1984). By relocating reason in the intersubjective structures of communicative action — in the validity claims raised and redeemed by speakers oriented to mutual understanding — Habermas argued that a non-foundationalist but genuinely normative conception of rationality was recoverable from within modernity itself rather than imposed upon it from without. Delanty describes this as the core of the Habermasian achievement: establishing that truth claims are implicit in communication and that they provide critique with its conditions of possibility, so that domination is perpetually challenged by people demanding justifications for the exercise of power (Delanty 2026).

This literature review traces the development, philosophical sources, and critical reception of Habermas's account of modernity across three interconnected sections. The first examines how the communicative paradigm was constructed: its philosophical debts to Hegel, Marx, and the counter-Enlightenment tradition, and its sociological architecture drawn from Weber, Durkheim, Mead, and Parsons. The second section analyzes Habermas's diagnosis of modernity — above all the thesis that modern pathologies are best understood not as the inevitable outcome of rationalization as such but as the colonization of the communicative lifeworld by the systemic media of money and administrative power. The third section surveys the critical reception of the project, from feminist and postcolonial challenges to its historical parochialism, to internal debates within the Frankfurt School tradition about whether the communicative turn represented an advance or a domestication of the first generation's radical critique.

The career can be periodized into four phases, each representing a new attempt to resolve what the previous phase left unsettled. The early phase (1962–1975) encompasses the public sphere thesis, the quasi-transcendental framework of *Knowledge and Human Interests*, and the crisis-theoretic work of *Legitimation Crisis* (Habermas 1975). The communicative turn (1975–1990) produced *The Theory of Communicative Action* and its programmatic defense in *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity*. The discourse-theoretic phase (1990–2000) extended the communicative paradigm to law and democratic legitimacy in *Between Facts and Norms*. The postsecular and historical-philosophical turn (2001–2019) reoriented the project around the relationship between faith and secular reason, culminating in the two-volume *Auch eine Geschichte der Philosophie*. Specter's intellectual biography documents the first two phases in their West German political context, arguing that Habermas's theoretical choices were inseparable from his engagement with constitutionalism, the student movement, and the Historikerstreit (Specter 2010). Verovšek's analysis of the 2019 work situates the final phase as a genuine turning point — a revaluation of religious traditions as normative resources that revises the secularization assumptions embedded in TCA (Verovšek 2020).

The review's central analytical claim is that these phases are unified by a single problem — the grounding of critique — and that the most significant debates in the secondary literature track the adequacy of successive solutions to that problem. Three unresolved tensions run through the whole project. The grounding tension concerns how Habermas can maintain normative claims robust enough to support critical theory while renouncing metaphysical foundations. The scope tension concerns the dual status of the lifeworld concept, which functions simultaneously as a transcendental-phenomenological background condition and as an empirical domain subject to distortion. The universality tension concerns Habermas's claim that the normative standards reconstructed from modern communicative practice carry genuine universal authority, despite having been derived from the specific trajectory of Western European modernity. Finlayson's introductory account captures the structural interdependence of these tensions within Habermas's project (Finlayson 2005). McCarthy's foundational study identified, already in 1978, the quasi-transcendental character of the early framework as the pressure point that drove Habermas toward the communicative turn (McCarthy 1978). Together, these tensions structure both the productivity of Habermas's project and the terms on which it remains, a generation after its systematic articulation, genuinely open.
