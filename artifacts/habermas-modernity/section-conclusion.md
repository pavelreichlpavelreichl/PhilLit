## Conclusion

This review has traced Habermas's account of modernity across three axes: the internal development of his theoretical framework from the public sphere thesis through the communicative turn to the postsecular writings; the philosophical sources from which that framework was constructed; and the reception it has generated from feminist, communitarian, and postcolonial critics. Each axis intersects with three unresolved tensions that no fully adequate account of Habermas can dissolve — only more or less honestly acknowledge.

The grounding tension concerns the formal-pragmatic strategy central to Habermas's mature project. The Theory of Communicative Action (Habermas 1984) grounds normative claims in the validity presuppositions of communicative action, and discourse ethics attempts to derive the universalization principle (U) from those presuppositions through a quasi-transcendental argument. The secondary literature has subjected this derivation to sustained technical pressure. Finlayson (2000) demonstrates that neither Habermas nor his defenders have succeeded in producing the formal derivation the program required, and that Habermas's later recourse to "abductive" grounding via modernization theory creates a circularity: moral theory was supposed to provide independent evidence for modernization theory, but now depends upon it. Heath (2014) shows that Habermas's own revisions severed the connection between practical discourse and speech act theory on which the original argument rested, leaving the program structurally hollowed out even as it continued to generate substantive conclusions. Cooke (2005) and Niemi (2005) identify related tensions between anti-foundationalist commitments and the demand for context-transcendent normative standards. What is notable about this critical literature is not that it refutes Habermas's project wholesale, but that it shows the project to be more reliant on background assumptions — including assumptions drawn from the sociological theory of modernization — than its self-presentation acknowledges. Prasai (2024) adds a further internal complication: the utopia implicit in Habermas's democratic theory tends toward a lifeworldization of systemic domains that contradicts his explicit defense of functional differentiation, suggesting the lifeworld-system framework does not stably contain the emancipatory impulses it channels. No competitor framework has, however, produced a more defensible postmetaphysical grounding of normative claims. The recognition-theoretic alternative associated with Honneth displaces rather than resolves the problem; postmodern alternatives abandon the normative aspiration entirely. The formal-pragmatic strategy remains contested but without a successor.

The scope tension concerns what the lifeworld-system distinction and the colonization thesis can adequately diagnose. The framework continues to generate productive critical research. Condon (2021) reformulates the colonization thesis as "relinguistification" — the penetration of monetary coding into communicative action itself — to address neoliberal capitalism; Kämper (2024) extends the framework to the natural environment, arguing that capitalist systems undermine not only the human lifeworld but the ecological conditions for its reproduction; Habermas himself (2022) applies the structural transformation analysis to digital platform capitalism, identifying a new fragmentation of the public sphere that threatens the epistemic conditions for deliberative opinion-formation. These extensions show that the basic diagnostic architecture retains analytical traction well beyond the historical moment of TCA. Nevertheless, Jütten (2011) identifies a normative gap at the core of the colonization thesis that these extensions do not close: Habermas never explains why reification is wrong from the standpoint of those whose relations are reified, which leaves the thesis's normative grounding implicit and explains anomalies in Habermas's own account of which colonization processes are pathological. The gender-blindness of the original framework, identified by Fraser (1990) as ideological masking of structural exclusions, was partially but not fully addressed by Habermas's 1990 revisions; Young (2000) and Benhabib (1992) develop transformations of discourse ethics and deliberative democracy that remain in productive tension with the original rather than simply superseding it. Whether the normative gap can be closed within the lifeworld-system framework, or whether it requires the kind of more fundamental reconstruction that Jütten gestures toward, remains an open question.

The universality tension is the most fundamental, and Habermas's late work arguably deepens rather than resolves it. Bhambra (2007, 2014) argues that the communicative action framework, like Weberian rationalization theory, treats modernity as internally European in origin, occluding the constitutive role of colonial relations. Chakrabarty (2000) identifies a "waiting room" structure in Enlightenment historicism that positions non-Western societies as not-yet-rational; Dussel (1993) locates the origin of modernity not in the Reformation or Enlightenment but in 1492 and the colonial constitution of the European center through its periphery. Against these critiques, the late Habermas doubled down on a philosophical history of Western reason as the source of the normative resources available for a postsecular, postmetaphysical democratic politics — a move Verovshek (2020) reads as a forward-looking appeal for dialogical democracy but that Benhabib (2021) charges with recapitulating the structure of Hegel's Phenomenology of Spirit. Benhabib's argument is precise: Auch eine Geschichte der Philosophie presupposes a unified "we" subject whose development unfolds in a homogeneous temporal process, reproducing the Eurocentric structure of the Hegelian narrative at the level of the philosophy of religion and history. The charge does not entail rejecting the normative legacy of modern rationality — Benhabib herself insists on fallibilism, refutability, and revisability as normative anchors — but it does mark a genuine impasse for the internal reconstructive strategy.

Taken together, these tensions suggest that Habermas's account of modernity is best understood as an ongoing research program rather than a completed system. Its most productive feature is precisely that it has proven revisable — responsive to feminist critique, to postsecular challenges, and to the democratic-theoretical demands that issued in Between Facts and Norms. Delanty (2026) captures this productively in his assessment of the legacy: the guiding thread of Habermas's writings is that truth claims are implicit in communication and that domination is therefore perpetually challenged by demands for justification — a normative achievement that survives the technical difficulties with the grounding program. Whether the revisions Habermas and his interpreters have made merely accommodate the structural critiques from feminist and postcolonial perspectives, or whether they address the deeper difficulties those critiques identify, is the most pressing unresolved question.

The present review contributes to the secondary literature by holding these threads together — tracing the internal development, assessing the philosophical sources, and evaluating the critiques against the internal logic of the framework. The tendency of the existing literature to address one domain at a time leaves the mutual implications of technical philosophical, sociological, and reception-historical debates underexplored. A synthetic account that reads Finlayson's critique of the derivation of (U) alongside Fraser's critique of the public sphere, or Benhabib's Hegelian charge alongside Prasai's analysis of lifeworldization, reveals structural connections that domain-specific studies cannot easily see. Whether Habermas's project can bear the weight it places on communicative reason remains an open question — but the question is sharper, and more productive, when the full arc of the project and its critical reception are held in view at once.
