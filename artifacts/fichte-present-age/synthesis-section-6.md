## Section 5: Religion, the Late Wissenschaftslehre, and the Johannine Thesis

Section 4 ended with the claim, shared by Maesschalck (1991) and Heinrichs (2003), that the critique of the age cannot be understood apart from Fichte's religion and late metaphysics. This section asks how the epoch scheme depends on the late *Wissenschaftslehre* (WL), whether the religious doctrine of 1804-06 continues or departs from earlier positions, and what the "Johannine" Christianity at the end of the scheme implies for the treatment of Judaism. Most items below are not about the *Grundzüge* itself. Their abstracts concern the 1804 WL, the *Anweisung zum seligen Leben* or the philosophy of religion generally, so their bearing on the lectures is an inference, and I mark it as such.

### 5.1 Popular philosophy and the late system

Two titles frame the question of genre. Traub (1992) is a monograph on Fichte's "popular philosophy" of 1804-06, and Gilli (2007) is titled on the presence of *Populärphilosophie* in the late work. Neither has an abstract in the located record, so I report only that they address the relation of the Berlin popular texts to the scientific system and do not attribute a position to either. Traub (1992) is the only monograph located whose title covers the 1804-06 popular texts as a group.

Three studies with abstracts bear on that relation. Rueda Garrido (2025) addresses it most directly for the lectures. His abstract, in Croatian, uses the fivefold synthesis of WL 1804-II to examine the "implicit logical necessity" of the epochs of the world plan in the *Grundzüge* and of the worldviews in the *Anweisung*. It states that earlier research treats the two works separately and that none establishes a precise correlation between worldviews and epochs. Section 3.1 considered this study as a reconstruction of the a priori plan. Here it is evidence for dependence: if the correlation holds, the epoch scheme rests on the structure of the late WL and not only on a moral or historical argument. Jaenecke (2023) argues from the *Anweisung* that it gives an independent, self-contained summary of the main results of the late WL, specifically its genetic phenomenology, following the sequence "Sein – Dasein – Begriff – Ich – Liebe" (being, existence, concept, I, love). The abstract does not mention the *Grundzüge*. It supports the premise that Fichte's popular texts carry the same metaphysics as the scientific lectures, though not that the lectures do. Nini (2024) considers the 1804 WL as the start of a Berlin period and argues that Fichte's output from 1804 to 1806 "forms an organic whole" that remains "faithful to the insight at the heart of the original philosophy." The abstract speaks of the period as a whole and does not single out the *Grundzüge*. Di Giovanni's (2020) chapter, known by title, reads the 1804 WL as a "transcendental Spinozism". The title shows how the Absolute of the late WL is being interpreted but gives no position on the lectures.

Schick (2024) enters from the other direction. His abstract reports a view held in several studies that the 1804 WL first affirms contradiction as a productive principle, because the Absolute is no longer identified with self-consciousness. He argues that the logical status of contradiction does not change in 1804 and that its ground remains the I's relation to its other. This addresses the late WL, not the lectures. It matters here because a break thesis would give the epoch scheme a different systematic base from the early works, and Schick denies the premise.

### 5.2 Continuity or turn in religion

The studies of religion converge, in their abstracts, on continuity, but none of them takes the *Grundzüge* as its object. Wang (2026) asks whether Fichte's philosophy of religion moved from an early "moral religion" to a late "ontological religion" of God, being and love. Wang argues that, read through the genetic construction of spiritual freedom in the Jena WL, the shift does not hold: the structure "God–the finite–faith" and the structure "being–image–love" express one logic. Wang says that other studies take the late themes to transcend and develop the early framework, which keeps the question open in the literature. Asmuth (1999), known only by title, covers philosophy and religion in 1800-1806 and so the period of the lectures, but its argument is not reported. Asmuth (2024) describes the late philosophy of religion as a "bourgeois" one that preaches freedom, equality and brotherhood, expects a national upswing modelled on revolutionary France, and presents this as continuing Kant. This ties religion to the political reading in Section 6, though the abstract refers to the late philosophy of religion generally. A review of the *Cambridge Companion* reports that Zöller's chapter suggests, against the narrative of a turn from Kantian transcendentalism to metaphysics and theology, "a more seamless and complex development" (James and Zöller 2016). This is the reviewer's summary of the chapter, not a quotation from it.

Two recent items extend the question to the political and theological uses of the late doctrine. Wittekind (2024) places Fichte in the *Sattelzeit* and describes his theories of religion as attempts to determine the function of religion in modern society, transformed from a Kantian moral theology into a unified philosophy of mind. He adds that this alternative to Hegel and Schelling has been taken up by Protestant theology only at a small number of points, though intensively. Rametta (2026) argues that the 1813 *Staatslehre* introduces Christ as the "absolute historical fact" that makes possible a move from pre-modern political theology, based on miracles and coercion, to a "post-Christian" order of freedom and rational self-determination. It concerns a later text. Its relevance is that the late Fichte gave Christ a function in the philosophy of history, which invites comparison with the *Grundzüge*'s own fifth epoch. Whether the lectures anticipate that function is not addressed in the abstract.

### 5.3 Johannine Christianity and Judaism

The religious claims of the lectures are the object of Maesschalck (1991), summarised in Section 4.2. Fichte criticises ancient religions, Christian gnoses and the "rational gospel" of modern Protestantism while claiming the standpoint of authentic Christianity, and conceives human action in terms of a divine plan of realising love among human beings. Folkers (2008), a title on the Gospel of John in the *Anweisung*, and Seyler's (2014) commentary on that text indicate where the Johannine thesis is worked out, but their arguments are not available. The Johannine reading therefore rests in the located record on Maesschalck and on the abstracts below.

Ben-Yehoshua (2025) draws the Judaism question from this religious doctrine. His abstract notes that debates about chauvinism in the *Reden* often stress Fichte's adherence to liberal and cosmopolitan ideals. He argues that these ideals implicitly contain a devaluation of Judaism, and reads the *Reden* in the context of the *Grundzüge* and the *Anweisung*, which "contain several statements about Judaism." The anti-Jewish content of the distinction between "Johannine" and "Pauline Christianity" recurs, he says, in the opposition of "Germanness" and "Foreignism" and is "transferred from a religious to an early nationalist discourse." If this holds, the religious scheme of the lectures is not neutral background to the nationalism of 1808 but a source of its categories. Bernasconi (2020) supports a related claim from a different direction. His abstract reports that Saul Ascher accused Fichte of inventing a new form of anti-Judaism, and argues that Kant used cosmopolitanism as a tool against Jews. Its object is Kant and Fichte's attack on Jews generally, not the lectures. It still shows that contemporary critics and later historians connected Fichte's universalism with exclusion. Titles document further scholarship on the subject: Fuchs (1990) on Fichte's position toward Judaism, Traub (2003) on his relation to Judaism and nationalism, and the Becker-Voigts exchange on Jewish reception of Fichte's idea of the nation (Becker 2000; Voigts 2001). None has an abstract, and none is verified as discussing the *Grundzüge*.

This is the principal objection to the universalist readings in Section 4 and Section 6. The internal-critique reading of Kloc-Konkołowicz and Solé interprets the religious telos as a continuation of Enlightenment, and Maesschalck takes the "emigration" from modern subjectivism to be a collective political task. Ben-Yehoshua's reading would make the same telos the point at which exclusion enters, and it is supported by one abstract about the *Reden*. Whether the *Grundzüge* alone, rather than the *Reden* read with it, justifies the claim is a question for the lectures' own text. The religion of the lectures is thus claimed for continuity with Fichte's earlier philosophy and for a universal Christianity, and it is also charged with a hidden devaluation of Judaism. Section 6 turns to the political form these claims take.
