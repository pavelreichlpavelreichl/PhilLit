## Section 2: 1900-1990: Appropriation, Counter-Reading, Recovery

The twentieth century supplies more documented reception of Fichte than the nineteenth, but the located sources attach it to the Wissenschaftslehre, the *Reden* and the figure of Fichte as a German exemplar, not to the *Grundzüge*. Only one strand, the neo-Kantian and theological renaissance, is signalled by its titles as concerned with Fichte's philosophy of history. The postwar decades then built the editorial and bibliographical infrastructure on which the dedicated studies reviewed in Part II depend. As in Section 1, each source is marked for how far its evidence reaches.

### 2.1 Neo-Kantian and theological renaissance

Around 1900 neo-Kantian philosophers returned to Fichte, and the south-west German school is the subject of a study by Heinz (1997). It is known to the located literature by title alone, and its engagement with the *Grundzüge* is unverified. More can be said of Emil Lask's *Fichtes Idealismus und die Geschichte* (1903). Ferraguto (2019) analyses Lask's book as showing how a radicalised transcendentalism leads to a non-empirical realism and to a valuation of praxis, and reconstructs Lask's position in relation to Windelband, Rickert and Liebmann. The abstract also announces discussion of the relation between philosophy and life and between individuality and sacrifice. It does not mention the *Grundzüge*, so Lask's treatment of history is evidence of the neo-Kantian interest in Fichte and history, not of a reading of the 1804-05 lectures.

The textual side of this renaissance is documented more directly. Fritz Medicus edited the *Grundzüge* in a second edition of 1922, which was reprinted in 1956 with an introduction and subject index by Alwin Diemer (Fichte 1956). The reprint shows that the Medicus text remained the available reading text into the postwar period. Graf and Christophersen (2004) treat Medicus, the teacher of Paul Tillich in Halle, within the neo-Kantian and the Fichte and Schelling renaissance, on the basis of letters and other documents. Their study gives biographical context for the editor, and its abstract does not address the lectures.

Theology provides a probable site of more specific engagement. Danz (2012) is titled as a study of the reception of Fichte's philosophy of history by Friedrich Gogarten, Paul Tillich and Emanuel Hirsch. Only the title and bibliographic data are available, and the *Grundzüge* are the obvious source for such a philosophy of history. Whether Danz discusses them, and what the three theologians made of them, is not established by the located material.

### 2.2 War, National Socialism and their critics

The documented ideological uses of Fichte concern the national Fichte. Alessiato (2020) argues that interest in Fichte grew around 1900 as part of a "German movement" seeking the properly German, and that after the outbreak of the First World War his work served as a benchmark for the myth of the "German character". Intellectuals presented his personality and thought as the embodiment of ideals and spiritual attitudes held to be specifically German. Alessiato proposes the "Fichte case" as an example of the ideological misuse of philosophical and cultural issues. A review of her Italian monograph on the political reception of Fichte around the First World War (Raulet 2020) reports, according to a web snippet that was not checked against the review, that conservative and nationalist thinkers reinterpreted Fichte's concept of war while socialist thinkers took up his social doctrine. Aichele's (2007) study of the "authentic Fichteanism" of the war, centred on the Langemarck myth, is known by title.

For the Nazi period, Heinz and Schäfer (2010) argue that Fichte's reception cannot be reconstructed through conceptual history alone and requires connection to the political-historical context. They build on the ideology-theoretic research group around Wolfgang F. Haug and take Bruno Bauch and Arnold Gehlen as cases. A 1933 title pairing Fichte with National Socialism (Bergmann 1933) and a 1934 review of Engelbrecht's study of Fichte's political writings and nationalism (Anderson 1934) document contemporary interest from the Nazi seizure of power. Their contents were not read. Phillips (2025) reassesses the question in the light of scholarship on fascism by Griffin, Eatwell and Sternhell. He examines Fichte's views on the economy, Germany and anti-Semitism and concludes that Fichte was not a proto-fascist, though aspects of his writing and style had a strong appeal to fascists. A counter-strand is signalled by the subtitle of Becker's (2000) book, which restores the "forgotten generations" of Jewish reception of Fichte's idea of the nation. A companion article (Becker 2003) and the later controversy over the book belong to Section 5.

None of these sources verifiably engages the *Grundzüge*. Their abstracts and titles centre on the *Reden*, on political and economic writings and on Fichte's personality. Phillips's abstract names the economy, nationhood and anti-Semitism as his topics. The role of the 1804-05 lectures in the First World War and Nazi-era uses is therefore a gap in the located literature, and it should not be filled by inference from the better-documented use of the *Reden*.

### 2.3 GDR and Marxist readings

Marxist-oriented sources are fewer still. A report in the GDR's philosophy journal on the state's commemoration of Fichte in 1962, the bicentenary of his birth, is known by title (Bergner 1962), as is a 1988 article on the history of philosophy and "ideological class struggle" (Buhr 1988), whose treatment of Fichte is unknown. Quétier (2025) addresses Jaurès's Fichte as seen by German socialists, also by title.

The one Marxist-adjacent source with an available argument is Rockmore (1980), as reported by Kain (1983). On Kain's account, Rockmore compares a concept of activity in Fichte and Marx, derived from Aristotle, in which activity is both means and end, and he explains the obscuring of their similarity by Hegel's reading of the German tradition and his misinterpretation of Fichte. Kain holds that the comparison documents a parallel but yields no new reading of Marx, and he finds the interpretation of Fichte original. The comparison rests on the activity of the self and does not, in Kain's summary, turn on the philosophy of history. Fusaro's later work on the "accomplished sinfulness" discussed in Section 4 was published after the period covered here.

### 2.4 Postwar recovery: editions, bibliographies, national traditions

The infrastructure for independent study of the *Grundzüge* took shape through the Bavarian Academy's *Gesamtausgabe*, which, according to the NDPR review of the Cambridge Companion, was produced between 1962 and 2012 (James and Zöller 2016). Volume I/8, *Werke 1801-1806*, appeared in 1991 and contains the *Grundzüge* together with *Über das Wesen des Gelehrten*, newspaper announcements of the lectures, poems and a declaration against the publisher Gabler (Fichte 1991). Bibliographical tools accompanied the edition: Baumgartner and Jacobs (1968), and Doyé's continuation for 1968 to 1992/93 (Doyé 1993). The latter's published table of contents, divided into writings by Fichte, literature on his life and work, studies of particular topics and studies of Fichte and other thinkers, shows its organisation. Neither bibliography was read for entries on the *Grundzüge*, and they remain the finding aids for such a search.

Systematic research in the same decades had a different centre. Henrich's *Fichtes ursprüngliche Einsicht* (1967) is presented, in the abstract of its 2019 reissue, as the work in which Henrich showed that Fichte first grasped the problem of self-consciousness and made it the guiding theme of his thinking (Henrich 2019). The located material does not show that Henrich discusses the *Grundzüge*. It suggests that this programme directed attention to the Wissenschaftslehre, as does the Zöller essay described in the NDPR review of the Companion, which maps the late philosophy after 1800 (James and Zöller 2016). That the popular Berlin works received less attention as a result is an inference from this emphasis and not a documented finding.

National traditions then supplied translations and a different set of questions. French scholarship is represented by Gueroult's study of the evolution and structure of the Wissenschaftslehre, known through a short English review (Norton 1931), and by Ives Radrizzani's 1990 translation, *Le Caractère de l'époque actuelle* (Fichte 1990), who also assisted in editing GA I/8 (Fichte 1991). Italian interest is represented by Pareyson's study of Fichte's system of freedom (Pareyson 1976), whose engagement with the *Grundzüge* is unverified. A Spanish translation by José Gaos, *Los caracteres de la Edad Contemporánea*, is recorded under 1976, a date that may denote a reissue (Fichte 1976). The Spanish title is the one used by later Hispanic scholars of the text. Much of the dedicated literature discussed in Part II is accordingly written in Italian, French and Spanish, and the translations of 1976 and 1990 precede it.

### Summary

For the twentieth century the located sources support three statements. The ideological appropriations documented in detail, Wilhelmine, wartime and Nazi-era, concern the national Fichte, and the role of the *Grundzüge* in them is unverified. The renaissance of neo-Kantian and theological interest includes studies titled on Fichte's philosophy of history, among them Lask's book and Danz's study, but their treatment of the 1804-05 lectures cannot be established. The edition of 1991, the bibliographies and the translations of 1976 and 1990 made independent study possible. Together they suggest that the *Grundzüge* was read, where it was read, through the *Reden* and the commemorative image of Fichte, and that sustained work on the text belongs to the period after the French translation. Part II turns to that work, beginning with the status of the world plan.
