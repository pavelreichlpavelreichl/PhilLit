## Section 2: Naturalism, Darwinism, and the Evolutionary Reading of History

Naturalism is not an accidental feature of Kautsky's philosophy of history but its structural foundation. His mature system, articulated most fully in *Die materialistische Geschichtsauffassung* (1927), rests on the claim that social development follows laws continuous with — though not simply identical to — the laws governing nature. The consequence is a philosophy of history in which socialism figures as the outcome of a scientifically legible developmental process, not merely a normative aspiration. How to characterize this naturalistic program — whether it constitutes a form of "social Darwinism," a mediated dialectical naturalism, or something else entirely — is the organizing dispute of this section.

### Subsection 2.1: The Socialist-Darwinism Thesis and Its Critics

The most sustained scholarly case for treating Kautsky's evolutionary framework as a form of social Darwinism comes from Weikart. In his book-length study, Weikart (1999) argues that major German socialist theorists from Marx to Bernstein — with Kautsky as a central figure — developed a distinctive "socialist Darwinism": a selective appropriation of Darwinian evolutionary concepts that retained biological progress while rejecting Malthusian competition and the struggle of individuals. Weikart's account treats Kautsky's *Vermehrung und Entwicklung* and the 1907 *Ethics* as evidence that Kautsky grounded socialist inevitability in the natural trajectory of biological and social evolution, assimilating historical development to a quasi-biological law of progress. In the earlier article, Weikart (1993) places this within the broader German reception of Darwin, showing that "social Darwinism" in Germany was politically diverse — with socialist and conservative variants drawing on different aspects of Darwin's theory — and that Kautsky's version belongs to the progressive-evolutionary, not the competitive-selectionist, strand.

Blackledge (2004) contests this framing directly. In a critical review of Weikart's monograph, he argues that the "socialist Darwinism" label caricatures the actual dialectical complexity of Kautsky's early evolutionary materialism. For Blackledge, Kautsky's engagement with Darwin was embedded in a broader materialist framework that attended to the specific conditions of human historical development, not merely transposing biological categories onto social life. This argument is extended in Blackledge (2006), which contends that the standard dismissal of Kautsky as a "mechanical or even Darwinian evolutionist" is a caricature obscuring his genuine contributions to historical materialism, particularly in the pre-1910 writings. Blackledge's revision is significant because it connects the naturalism question to the broader periodization debate: if the early Kautsky was dialectically sophisticated, then the social Darwinism charge depends on collapsing a distinction the evidence does not support.

Stack (2000) maps the wider landscape of socialist responses to Darwin in the period 1859–1914, distinguishing the "first Darwinian left" — which selectively appropriated natural altruism, cooperative instincts, and mutual aid — from right-wing social Darwinism. This comparative frame allows for a more precise characterization of Kautsky: his naturalism belongs to a recognizable tradition of left-wing Darwinian appropriation, but locating him within that tradition does not settle the question of whether his particular synthesis was philosophically adequate or reductive.

### Subsection 2.2: Haeckelian Monism, the Engels Inheritance, and Naturalistic Ethics

Behind Kautsky's Darwinism lies a more specific philosophical source: the Haeckelian monist tradition. Weir (2012) analyzes monism as the key intellectual framework in Imperial Germany linking Darwinian biology to comprehensive claims about social development, ethics, and politics. Haeckelian monism held that all natural and social processes obey unified evolutionary laws, making it a powerful vehicle for evolutionary social philosophy that could support socialist conclusions. Kautsky's naturalism draws directly on this framework, treating social evolution as governed by laws of the same general type as natural laws, while insisting on the specificity of the social domain.

The monist bridge to ethics is examined in Stewart, Hossfeld, and Levit (2019), who trace how Haeckelian monism generated a distinctive evolutionary ethics in which the moral good consists in what promotes existence in accordance with evolutionary direction. This is the intellectual context for reading Kautsky's (1907) *Ethics and the Materialist Conception of History*, in which he attempts to derive a socialist ethical standpoint from evolutionary-materialist premises — arguing that morality is a product of social conditions shaped by evolutionary development, while affirming the role of ethical motivation in political practice. The *Ethics* thus represents Kautsky's own attempt to navigate the is/ought problem from within a naturalistic framework, before the late synthesis.

The Engelsian licensing of this naturalism is traced by Leslie (2020), who reads Engels's *Dialectics of Nature* as the foundational move that authorized later thinkers to treat social evolution as continuous with natural evolution. Engels's project of extending dialectical materialism to nature is, on this reading, the direct intellectual source for Kautsky's evolutionary historiosophy — a lineage that Gronow (2016) also traces in showing how Kautsky's synthesis of Darwinian naturalism with Marxist political economy produced a distinctive "scientific Marxism" in which the laws of capitalist development are understood as analogous to natural laws.

The comparative dimension of Kautsky's naturalism comes into sharper relief when set against contemporaries who radicalized the same premises. Rowley (2024) examines Alexander Bogdanov's project of treating Marxism as a natural science — conceiving society as a form of life subject to biological laws of energy assimilation and environmental adaptation — as a more radical variant of the same naturalistic paradigm that Kautsky and Engels shared. Where Kautsky retained the distinction between natural and social development, Bogdanov pushed toward a fully unified scientific worldview in which that distinction effectively dissolved. Similarly, the volume edited by Tai, van der Steen, and van Dongen (2019) traces Anton Pannekoek's trajectory from Kautskyan naturalism to council communism, illuminating how a thinker who began within the evolutionary-determinist paradigm came to see natural-scientific determinism as politically disabling. These comparisons clarify the spectrum of naturalisms available in Second International Marxism and distinguish Kautsky's position — which maintained the continuity of natural and social laws while insisting on the irreducible role of class struggle — from both more reductive and more voluntarist alternatives.

The section as a whole shows that naturalism is the foundational but philosophically contested feature of Kautsky's historiosophy. Whether his integration of Darwinian and Haeckelian frameworks constitutes a biologizing reduction of the social or a mediated, dialectically controlled naturalism is not resolved by the scholarship. That unresolved question feeds directly into the next problem: the modal status of historical "laws" and the standing of the claim that socialism is, in some sense, necessary.
