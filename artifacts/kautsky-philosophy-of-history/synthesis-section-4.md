## Section 3: Necessity, Teleology, Determinism, and Agency

Kautsky's claim that socialism is historically inevitable is the philosophical nerve of his system — and its most contested feature. Three questions converge on it: what kind of necessity he invokes (universal law, structural tendency, or covert teleology); whether that necessity is compatible with the motivating force of political agency; and whether any science of history can, in principle, ground the normative "ought" that socialism requires. Each of these questions attracted sustained philosophical challenge, and the secondary literature on Kautsky's determinism is largely organized around the responses it generated.

### Subsection 3.1: What Kind of Necessity? Laws, Tendencies, and Teleology

The analytic benchmark against which Kautsky's historical necessity is most often measured is G. A. Cohen's reconstruction of Marx's theory of history. Cohen (1979) formalizes a productive-forces determinism: the tendency of productive forces to develop explains the succession of modes of production, and the economic base in turn explains the superstructure. Critics of Second International orthodoxy — including Kautsky's Western Marxist detractors — often rely on Cohen's framework as a proxy for the Kautskyite position, and the parallel is instructive. Like Cohen's Marx, Kautsky treats the development of technology and productive organization as the motor of historical change, with socialism following from the maturation of capitalist forces as a lawlike, not merely probable, outcome. Whether this constitutes a philosophically defensible claim about historical necessity depends in part on what kind of law is in play.

Suchting (1972) provides an analytic taxonomy that clarifies the options: historical laws may be universal laws of succession (every society of type A is followed by type B), or they may be tendential laws that hold other things equal, or they may be probabilistic regularities. Suchting shows that Popper's objections to "historicism" largely fail against the tendential reading, but they do bite against the stronger universal-law version. The difficulty for Kautsky is that his rhetoric oscillates between these registers. His programmatic statements — that capitalism must give way to socialism, that this is as certain as natural-scientific prediction — sound like universal law. But his concrete historical analysis, as Blackledge (2006) demonstrates, often operates with something closer to a tendential, conditional claim: given the structural dynamics of capital and the organized working class, socialist transformation is the most probable and structurally grounded outcome.

This oscillation raises the question of whether Kautsky's system is covertly teleological. Honderich (1982) argues that so-called teleological explanations in historical materialism — where present structures are explained by their future consequences — are in fact disguised causal claims, and that the move to genuine teleology conflicts with the materialist thesis that history is independent of human consciousness. The diagnosis applies pointedly to Kautsky: his scientific self-presentation insists on causal, law-governed explanation; yet the architecture of his system — history as an ascent from nature through class societies toward a rational socialist order — carries the structure of a goal-directed narrative. Trüper, Chakrabarty, and Subrahmanyam (2015) situate this tension in its broader intellectual context, documenting how teleological conceptions of history proliferated across socialist, nationalist, and scientific discourse in the late nineteenth century. Kautsky's "scientific" inevitability claim was not an anomaly but a reflex of a wider epistemic culture in which history was expected to have a direction.

Isaiah Berlin (1955) articulates the strongest liberal-pluralist objection to this entire tradition. His target is any doctrine of historical inevitability — Marxist, Hegelian, or otherwise — that treats human choices as determined outputs of impersonal laws, and thereby dissolves the grounds for moral responsibility. Berlin's critique is a foil rather than an engagement with Kautsky specifically, but it frames the philosophical stakes: to claim that socialism is inevitable in the strong sense is to claim that what people do is ultimately causally fixed by forces they do not control, which appears to undercut the very agency that socialist politics presupposes.

### Subsection 3.2: Inevitability and Agency — Fatalism or Compatibilism?

The apparent tension between historical inevitability and political agency is addressed most directly by Cohen (1986). Cohen argues that inevitability and agency are compatible on a causal reading: socialism comes not despite human action but through it, because material conditions causally dispose people toward revolutionary organization. The "conditional inevitability" model — socialism arrives if people do what they are causally moved to do — maps closely onto Kautsky's own repeated insistence that historical laws explain rather than replace revolutionary will. Gronow (2016) shows that for Kautsky, the scientific certainty of socialism's coming was precisely what gave the socialist movement its confidence: determinism here functions as an enabling premise for political mobilization, not as a counsel of passivity.

Lih (2006) develops this interpretation into what he calls Kautsky's "heroic Marxism" — the reading of the Erfurtian synthesis in which the inevitability of socialism generates a demanding political vocation. On Lih's account, the determinism charge fundamentally misconstrues how Kautsky understood the relationship between historical law and party agency: workers were to become conscious of what history demanded of them, not to wait for history to act without them. Blackledge's comparison of Kautsky and Trotsky (2006) illuminates the distinction between sophisticated and mechanical determinism by contrasting these two figures: Trotsky's theory of uneven and combined development both drew on and departed from Kautsky's framework, preserving causal determinism while building in greater sensitivity to the contingency of revolutionary conjunctures. The comparison suggests that Kautsky's handling of agency, while not incoherent, was less theoretically developed than his successors' — a point Blackledge (2006) also makes about the pre-1910 versus post-1910 trajectory.

### Subsection 3.3: The Neo-Kantian Ethical Challenge

The philosophically sharpest challenge to Kautsky's historiosophy came not from the fatalism charge but from the neo-Kantian is/ought distinction. Bernstein (1899 [1993]) crystallized this challenge: Marx's economic predictions had not been borne out, and even if they had, no science of historical development could by itself justify socialism as a normative goal. Bernstein's argument was not that historical laws are impossible but that they are insufficient — the "ought" of socialist transformation requires an independent ethical grounding that no historical science can provide. Steger (1997) reconstructs how Bernstein's encounter with British Fabianism and neo-Kantian philosophy in the 1890s led him to this position, showing that the revisionist turn was as much a philosophical as an empirical reorientation.

The neo-Kantian challenge was not, however, a unified philosophical position. Widmer (2023) demonstrates that the Marburg school produced a spectrum of approaches to the socialism-ethics relation, ranging from Friedrich Albert Lange's proto-socialist neo-Kantianism through Hermann Cohen's more systematic ethical socialism. Beiser (2009) traces how the Southwestern Neo-Kantian school (Windelband, Rickert) made the normativity concept — the categorical distinction between fact and value — central to epistemology and ethics, which then became the philosophical weapon of choice against any scientistic historiosophy. Widmer (2024) shows that Cohen's version of the challenge was the most philosophically rigorous: Cohen argued that Kantian ethics provides an independent foundation for democratic socialism that does not depend on, and cannot be derived from, materialist historical laws. For Cohen, Kautsky's scientific socialism could account for the trajectory of capitalism but not for why anyone was obligated to replace it.

Kautsky's response in *Ethics and the Materialist Conception of History* (1907) insisted that ethics and historical necessity are compatible: morality is itself a product of social conditions, and the socialist movement's ethical commitments are causally explicable by the material interests and solidarities generated by capitalist production. Critics found this response question-begging. As Widmer (2024) argues, Cohen's objection is precisely that explaining the causal origins of moral beliefs is not the same as grounding their normative validity. The gap between an account of why people come to hold socialist values and an account of why those values are justified is exactly what the is/ought distinction marks. Kautsky's resistance to this distinction — his insistence that evolutionary-materialist analysis dissolves the problem rather than avoiding it — is a defining feature of his historiosophy, and one that the secondary literature consistently identifies as philosophically under-developed.

These three problems — the modal status of historical laws, the compatibility of necessity with agency, and the normative sufficiency of a science of history — are the philosophical core of Kautsky's system and the source of its enduring controversy. None was resolved in the secondary literature; together they define why Kautsky's philosophy of history attracted both sustained dismissal and persistent rehabilitation efforts.
