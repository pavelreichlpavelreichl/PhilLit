## Introduction

Karl Kautsky (1854–1938) occupied a singular position in the intellectual history of Marxism. As the principal theoretician of the Second International and the self-appointed guardian of Marx's scientific legacy, he shaped the theoretical vocabulary through which an entire generation understood historical materialism. Yet his reputation in the scholarly record has been largely determined not by his own texts but by Lenin's polemical label "the renegade" — a verdict amplified and philosophically systematized by Western Marxism, from Lukács and Korsch through to Perry Anderson (1976), whose canonical account of Western Marxism identifies the Kautskyan model of mechanical economism and passive fatalism as precisely the foil against which critical Marxist philosophy constituted itself. The result is a peculiar situation: Kautsky remains one of the most cited negative reference points in twentieth-century social theory while his actual philosophical work — above all the two-volume *Die materialistische Geschichtsauffassung* (1927), the most systematic attempt by any Second International thinker to ground historical materialism in a comprehensive philosophy of history — remains largely unstudied as philosophy in the English-language literature.

The central interpretive question this literature review addresses is whether Kautsky's historiosophy amounts to a mechanistic, fatalist, biologizing "vulgar Marxism" — a Darwinian determinism that dissolves historical agency into natural necessity and evacuates any normative content from Marxist theory — or whether it represents a coherent, scientifically self-conscious theory of law-governed social development whose apparent fatalism is strategic and whose naturalism is dialectically mediated. Leszek Kołakowski (1978) presents the sharpest philosophical version of the former charge, arguing that Kautsky's evolutionism transformed historical materialism into social Darwinism, history operating through necessary evolutionary laws independent of human will and socialism appearing as an inevitable natural outcome. Against this reading, a revisionist tradition inaugurated by Massimo Salvadori (1979) and Gary Steenson (1978) has argued that Kautsky's determinism reflected deliberate strategic choices — what Salvadori terms "passive radicalism" — rather than theoretical confusion. Jukka Gronow (2016) has extended this rehabilitation by reconstructing the internal coherence of Kautsky's Marxism as a general theory of capitalist history, showing it to be philosophically serious and divergent from Marx in ways that are analytically significant rather than merely degenerative.

The stakes of this interpretive dispute have been raised by what Lewis (2017) and Poy (2024) identify as a post-2008 "Kautsky revival," driven partly by the crisis of Leninist and social-democratic orthodoxies and partly by the reassessment of the relationship between democratic socialism and the Marxist theoretical tradition. Poy (2024) situates this renewal within the longer-run Cold War suppression of Second International historiography, while Lewis (2017) documents how Kautsky's authority was systematically dismantled by Lenin's polemic and notes the extent to which recent scholarship has recovered a more differentiated picture of his theoretical contributions. These historiographical developments give the question of what Kautsky's philosophy of history actually asserts renewed analytical urgency.

This review addresses that question across seven dimensions: the content and structure of Kautsky's materialist conception of history; his evolutionary naturalism and its relationship to Darwinism; the modal status of historical necessity and the determinism-agency problem; the revisionism debate as a test of his historiosophical commitments; his political theory of class struggle and revolution; his position among Second International contemporaries; and the reception history that has shaped — and often distorted — how his philosophy has been read. A recurring axis throughout is the tension between the dismissal tradition (Lukács, Korsch, Anderson, Kołakowski) and the rehabilitation tradition (Salvadori, Steenson, Blackledge, Gronow), with a secondary debate over periodization: whether there is a sophisticated pre-1910 Kautsky and a theoretically diminished late Kautsky, or whether the continuities across his career are more fundamental than the breaks.

One gap is flagged at the outset: Kautsky's 1927 *Geschichtsauffassung*, despite being his most systematic philosophical statement, remains largely unanalyzed as philosophy in English. As Gronow (2016) and Blackledge (2006) both note, most critical engagement has been indirect — conducted through the mediation of Lukács's *History and Class Consciousness* and the Western Marxist tradition that Anderson codified — rather than through close reading of Kautsky's own mature text. Baring (2023) adds a genealogical twist: the very concept of "vulgar Marxism" is partly constituted by Kautsky's own problematic of instinct and consciousness, making the standard critique parasitic on the framework it ostensibly refutes. Together, these observations define both the state of the field and the opening that a philosophically focused reconstruction of Kautsky's historiosophy can exploit.

## Section 1: The Content and Structure of Kautsky's Materialist Conception of History

Before evaluating Kautsky's philosophy of history on the terms of the dismissal/rehabilitation debate, it is necessary to establish what his theory actually claims. Two questions organize this task: first, what are the substantive components of his materialist conception — its treatment of base and superstructure, modes of production, technology, and nature? Second, does his codification of Marxism extend or distort the theoretical inheritance from Marx and Engels?

### 1.1 The Erfurtian Synthesis and the 1927 System

Kautsky's mature philosophical position is most fully expressed in *Die materialistische Geschichtsauffassung* (1927), a two-volume synthesis integrating evolutionary biology, ecology, and anthropology into a comprehensive account of how material and technological conditions determine social structure and ideology. Volume one, *Natur und Gesellschaft*, argues that the natural foundations of history — human drives, ecological constraints, and above all technology — constitute the ultimate determinants of historical change; volume two applies this framework to the development of the state and human social organization. The ambition is encyclopaedic: Kautsky sets out not a philosophy of history as a complement to economic analysis but as an overarching general theory of history within which Marx's political economy is itself subsumed.

Gronow's (2016) systematic reconstruction of Kautsky's Marxism traces this ambition to the Erfurt Programme period of the early 1890s, when Kautsky first codified historical materialism as a *general* theory of historical development applicable to any social formation. The 1927 synthesis is the culmination of this project, consolidating decades of theoretical work. Gronow argues that Kautsky's framework was more internally coherent than the "vulgar Marxism" charge implies, even while insisting it diverges from Marx's own more immanent-critical project in *Capital*. Crucially, Gronow distinguishes Kautsky's conception of Marxism — as a nomological theory of historical stages and tendencies — from Marx's orientation toward the critique of political economy as such. The gap, on this account, is real but philosophically specifiable rather than a mark of simple theoretical failure.

This nomological character of Kautsky's reading receives explicit taxonomic treatment in Rakhinsky et al. (2024), who examine Leszek Nowak's reconstruction of historical materialism and his attribution of the "nomological interpretation" — treating Marxism as a system of law-like historical regularities — specifically to Kautsky's systematization, contrasting it with the "praxis" interpretation traced to Lukács. The nomological/praxis axis is useful precisely because it captures what is structurally distinctive about Kautsky's approach: rather than centering historical materialism on the self-activity of classes, Kautsky grounds it in the lawlike operation of material conditions independently assessable by science.

### 1.2 Extension or Distortion of Marx? The Engels–Kautsky Question

Whether Kautsky's codification extends or distorts Marx is the core interpretive fault-line. The "distortion thesis" in its sharpest form holds that Engels and Kautsky transformed Marx's immanent critique into a deterministic naturalism: where Marx's *Capital* operates by internal critique of bourgeois categories, the Engelsian-Kautskyan synthesis produces a metaphysical schema of universal historical laws grounded in natural science. Ross (2019) endorses this view, arguing that the transformation of Marxism into "dialectical materialism" began with Engels and Kautsky's "bad reading" of Marx, producing a theoretical framework prone to mechanical application and authoritarian misuse. On this account, the 1927 synthesis is the culmination of a deviation already underway in the 1880s–90s.

Gronow (2016, 2020) offers a more measured version of the divergence thesis. His later work (2020) develops the argument that orthodox Marxists — Kautsky centrally — largely neglected the analysis of the value form and labour power as Marx theorized them, making their theoretical position closer to classical political economy than to Marx's critique of it. This is a claim about the specific categories of economic analysis rather than a global indictment of distortion, and it allows Gronow to acknowledge real divergence without endorsing the political-contamination narrative that accompanies Ross's account.

The rehabilitation position, as articulated by Blackledge (2006), challenges both the distortion thesis and the inference from political failure to theoretical deficiency. Examining Kautsky's historical studies of early Christianity, the Reformation, and the German Peasant Wars, Blackledge argues that the pre-1914 writings demonstrate a sophisticated, non-reductive theory of historical causation in practice. The consistency between theoretical claims and historical work in this period is taken as evidence that the mechanism charge is a caricature. Blackledge (2013) extends this argument contextually, placing Kautsky alongside Plekhanov and Labriola as thinkers who produced genuinely nuanced accounts of historical materialism that neither Anderson's "fatalism" critique nor Colletti's "evolutionary interpretation" charge adequately characterizes.

A productive test case is provided by Goldstein (2014), who shows that Kautsky's materialist analyses of religious history — his treatment of early Christianity and the Reformation — were influential enough that Weber and Troeltsch engaged with his base-superstructure framework directly, appropriating and critically transforming his tools. This reception attests to the analytical traction of Kautsky's method in concrete historical work, even if the philosophical foundations of that method remained contested. At the same time, the early/late periodization that Blackledge (2006) insists upon — a sophisticated pre-1910 Kautsky versus a politically weakened and theoretically ossified later Kautsky — introduces a tension that runs through the secondary literature: is the 1927 synthesis a completion or a deterioration of the Erfurtian project? Gronow's (2016) reconstruction leans toward completion; Blackledge's toward deterioration. The question is not resolved by the existing scholarship, and it defines one of the key unresolved problems for any philosophical assessment of Kautsky's mature historiosophy.

What emerges from the domain as a whole is the theory's distinctive architecture: a systematic, law-scientific materialism grounded in nature and technology, theorizing historical development through modes of production and base-superstructure causation, and positioning Marxism as a general science of history. Whether this architecture is a coherent advance on Marx or a philosophically consequential departure from him is the question that motivates the debates examined in subsequent sections.

## Section 2: Naturalism, Darwinism, and the Evolutionary Reading of History

Naturalism is not an accidental feature of Kautsky's philosophy of history but its structural foundation. His mature system, articulated most fully in *Die materialistische Geschichtsauffassung* (1927), rests on the claim that social development follows laws continuous with — though not simply identical to — the laws governing nature. The consequence is a philosophy of history in which socialism figures as the outcome of a scientifically legible developmental process, not merely a normative aspiration. How to characterize this naturalistic program — whether it constitutes a form of "social Darwinism," a mediated dialectical naturalism, or something else entirely — is the organizing dispute of this section.

### 2.1 The Socialist-Darwinism Thesis and Its Critics

The most sustained scholarly case for treating Kautsky's evolutionary framework as a form of social Darwinism comes from Weikart. In his book-length study, Weikart (1999) argues that major German socialist theorists from Marx to Bernstein — with Kautsky as a central figure — developed a distinctive "socialist Darwinism": a selective appropriation of Darwinian evolutionary concepts that retained biological progress while rejecting Malthusian competition and the struggle of individuals. Weikart's account treats Kautsky's *Vermehrung und Entwicklung* and the 1907 *Ethics* as evidence that Kautsky grounded socialist inevitability in the natural trajectory of biological and social evolution, assimilating historical development to a quasi-biological law of progress. In the earlier article, Weikart (1993) places this within the broader German reception of Darwin, showing that "social Darwinism" in Germany was politically diverse — with socialist and conservative variants drawing on different aspects of Darwin's theory — and that Kautsky's version belongs to the progressive-evolutionary, not the competitive-selectionist, strand.

Blackledge (2004) contests this framing directly. In a critical review of Weikart's monograph, he argues that the "socialist Darwinism" label caricatures the actual dialectical complexity of Kautsky's early evolutionary materialism. For Blackledge, Kautsky's engagement with Darwin was embedded in a broader materialist framework that attended to the specific conditions of human historical development, not merely transposing biological categories onto social life. This argument is extended in Blackledge (2006), which contends that the standard dismissal of Kautsky as a "mechanical or even Darwinian evolutionist" is a caricature obscuring his genuine contributions to historical materialism, particularly in the pre-1910 writings. Blackledge's revision is significant because it connects the naturalism question to the broader periodization debate: if the early Kautsky was dialectically sophisticated, then the social Darwinism charge depends on collapsing a distinction the evidence does not support.

Stack (2000) maps the wider landscape of socialist responses to Darwin in the period 1859–1914, distinguishing the "first Darwinian left" — which selectively appropriated natural altruism, cooperative instincts, and mutual aid — from right-wing social Darwinism. This comparative frame allows for a more precise characterization of Kautsky: his naturalism belongs to a recognizable tradition of left-wing Darwinian appropriation, but locating him within that tradition does not settle the question of whether his particular synthesis was philosophically adequate or reductive.

### 2.2 Haeckelian Monism, the Engels Inheritance, and Naturalistic Ethics

Behind Kautsky's Darwinism lies a more specific philosophical source: the Haeckelian monist tradition. Weir (2012) analyzes monism as the key intellectual framework in Imperial Germany linking Darwinian biology to comprehensive claims about social development, ethics, and politics. Haeckelian monism held that all natural and social processes obey unified evolutionary laws, making it a powerful vehicle for evolutionary social philosophy that could support socialist conclusions. Kautsky's naturalism draws directly on this framework, treating social evolution as governed by laws of the same general type as natural laws, while insisting on the specificity of the social domain.

The monist bridge to ethics is examined in Stewart, Hossfeld, and Levit (2019), who trace how Haeckelian monism generated a distinctive evolutionary ethics in which the moral good consists in what promotes existence in accordance with evolutionary direction. This is the intellectual context for reading Kautsky's (1907) *Ethics and the Materialist Conception of History*, in which he attempts to derive a socialist ethical standpoint from evolutionary-materialist premises — arguing that morality is a product of social conditions shaped by evolutionary development, while affirming the role of ethical motivation in political practice. The *Ethics* thus represents Kautsky's own attempt to navigate the is/ought problem from within a naturalistic framework, before the late synthesis.

The Engelsian licensing of this naturalism is traced by Leslie (2020), who reads Engels's *Dialectics of Nature* as the foundational move that authorized later thinkers to treat social evolution as continuous with natural evolution. Engels's project of extending dialectical materialism to nature is, on this reading, the direct intellectual source for Kautsky's evolutionary historiosophy — a lineage that Gronow (2016) also traces in showing how Kautsky's synthesis of Darwinian naturalism with Marxist political economy produced a distinctive "scientific Marxism" in which the laws of capitalist development are understood as analogous to natural laws.

The comparative dimension of Kautsky's naturalism comes into sharper relief when set against contemporaries who radicalized the same premises. Rowley (2024) examines Alexander Bogdanov's project of treating Marxism as a natural science — conceiving society as a form of life subject to biological laws of energy assimilation and environmental adaptation — as a more radical variant of the same naturalistic paradigm that Kautsky and Engels shared. Where Kautsky retained the distinction between natural and social development, Bogdanov pushed toward a fully unified scientific worldview in which that distinction effectively dissolved. Similarly, the volume edited by Tai, van der Steen, and van Dongen (2019) traces Anton Pannekoek's trajectory from Kautskyan naturalism to council communism, illuminating how a thinker who began within the evolutionary-determinist paradigm came to see natural-scientific determinism as politically disabling. These comparisons clarify the spectrum of naturalisms available in Second International Marxism and distinguish Kautsky's position — which maintained the continuity of natural and social laws while insisting on the irreducible role of class struggle — from both more reductive and more voluntarist alternatives.

The section as a whole shows that naturalism is the foundational but philosophically contested feature of Kautsky's historiosophy. Whether his integration of Darwinian and Haeckelian frameworks constitutes a biologizing reduction of the social or a mediated, dialectically controlled naturalism is not resolved by the scholarship. That unresolved question feeds directly into the next problem: the modal status of historical "laws" and the standing of the claim that socialism is, in some sense, necessary.

## Section 3: Necessity, Teleology, Determinism, and Agency

Kautsky's claim that socialism is historically inevitable is the philosophical nerve of his system — and its most contested feature. Three questions converge on it: what kind of necessity he invokes (universal law, structural tendency, or covert teleology); whether that necessity is compatible with the motivating force of political agency; and whether any science of history can, in principle, ground the normative "ought" that socialism requires. Each of these questions attracted sustained philosophical challenge, and the secondary literature on Kautsky's determinism is largely organized around the responses it generated.

### 3.1 What Kind of Necessity? Laws, Tendencies, and Teleology

The analytic benchmark against which Kautsky's historical necessity is most often measured is G. A. Cohen's reconstruction of Marx's theory of history. Cohen (1979) formalizes a productive-forces determinism: the tendency of productive forces to develop explains the succession of modes of production, and the economic base in turn explains the superstructure. Critics of Second International orthodoxy — including Kautsky's Western Marxist detractors — often rely on Cohen's framework as a proxy for the Kautskyite position, and the parallel is instructive. Like Cohen's Marx, Kautsky treats the development of technology and productive organization as the motor of historical change, with socialism following from the maturation of capitalist forces as a lawlike, not merely probable, outcome. Whether this constitutes a philosophically defensible claim about historical necessity depends in part on what kind of law is in play.

Suchting (1972) provides an analytic taxonomy that clarifies the options: historical laws may be universal laws of succession (every society of type A is followed by type B), or they may be tendential laws that hold other things equal, or they may be probabilistic regularities. Suchting shows that Popper's objections to "historicism" largely fail against the tendential reading, but they do bite against the stronger universal-law version. The difficulty for Kautsky is that his rhetoric oscillates between these registers. His programmatic statements — that capitalism must give way to socialism, that this is as certain as natural-scientific prediction — sound like universal law. But his concrete historical analysis, as Blackledge (2006) demonstrates, often operates with something closer to a tendential, conditional claim: given the structural dynamics of capital and the organized working class, socialist transformation is the most probable and structurally grounded outcome.

This oscillation raises the question of whether Kautsky's system is covertly teleological. Honderich (1982) argues that so-called teleological explanations in historical materialism — where present structures are explained by their future consequences — are in fact disguised causal claims, and that the move to genuine teleology conflicts with the materialist thesis that history is independent of human consciousness. The diagnosis applies pointedly to Kautsky: his scientific self-presentation insists on causal, law-governed explanation; yet the architecture of his system — history as an ascent from nature through class societies toward a rational socialist order — carries the structure of a goal-directed narrative. Trüper, Chakrabarty, and Subrahmanyam (2015) situate this tension in its broader intellectual context, documenting how teleological conceptions of history proliferated across socialist, nationalist, and scientific discourse in the late nineteenth century. Kautsky's "scientific" inevitability claim was not an anomaly but a reflex of a wider epistemic culture in which history was expected to have a direction.

Isaiah Berlin (1955) articulates the strongest liberal-pluralist objection to this entire tradition. His target is any doctrine of historical inevitability — Marxist, Hegelian, or otherwise — that treats human choices as determined outputs of impersonal laws, and thereby dissolves the grounds for moral responsibility. Berlin's critique is a foil rather than an engagement with Kautsky specifically, but it frames the philosophical stakes: to claim that socialism is inevitable in the strong sense is to claim that what people do is ultimately causally fixed by forces they do not control, which appears to undercut the very agency that socialist politics presupposes.

### 3.2 Inevitability and Agency: Fatalism or Compatibilism?

The apparent tension between historical inevitability and political agency is addressed most directly by Cohen (1986). Cohen argues that inevitability and agency are compatible on a causal reading: socialism comes not despite human action but through it, because material conditions causally dispose people toward revolutionary organization. The "conditional inevitability" model — socialism arrives if people do what they are causally moved to do — maps closely onto Kautsky's own repeated insistence that historical laws explain rather than replace revolutionary will. Gronow (2016) shows that for Kautsky, the scientific certainty of socialism's coming was precisely what gave the socialist movement its confidence: determinism here functions as an enabling premise for political mobilization, not as a counsel of passivity.

Lih (2006) develops this interpretation into what he calls Kautsky's "heroic Marxism" — the reading of the Erfurtian synthesis in which the inevitability of socialism generates a demanding political vocation. On Lih's account, the determinism charge fundamentally misconstrues how Kautsky understood the relationship between historical law and party agency: workers were to become conscious of what history demanded of them, not to wait for history to act without them. Blackledge's comparison of Kautsky and Trotsky (2006) illuminates the distinction between sophisticated and mechanical determinism by contrasting these two figures: Trotsky's theory of uneven and combined development both drew on and departed from Kautsky's framework, preserving causal determinism while building in greater sensitivity to the contingency of revolutionary conjunctures. The comparison suggests that Kautsky's handling of agency, while not incoherent, was less theoretically developed than his successors' — a point Blackledge (2006) also makes about the pre-1910 versus post-1910 trajectory.

### 3.3 The Neo-Kantian Ethical Challenge

The philosophically sharpest challenge to Kautsky's historiosophy came not from the fatalism charge but from the neo-Kantian is/ought distinction. Bernstein (1899 [1993]) crystallized this challenge: Marx's economic predictions had not been borne out, and even if they had, no science of historical development could by itself justify socialism as a normative goal. Bernstein's argument was not that historical laws are impossible but that they are insufficient — the "ought" of socialist transformation requires an independent ethical grounding that no historical science can provide. Steger (1997) reconstructs how Bernstein's encounter with British Fabianism and neo-Kantian philosophy in the 1890s led him to this position, showing that the revisionist turn was as much a philosophical as an empirical reorientation.

The neo-Kantian challenge was not, however, a unified philosophical position. Widmer (2023) demonstrates that the Marburg school produced a spectrum of approaches to the socialism-ethics relation, ranging from Friedrich Albert Lange's proto-socialist neo-Kantianism through Hermann Cohen's more systematic ethical socialism. Beiser (2009) traces how the Southwestern Neo-Kantian school (Windelband, Rickert) made the normativity concept — the categorical distinction between fact and value — central to epistemology and ethics, which then became the philosophical weapon of choice against any scientistic historiosophy. Widmer (2024) shows that Cohen's version of the challenge was the most philosophically rigorous: Cohen argued that Kantian ethics provides an independent foundation for democratic socialism that does not depend on, and cannot be derived from, materialist historical laws. For Cohen, Kautsky's scientific socialism could account for the trajectory of capitalism but not for why anyone was obligated to replace it.

Kautsky's response in *Ethics and the Materialist Conception of History* (1907) insisted that ethics and historical necessity are compatible: morality is itself a product of social conditions, and the socialist movement's ethical commitments are causally explicable by the material interests and solidarities generated by capitalist production. Critics found this response question-begging. As Widmer (2024) argues, Cohen's objection is precisely that explaining the causal origins of moral beliefs is not the same as grounding their normative validity. The gap between an account of why people come to hold socialist values and an account of why those values are justified is exactly what the is/ought distinction marks. Kautsky's resistance to this distinction — his insistence that evolutionary-materialist analysis dissolves the problem rather than avoiding it — is a defining feature of his historiosophy, and one that the secondary literature consistently identifies as philosophically under-developed.

These three problems — the modal status of historical laws, the compatibility of necessity with agency, and the normative sufficiency of a science of history — are the philosophical core of Kautsky's system and the source of its enduring controversy. None was resolved in the secondary literature; together they define why Kautsky's philosophy of history attracted both sustained dismissal and persistent rehabilitation efforts.

## Section 4: The Revisionism Debate as a Conflict of Philosophies of History

The *Revisionismusstreit* of 1896–1899 is the moment at which Kautsky's philosophy of history passed from theoretical exposition to polemical defense. Forced by Bernstein's challenge to state precisely what he meant by historical necessity, scientific socialism, and the inevitability of capitalist breakdown, Kautsky revealed the deeper philosophical commitments of his historiosophy more clearly than in any of his systematic works. The debate was not primarily an exchange of economic predictions; at stake were rival conceptions of what historical tendencies are, how they relate to ethical commitment, and what kind of authority a "science" of history can claim.

### 4.1 Breakdown, Tendency, and the Structure of the Dispute

Bernstein's *Preconditions of Socialism* (1899) mounted a two-pronged attack on Kautsky's orthodoxy (Bernstein 1993). Empirically, he argued that capitalism's tendencies toward concentration, immiseration, and crisis were not being borne out; tendencies were just that — tendencies, not necessities, revisable in light of new evidence about cartels, credit, and trade unions. Philosophically, he insisted that no amount of scientific-historical analysis could generate the ethical "ought" that socialism required: the normative case for socialism depended on Kantian moral imperatives independent of any causal account of historical development. Both prongs targeted the same feature of Kautsky's position: the claim that structural laws of capitalist development ground both the prediction of breakdown and the obligation to pursue socialism.

Steger's reconstruction of Bernstein's intellectual development shows that the revisionist challenge was not an ad hoc response to unwelcome economic data but a deliberate philosophical reorientation, shaped by British Fabianism, neo-Kantian ethics (Hermann Cohen, Natorp), and Spencer's social evolutionism (Steger 1997). Bernstein displaced necessity with normative value-guidance: historical development was to be steered by ethical ideals rather than read off from structural laws. In a companion article, Steger argues that Bernstein's core move was to reframe historical materialism itself as an obstacle to socialist politics — a theoretical apparatus that excluded ethical evaluation from the analysis of social development (Steger 1992). The philosophical diagnosis was that Kautsky's conception of agency was inadequate: if the laws of history "explain revolutionary will," they cannot simultaneously supply the normative grounds for it.

Gronow's chapter-length analysis of the controversy locates Kautsky's reply in *Bernstein und das sozialdemokratische Programm* (1899) within the full structure of Kautsky's theory of capitalist development (Gronow 2016). Kautsky was not, Gronow argues, defending dogmatic fidelity to Marx but a specific theoretical claim: that capital centralisation, monopolisation, and immiseration were structural tendencies grounded in the logic of capital, not empirically contingent trends susceptible to indefinite revision. The *Zusammenbruchstheorie* functioned simultaneously as an economic prediction and as a philosophy-of-history thesis about the modal status of historical tendencies — the claim that these processes possess the kind of necessity that distinguishes scientific law from mere correlation. It is this dual status, Gronow argues, that made Kautsky's response philosophically rather than merely empirically motivated.

Luxemburg's *Social Reform or Revolution* (1900) — also directed against Bernstein — sharpened the philosophical point. As Vance (2017) reconstructs the Bernstein–Luxemburg exchange, the real question was not whether reforms were permissible but whether they could alter the underlying structural logic of capitalist development. From Luxemburg's standpoint, Bernstein's empirical fallibilism about tendencies was philosophically incoherent: it treated as revisable precisely those structural features that Marxist theory identified as necessary determinations of the mode of production. Kautsky and Luxemburg thus occupied a common philosophical position against Bernstein, even as they would later diverge sharply on the question of spontaneous agency.

Melrose's sociology-of-knowledge analysis adds a further dimension: the orthodoxy/revisionism split was not purely a dispute about philosophical or empirical truth but a contest over epistemic authority (Melrose 2016). Both Kautsky and Bernstein positioned themselves as authentic inheritors of Marx, making claims to "orthodox" knowledge of Marx's thought function as identity markers within the SPD. The philosophical debate was therefore also a struggle over who had the authority to determine what Marxism meant, which illuminates why Kautsky's reply was so preoccupied with questions of method and scientific standing.

### 4.2 The Instability of the Alternatives, and the Empirical Test

The revisionism debate did not resolve cleanly in favor of either party — philosophically or empirically. Dale's analysis of Bernstein's evolutionary socialism and Austro-Marxism identifies a structural irony: having repudiated Kautsky's economic determinism in favor of ethical autonomy, Bernstein's revisionist tradition simultaneously incorporated a quasi-fatalistic determinism about democratization (Dale 2014). The revisionist escape from necessity was therefore incomplete: it displaced one form of historical inevitability (the breakdown of capitalism) with another (the law-like expansion of democratic participation). This suggests that neither orthodoxy nor revisionism succeeded in resolving the underlying philosophical problem of how to combine evolutionary historical laws with genuine voluntarist agency.

Van Ree's longer-run account of German Social Democratic strategy complicates the binary further. He argues that the evolution from "permanent revolution" to parliamentary gradualism reflected a structural adaptation to parliamentary conditions that had already "evolutionized" Kautsky's conception of revolution before the revisionism debate had even begun (van Ree 2012). On this reading, Kautsky's defense of revolutionary necessity against Bernstein masked a prior and largely tacit reformulation of what "revolution" meant under parliamentary conditions — a redefinition that blurred the philosophical line he was trying to defend.

Kautsky's last major anti-revisionist intervention, *The Road to Power* (1909), attempted to hold the line against this absorption. Lih's analysis shows that the text argued for the necessity of a democratic republic as a prerequisite for socialism, making Kautsky neither simply reformist nor simply insurrectionary (Lih 2018). The dramatically divergent receptions of *Road to Power* in Germany (where it earned Kautsky hostility from both left and right of the SPD) and Russia (where Bolsheviks read it as endorsing their positions) are, on Lih's reading, a symptom of the philosophical instability of Kautsky's position: a historiosophy committed to structural necessity that could not unambiguously determine its own political implications.

The recent empirical work of Bartels, Kersting, and Wolf (2025) extends the test of the debate's economic claims into regional Prussian data. They find a mixed verdict: capital accumulation did produce increased capital share and income inequality, consistent with orthodox Marxist prediction; but it did not produce further capital concentration or increased socialist voting, consistent with Bernstein's critique. Trade union activity limited inequality and fostered socialist support, as revisionists argued. This mixed result illuminates the philosophical stakes directly: the predictions that Kautsky derived from his theory of structural tendency were partially confirmed, suggesting that the debate cannot be resolved by treating historical tendencies as either unconditionally necessary or as freely revisable empirical generalizations. The orthodox and revisionist philosophies of history shared a common difficulty in specifying what precisely follows, practically and normatively, from the partial truth of structural-tendency claims.

Taken together, the revisionism debate constitutes the sharpest available lens onto the fault-lines of Kautsky's historical thinking. His defense of structural necessity against Bernstein's empirical fallibilism exposed the dual burden he placed on historical law: it was simultaneously supposed to ground scientific prediction and provide the motivational and normative basis for socialist politics. Whether any philosophy of history can sustain both roles without either collapsing into fatalism or conceding the neo-Kantian is/ought gap is the question the debate leaves unresolved — and the question that motivates the analytical reconstruction this project undertakes.

## Section 5: Class Struggle, Revolution, and the Political Theory of History

Kautsky's philosophy of history was never purely theoretical: its determinist premises generated specific, contested conclusions about the proletariat as historical subject, the function of parliamentary democracy, and the political meaning of revolution. The transition from abstract historiosophy to political theory is most visible in three connected disputes — the controversy with Lenin over the dictatorship of the proletariat, the theory of ultra-imperialism, and the analysis of the agrarian question — each of which turns on the modal status of historical necessity and what it licenses as political action.

### 5.1 Proletarian Agency, Parliamentary Democracy, and the Lenin Dispute

The governing premise of Kautsky's political theory is that the structural development of capitalism — centralisation of capital, concentration of the proletariat, the sharpening of class antagonisms — objectively generates the conditions for socialist transformation. What this means for the form of revolution was the central question of his career. Gronow's reconstruction in *On the Formation of Marxism* (2016) is the most detailed recent account of how Kautsky derived a parliamentary strategy from these determinist foundations: capitalism's own logic creates the organized working class and the conditions under which it can win a democratic majority; revolutionary transformation must therefore pass through electoral legitimation rather than insurrectionary seizure. This is not a reformist capitulation but a theoretically grounded position about which historical subjects can act and through which institutions. Gronow's chapter on parliamentary democracy and revolutionary tactics (2016) shows the argument's internal coherence, while his chapter on the Lenin polemic (2016) reconstructs how the same premises yielded opposite conclusions when filtered through Lenin's account of bourgeois democracy as structurally class-partial.

The content of the Lenin–Kautsky dispute is a disagreement about how to read a phrase in Marx. Kautsky argued that "the dictatorship of the proletariat" referred to a social condition — a state of affairs in which the working class held political preponderance — not to a specific constitutional form that excludes bourgeois rights and universal suffrage. Lenin maintained that Kautsky's reading was philosophically evasive: all democracy is class democracy, and proletarian rule requires suppressing the bourgeoisie as a class. Fausto's (2001) close analysis of the three-way polemic among Kautsky, Lenin, and Trotsky concludes that Kautsky's critique was philosophically grounded rather than mere reformist timidity — he anticipated structural pathologies of Bolshevik rule that Trotsky later acknowledged. Schulman (2019), building on Lih's *Lenin Rediscovered*, complicates the standard opposition by arguing that Lenin's early thought owed far more to Kautsky's democratic Marxism than commonly admitted, and that it was *The State and Revolution*'s technocratic account of proletarian dictatorship — not *What Is to Be Done?* — that marked a genuine departure from Kautsky's framework.

The pre-1917 ambiguity is equally instructive. Avila and Gaido (2017) reconstruct Kautsky's 1906 response to Plekhanov's questionnaire on the Russian Revolution, showing that Kautsky rejected coalition with the bourgeoisie while accepting the bourgeois character of the revolution — a position both Lenin and Trotsky claimed as confirmation of their divergent theories of permanent revolution. The episode illustrates how Kautsky's philosophy of historical stages generated genuine interpretive indeterminacy: his framework could be read as supporting either a cautious Menshevik gradualism or a more radical rupture, depending on how strictly one applied the thesis that objective conditions must precede political action. Blackledge (2006) argues in the same direction that Kautsky's historical materialism before 1910 was sophisticated enough to sustain multiple political readings, and that the caricature of mechanical determinism post-dates, rather than explains, its political difficulties.

### 5.2 Imperialism, Ultra-Imperialism, and the Agrarian Question

Kautsky's theory of ultra-imperialism and his analysis of the agrarian question are the two domains where the modal status of historical "necessity" has the most direct political consequences. Both bear on the same underlying question: can capitalism's developmental trajectory be modulated by organized political agency, or does it follow an inexorable logic of breakdown?

On imperialism, Kautsky proposed that capitalist great powers could, under certain conditions, form an international cartel — an "ultra-imperialist" phase — that would manage inter-state conflict and allow capitalism to survive without the cataclysmic war Lenin held to be inevitable. Furse (2025) argues that ultra-imperialism was not naive pacifism but a theoretically motivated thesis embedded in Kautsky's democratic socialism: if capitalism can evolve toward peaceful international cooperation, then the parliamentary road to power remains open and revolutionary rupture is unnecessary. The theory thus exemplifies how Kautsky's evolutionary-determinist premises generate political conclusions — by denying that capitalist breakdown is imminently necessary, he preserved space for organized democratic action. Larise (2025) situates Kautsky's position within a broader Austro-Marxist current that held imperialism to be a product of specific financial-capitalist interests rather than an economic necessity, contrasting this with Lenin's and Luxemburg's collapse-inevitabilism. The disagreement over ultra-imperialism is therefore, at one remove, a disagreement about historical necessity: whether the laws of capitalist development permit or foreclose political alternatives.

On the agrarian question, Kautsky's *Die Agrarfrage* (1899) confronted a persistent challenge to any philosophy of history premised on the progressive dissolution of pre-capitalist forms. Ashwath (2025) reconstructs Kautsky's analysis of how capitalism's structural inability to fully subsume agriculture — the persistence of small peasant farming alongside capitalist large-scale production — generated strategic complications for socialist transformation. Capitalism did not simply dissolve the peasantry; it restructured it in ways that resisted the clean narrative of proletarianization that Kautsky's broader framework implied. This was not merely a problem of political tactics but of the theory of historical development: the peasantry's persistence revealed the limits of a unilinear evolutionary account. Kautsky's response — that the peasantry would eventually be drawn into the proletarian orbit as capitalism matured — preserved the evolutionary framework but at the cost of an indefinitely deferred resolution.

Together, these disputes reveal the characteristic instability of Kautsky's political theory of history. His position on the dictatorship of the proletariat, on ultra-imperialism, and on the peasantry each reflects the same underlying structure: objective conditions set the horizon of possibility, but they neither eliminate the role of political organisation nor eliminate the need to judge which tendencies are genuinely necessary and which are merely contingent. The difficulty, as the recurring disagreements with Lenin, Luxemburg, and Trotsky show, is that this distinction proved hard to stabilise — and the unresolved tension between structural determination and political will is precisely the fault-line that divided the Second International generation.

## Section 6: Kautsky Among His Contemporaries

### 6.1 Plekhanov's Monism and the Shared Anti-Kantian Orthodoxy

Kautsky's philosophy of history did not stand in isolation: it occupied one articulation within a broader naturalistic-developmental framework that the principal theorists of the Second International shared, derived above all from Engels's late natural-philosophical writings. Situating Kautsky comparatively — against Plekhanov, Luxemburg, and Hilferding — makes visible what his approach held in common with this generation and, more importantly, what distinguished it.

The figure closest to Kautsky in philosophical orientation is Georgi Plekhanov. In *In Defence of Materialism* (1895), Plekhanov elaborated a "monist" conception of history that synthesized Spinozan substance monism, Hegelian dialectics, and Darwinian evolution into a single explanatory framework: nature and society are governed by one set of material laws, social development follows necessary stages determined by productive forces, and consciousness is entirely derivative of material conditions (Plekhanov 1947). The structural resemblance to Kautsky's own deterministic evolutionism is striking. Both drew authority from Engels's *Anti-Dühring* and *Dialectics of Nature* and both presented historical materialism as a natural science of history — a "scientific socialism" that treated social regularities on the model of natural-law regularities.

Yet the philosophical inflections differ. Plekhanov's monism drew explicitly on Spinoza's attribute-parallelism as its metaphysical backbone, whereas Kautsky's naturalism is more straightforwardly Darwinian-biological, grounding historical development in ecological pressures, population dynamics, and technological adaptation. Soboleva (2021) reconstructs how Bogdanov's challenge to Plekhanov targeted precisely the Spinozan dimension — the extension-thought parallelism — revealing the contested status of this inheritance within Second International orthodoxy. The dispute between Plekhanov and Bogdanov over the correspondence theory of truth (Pavlov 2017) further exposed the fragility of the scientific-objectivist self-understanding that Kautsky equally claimed: whether Marxism was a science or a faith — and what theory of truth that implied — was not settled but contested within the generation.

Where Kautsky and Plekhanov converged most decisively was in their shared rejection of neo-Kantianism. Rumyantseva (2025) shows that Plekhanov engaged in sustained polemics against Bernstein and the German neo-Kantians, characterizing neo-Kantianism as "a step backwards" from Kant and as a philosophical betrayal of materialism. Sockol (2023) traces how Plekhanov's anti-Kantian commitments shaped even his aesthetic writings — polemically directed against the notion of disinterested aesthetic contemplation and toward class-engaged cultural production. Both gestures parallel Kautsky's own resistance, in the 1907 *Ethics* and the *Anti-Bernstein* writings, to any separation of scientific history from socialist normativity. The shared front against neo-Kantianism was thus not merely tactical but rooted in the common conviction that a science of history could ground political obligation without recourse to a separate normative sphere.

On the agency problem — the question of how structural determination leaves room for political will — Plekhanov's treatment in "The Role of the Individual in History" (1940) provides an instructive parallel. Great individuals, Plekhanov argues, can accelerate or retard historically necessary processes but cannot alter their fundamental direction. This formulation closely mirrors the compatibilist reading of Kautsky's own position (as reconstructed, for instance, in Gronow 2016): necessity operates at the level of structural tendency, while the organized working class, as the relevant collective individual, accelerates what conditions have made possible. The comparison reveals that the determinism/agency tension Kautsky faced was constitutive of Second International orthodoxy as such, not a peculiarity of his approach.

### 6.2 Luxemburg's Spontaneism and Hilferding's Finance Capital

Rosa Luxemburg represents the sharpest internal challenge to the evolutionary-gradualist articulation of their shared naturalistic inheritance. In *The Accumulation of Capital* (Luxemburg 2003), she argues that capitalism requires non-capitalist "outside" spaces to absorb surplus value and that imperialism is structurally necessary rather than a policy choice — a thesis that predicts catastrophic rupture once those spaces are exhausted. Where Kautsky envisaged a gradual, law-governed transition to socialism through parliamentary majorities, Luxemburg's breakdown theory implies that historical change would arrive through capitalist collapse and the spontaneous self-organization of the proletariat rather than through organizational preparation for an electoral majority. Bellofiore (2004) shows that Luxemburg's position was more philosophically coherent than the standard "spontaneist" caricature allows — her theory of party organization, far from being anti-organizational, emphasized building class consciousness from below — but the contrast with Kautsky remains real: the mechanism of historical change is for Luxemburg dialectical rupture, not evolutionary accumulation of organizational capacity.

This divergence had direct consequences for how each understood the relationship between historical necessity and political form. Kautsky's ultra-imperialism thesis — that capitalist great powers could form an international cartel and manage competition peacefully — presupposed that capitalism retained adaptive capacity within an evolutionary framework and that the parliamentary-democratic road therefore remained viable. Furse (2025) demonstrates that ultra-imperialism was not naive pacifism but a theoretically motivated argument: it allowed Kautsky to theorize capitalism's survival of war while maintaining the SPD's parliamentary strategy, connecting his philosophy of historical development directly to a democratic-socialist politics. Larise (2025) situates this within a broader Austro-Marxist position that distinguished Kautsky and Hilferding from Lenin, Bukharin, and Luxemburg on precisely the question of whether capitalist collapse was inevitable or whether capitalism could evolve through cooperative imperialist management.

Rudolf Hilferding occupies a distinctive position in this comparative map. Kautsky greeted *Finance Capital* (1910) as "a completion of Capital" (King 2010), and the two shared the theoretical platform of *Neue Zeit* (Gaido 2016). Howard and King (1989) trace how Kautsky and Hilferding converged on the analysis of finance capital's concentration and centralization of the economy while diverging on the political trajectory: Hilferding's "organized capitalism" model was compatible with reformist strategies that Kautsky's revolutionary-democratic framework placed under greater tension. The Hilferding relationship thus illustrates a recurring pattern: Kautsky drew on and praised the economic analyses produced by his contemporaries, but his integration of those analyses into a broader philosophy of law-governed historical development consistently produced conclusions about historical necessity and political strategy that neither Luxemburg nor Hilferding themselves fully endorsed.

The comparative picture clarifies what was structurally distinctive in Kautsky's position. Plekhanov shared the deterministic-monist framework but inflected it through Spinozan metaphysics rather than Darwinian naturalism. Luxemburg shared the commitment to Marxist breakdown theory but grounded historical change in spontaneous rupture rather than organizational evolution. Hilferding supplied the economic scaffolding that Kautsky incorporated while himself remaining closer to an "organized capitalism" perspective less committed to the inevitability of socialist transition. Kautsky's evolutionary-gradualist, law-scientific articulation was, on this reading, one specific configuration within a contested shared inheritance — a configuration whose distinctiveness becomes visible only in the comparative context that the secondary literature, particularly Gronow (2016) on the formation of Second International Marxism, has begun to map systematically. The outline's noted gap — the thin secondary coverage of Mehring and Bebel — remains: the comparative picture is necessarily partial, and a fuller account of the shared framework would require reconstructing their contributions alongside Kautsky's and Plekhanov's.

## Section 7: Critical Reception and the Politics of Reassessment

The critical fortunes of Kautsky's philosophy of history are themselves a philosophical problem. How a thinker is received, dismissed, and rehabilitated reflects not only the quality of his arguments but the political stakes that attach to them. In Kautsky's case, the reception history divides into two broad phases: a long period of condemnation that ran from roughly 1920 to 1980, anchored in Western Marxist and Leninist polemic; and a revisionist rehabilitation, beginning with Salvadori (1979) and intensifying since 2000, that challenges the fairness and precision of those earlier verdicts. Neither phase has produced consensus, and the unresolved tension between them is the central meta-level debate the present project must navigate.

### 7.1 The Western Marxist Indictment

The Western Marxist critique of Kautsky was less a textual engagement than a self-constituting gesture: "Kautskyanism" named the model of Marxism against which a tradition defined its own alternative. Anderson (1976) canonizes this narrative in *Considerations on Western Marxism*, where the Second International's economistic fatalism — with Kautsky as its dominant theorist — functions as the baseline departure point for the whole succession from Lukács and Korsch through the Frankfurt School and Althusser. Anderson's argument is structural: Western Marxism retreated from political economy and from the working class into philosophy and aesthetics precisely because the Kautskyan inheritance had made theory passive and contemplative, a reflection of objective processes rather than an instrument of transformation.

The most philosophically developed version of this charge appears in Kolakowski (1978). His treatment of Kautsky in volume two of *Main Currents of Marxism* argues that Kautsky's evolutionism collapses historical materialism into social Darwinism: history operates through necessary laws that function independently of human will, socialism is an inevitable natural outcome analogous to biological development, and revolutionary agency is absorbed into a fatalist waiting strategy. For Kolakowski, Kautsky's "scientism" empties Marxism of both its normative content and its dialectical complexity — what remains is a positivist natural science of society that cannot ground the political commitments it claims to support.

Yet a genealogical twist complicates any simple reception of this verdict. Baring (2023) argues that Lukács's foundational critique of "vulgar Marxism" in *History and Class Consciousness* is not an external critique of Kautsky but an internal working-out of Kautsky's own problematic. Kautsky had himself distinguished proletarian class instinct from class consciousness, assigning Social Democratic intellectuals the task of transmitting the conclusions of scientific socialism to a working class that could not independently arrive at method. Lukács's definition of "vulgar Marxism" — the forgetting of method in favor of conclusions — derives directly from this Kautskyan transmission model. The "vulgar Marxism" label thus turns out, on Baring's reconstruction, to be parasitic on the very framework it indicts. This does not exonerate Kautsky, but it does destabilize the clean opposition between sophisticated Western Marxism and mechanical Second International orthodoxy that Anderson's narrative requires.

### 7.2 The Rehabilitation and Its Qualifications

The revisionist tradition opens with Salvadori (1979), whose *Karl Kautsky and the Socialist Revolution* was the first sustained scholarly reassessment of Kautsky's trajectory across six decades. Salvadori's central claim is that Kautsky's apparent fatalism was not a theoretical confusion but a deliberate strategic choice: maintaining a maximalist socialist horizon while accepting the constraints of parliamentary reformism in Wilhelmine Germany. The concept of "passive radicalism" reframes what critics read as doctrinal rigidity as a conscious calculation about the conditions for socialist transformation. Salvadori (1989) later revisited and refined this interpretation, acknowledging greater complexity in the relationship between Kautsky's evolutionism and his political practice, but without abandoning the strategic-choice framework. A related contextualizing move appears in Salvadori's comparison of Kautsky with Weber (1989), where both thinkers are shown grappling with the same structural-historical forces — capitalism, rationalization, modernity — and both refusing pure voluntarism, even while reaching opposite political conclusions.

Steenson (1978) contributes a sympathetic intellectual biography arguing that Kautsky's synthesis of Darwinian naturalism and Marxist political economy was coherent on its own terms. The reputation for fatalism, Steenson contends, distorts a more nuanced position on necessity and political action. Shandro (1997) presses further on the theory-practice question, arguing that Kautsky maintained a more dialectical account of the relationship between socialist theory and working-class experience than the "injection from without" model attributed to him — and contested by Lenin — allows. If working-class experience plays a constitutive role in the formation of socialist consciousness, the mechanistic-determinist reading loses some of its force.

Blackledge (2006, 2006) offers what is perhaps the most textually grounded version of the rehabilitation, combining close reading of Kautsky's historical studies with a principled periodization argument. Blackledge argues in "Karl Kautsky and Marxist Historiography" that Kautsky's work on early Christianity, the Reformation, and the Russian and American social formations demonstrates a sophisticated, non-reductive historical materialism, especially before 1910. The dismissive caricature of mechanical evolutionism "obscures the very real and important contribution he made to historical materialism in the early years of the last century" (Blackledge 2006). In *Reflections on the Marxist Theory of History* (Blackledge 2006), the broader verdict is balanced: Kautsky's historiosophy shows systematic integration of evolution, class struggle, and historical agency alongside a tendency toward passive fatalism that becomes dominant in the later work. Gronow (2016) reinforces this conclusion at the level of political economy, demonstrating that Kautsky's theoretical framework was more internally coherent than the "vulgar Marxism" label implies, while tracing the precise ways in which his codification of Marx's categories diverged from Marx's own immanent-critical project.

Lewis (2017) maps the resulting "Kautsky revival," showing how Lenin's polemic and Cold War historiography had shaped the negative consensus that recent scholarship has begun to challenge. Rakhinsky et al. (2024) supply a philosophical taxonomy from Nowak that clarifies what is structurally at stake in the rehabilitation debate: Kautsky's historical materialism is the originating instance of the "nomological" interpretation — treating Marxism as a system of law-like historical regularities — which stands in systematic contrast to the Lukácsian "praxis" interpretation. Whether the nomological reading is a contribution or a deformation of Marx is, on this taxonomy, precisely the question that divides the dismissal and rehabilitation traditions.

The reception history thus leaves three questions unresolved. First, the periodization question: is the distinction between a sophisticated pre-1910 Kautsky and an ossified later one defensible, or does it serve mainly to rescue a preferred theoretical core? Second, the question of political inflection: the rehabilitation tradition is concentrated in the post-2000 Historical Materialism milieu, raising the question of whether revisionism reflects a reconsideration of the evidence or a changed political context — specifically, a renewed interest in electoral socialist strategy — that makes Kautsky's "passive radicalism" look prescient rather than deficient. Third, Baring's genealogical argument introduces a further complication: if the "vulgar Marxism" charge was Kautskyian in origin, what does it mean to rehabilitate Kautsky against it? Neither pure dismissal nor uncritical rehabilitation provides stable ground, and this is the horizon within which the present project must position its own reading of Kautsky's philosophy of history.

## Conclusion

The literature on Kautsky's philosophy of history is organized by a single persistent tension: whether his historiosophy constitutes a mechanistic, biologizing fatalism that evacuates agency and collapses historical materialism into social Darwinism, or a coherent, scientifically self-conscious theory of law-governed social development whose apparent determinism is either philosophically defensible or strategically motivated. Every dimension examined in this review — the architecture of the 1927 system, the evolutionary naturalism, the concept of historical necessity, the revisionism dispute, the political theory, the comparisons with Plekhanov and Luxemburg, and the reception history from Western Marxism to the recent revival — is a different angle onto this same disagreement.

The critical charge, most fully articulated by Kolakowski (1978), holds that Kautsky's "scientism" transforms Marx's dialectical theory into a naturalistic schema in which socialism arrives as inevitably as a biological outcome, rendering political will a mere epiphenomenon. Anderson (1976) canonized this diagnosis as the origin point of Western Marxism: the entire tradition of Lukács, Gramsci, and the Frankfurt School defined itself against the Kautskyian baseline of economistic passivity. The rehabilitation tradition, inaugurated by Salvadori (1979) and developed by Steenson (1978), Blackledge (2006), and Gronow (2016), contests this verdict. Salvadori reframed the "passive radicalism" as a strategic calculation; Blackledge argued that the pre-1910 historical writings demonstrate non-reductive sophistication; Gronow reconstructed Kautsky's theory as a systematic and internally coherent — if philosophically distinct from Marx's own — account of capitalist development. Baring (2023) added a genealogical twist: the "vulgar Marxism" label is not an external imposition but an internal product of Kautsky's own instinct/consciousness distinction, making the Western Marxist critique parasitic on the very framework it condemns.

Yet neither pure dismissal nor uncritical rehabilitation resolves the genuinely unresolved questions that the literature surfaces. The modal status of Kautsky's "necessity" remains contested: whether his historical laws are universal covering-law regularities, conditional tendencies, or covertly teleological projections of a socialist endpoint onto a supposedly value-free scientific framework is not settled by appealing to his intentions. The gap between Kautsky's teleological substance — socialism as the direction of history — and his anti-teleological scientific self-image is documented in the secondary literature but inadequately theorized. Cohen's (1986) compatibilist reading of "conditional inevitability," on which historical laws explain rather than supplant revolutionary will, offers one reconstruction, but whether Kautsky's own formulations sustain that reading remains open. Similarly, the periodization on which rehabilitation depends — a sophisticated early Kautsky deteriorating after 1910 — is argued but not demonstrated with the granularity the 1927 *Geschichtsauffassung* would require if it were examined as a philosophical text in its own right rather than as a late symptom.

Two coverage gaps define where this project enters the literature. First, the 1927 synthesis is largely unstudied in English as philosophy: secondary analysis has been indirect, treating the work as a foil for Lukács or focusing on its political implications rather than its arguments about nature, technology, teleology, and the structure of historical explanation. Second, the naturalism–ethics interface — Kautsky's attempt in the 1907 *Ethics* to ground normative socialist commitments in a materialist-evolutionary framework, and his resistance to the neo-Kantian is/ought separation — has received attention but no sustained philosophical reconstruction that takes both his scientific premises and the Bernsteinian challenge seriously on their own terms.

The present project addresses both gaps by undertaking a focused philosophical reconstruction of Kautsky's philosophy of history, taking the teleology/anti-teleology tension and the determinism/agency problem as its analytic center and reading Kautsky comparatively against his contemporaries — Plekhanov, Luxemburg, Bernstein — in order to isolate what is distinctive, and what remains genuinely unresolved, in his account.
