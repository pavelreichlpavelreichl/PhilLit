## Section 6: Kautsky Among His Contemporaries

### Subsection 6.1: Plekhanov's Monism and the Shared Anti-Kantian Orthodoxy

Kautsky's philosophy of history did not stand in isolation: it occupied one articulation within a broader naturalistic-developmental framework that the principal theorists of the Second International shared, derived above all from Engels's late natural-philosophical writings. Situating Kautsky comparatively — against Plekhanov, Luxemburg, and Hilferding — makes visible what his approach held in common with this generation and, more importantly, what distinguished it.

The figure closest to Kautsky in philosophical orientation is Georgi Plekhanov. In *In Defence of Materialism* (1895), Plekhanov elaborated a "monist" conception of history that synthesized Spinozan substance monism, Hegelian dialectics, and Darwinian evolution into a single explanatory framework: nature and society are governed by one set of material laws, social development follows necessary stages determined by productive forces, and consciousness is entirely derivative of material conditions (Plekhanov 1947). The structural resemblance to Kautsky's own deterministic evolutionism is striking. Both drew authority from Engels's *Anti-Dühring* and *Dialectics of Nature* and both presented historical materialism as a natural science of history — a "scientific socialism" that treated social regularities on the model of natural-law regularities.

Yet the philosophical inflections differ. Plekhanov's monism drew explicitly on Spinoza's attribute-parallelism as its metaphysical backbone, whereas Kautsky's naturalism is more straightforwardly Darwinian-biological, grounding historical development in ecological pressures, population dynamics, and technological adaptation. Soboleva (2021) reconstructs how Bogdanov's challenge to Plekhanov targeted precisely the Spinozan dimension — the extension-thought parallelism — revealing the contested status of this inheritance within Second International orthodoxy. The dispute between Plekhanov and Bogdanov over the correspondence theory of truth (Pavlov 2017) further exposed the fragility of the scientific-objectivist self-understanding that Kautsky equally claimed: whether Marxism was a science or a faith — and what theory of truth that implied — was not settled but contested within the generation.

Where Kautsky and Plekhanov converged most decisively was in their shared rejection of neo-Kantianism. Rumyantseva (2025) shows that Plekhanov engaged in sustained polemics against Bernstein and the German neo-Kantians, characterizing neo-Kantianism as "a step backwards" from Kant and as a philosophical betrayal of materialism. Sockol (2023) traces how Plekhanov's anti-Kantian commitments shaped even his aesthetic writings — polemically directed against the notion of disinterested aesthetic contemplation and toward class-engaged cultural production. Both gestures parallel Kautsky's own resistance, in the 1907 *Ethics* and the *Anti-Bernstein* writings, to any separation of scientific history from socialist normativity. The shared front against neo-Kantianism was thus not merely tactical but rooted in the common conviction that a science of history could ground political obligation without recourse to a separate normative sphere.

On the agency problem — the question of how structural determination leaves room for political will — Plekhanov's treatment in "The Role of the Individual in History" (1940) provides an instructive parallel. Great individuals, Plekhanov argues, can accelerate or retard historically necessary processes but cannot alter their fundamental direction. This formulation closely mirrors the compatibilist reading of Kautsky's own position (as reconstructed, for instance, in Gronow 2016): necessity operates at the level of structural tendency, while the organized working class, as the relevant collective individual, accelerates what conditions have made possible. The comparison reveals that the determinism/agency tension Kautsky faced was constitutive of Second International orthodoxy as such, not a peculiarity of his approach.

### Subsection 6.2: Luxemburg's Spontaneism and Hilferding's Finance Capital

Rosa Luxemburg represents the sharpest internal challenge to the evolutionary-gradualist articulation of their shared naturalistic inheritance. In *The Accumulation of Capital* (Luxemburg 2003), she argues that capitalism requires non-capitalist "outside" spaces to absorb surplus value and that imperialism is structurally necessary rather than a policy choice — a thesis that predicts catastrophic rupture once those spaces are exhausted. Where Kautsky envisaged a gradual, law-governed transition to socialism through parliamentary majorities, Luxemburg's breakdown theory implies that historical change would arrive through capitalist collapse and the spontaneous self-organization of the proletariat rather than through organizational preparation for an electoral majority. Bellofiore (2004) shows that Luxemburg's position was more philosophically coherent than the standard "spontaneist" caricature allows — her theory of party organization, far from being anti-organizational, emphasized building class consciousness from below — but the contrast with Kautsky remains real: the mechanism of historical change is for Luxemburg dialectical rupture, not evolutionary accumulation of organizational capacity.

This divergence had direct consequences for how each understood the relationship between historical necessity and political form. Kautsky's ultra-imperialism thesis — that capitalist great powers could form an international cartel and manage competition peacefully — presupposed that capitalism retained adaptive capacity within an evolutionary framework and that the parliamentary-democratic road therefore remained viable. Furse (2025) demonstrates that ultra-imperialism was not naive pacifism but a theoretically motivated argument: it allowed Kautsky to theorize capitalism's survival of war while maintaining the SPD's parliamentary strategy, connecting his philosophy of historical development directly to a democratic-socialist politics. Larise (2025) situates this within a broader Austro-Marxist position that distinguished Kautsky and Hilferding from Lenin, Bukharin, and Luxemburg on precisely the question of whether capitalist collapse was inevitable or whether capitalism could evolve through cooperative imperialist management.

Rudolf Hilferding occupies a distinctive position in this comparative map. Kautsky greeted *Finance Capital* (1910) as "a completion of Capital" (King 2010), and the two shared the theoretical platform of *Neue Zeit* (Gaido 2016). Howard and King (1989) trace how Kautsky and Hilferding converged on the analysis of finance capital's concentration and centralization of the economy while diverging on the political trajectory: Hilferding's "organized capitalism" model was compatible with reformist strategies that Kautsky's revolutionary-democratic framework placed under greater tension. The Hilferding relationship thus illustrates a recurring pattern: Kautsky drew on and praised the economic analyses produced by his contemporaries, but his integration of those analyses into a broader philosophy of law-governed historical development consistently produced conclusions about historical necessity and political strategy that neither Luxemburg nor Hilferding themselves fully endorsed.

The comparative picture clarifies what was structurally distinctive in Kautsky's position. Plekhanov shared the deterministic-monist framework but inflected it through Spinozan metaphysics rather than Darwinian naturalism. Luxemburg shared the commitment to Marxist breakdown theory but grounded historical change in spontaneous rupture rather than organizational evolution. Hilferding supplied the economic scaffolding that Kautsky incorporated while himself remaining closer to an "organized capitalism" perspective less committed to the inevitability of socialist transition. Kautsky's evolutionary-gradualist, law-scientific articulation was, on this reading, one specific configuration within a contested shared inheritance — a configuration whose distinctiveness becomes visible only in the comparative context that the secondary literature, particularly Gronow (2016) on the formation of Second International Marxism, has begun to map systematically. The outline's noted gap — the thin secondary coverage of Mehring and Bebel — remains: the comparative picture is necessarily partial, and a fuller account of the shared framework would require reconstructing their contributions alongside Kautsky's and Plekhanov's.
