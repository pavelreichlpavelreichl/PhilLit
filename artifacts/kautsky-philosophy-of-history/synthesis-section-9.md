## Conclusion

The literature on Kautsky's philosophy of history is organized by a single persistent tension: whether his historiosophy constitutes a mechanistic, biologizing fatalism that evacuates agency and collapses historical materialism into social Darwinism, or a coherent, scientifically self-conscious theory of law-governed social development whose apparent determinism is either philosophically defensible or strategically motivated. Every dimension examined in this review — the architecture of the 1927 system, the evolutionary naturalism, the concept of historical necessity, the revisionism dispute, the political theory, the comparisons with Plekhanov and Luxemburg, and the reception history from Western Marxism to the recent revival — is a different angle onto this same disagreement.

The critical charge, most fully articulated by Kolakowski (1978), holds that Kautsky's "scientism" transforms Marx's dialectical theory into a naturalistic schema in which socialism arrives as inevitably as a biological outcome, rendering political will a mere epiphenomenon. Anderson (1976) canonized this diagnosis as the origin point of Western Marxism: the entire tradition of Lukács, Gramsci, and the Frankfurt School defined itself against the Kautskyian baseline of economistic passivity. The rehabilitation tradition, inaugurated by Salvadori (1979) and developed by Steenson (1978), Blackledge (2006), and Gronow (2016), contests this verdict. Salvadori reframed the "passive radicalism" as a strategic calculation; Blackledge argued that the pre-1910 historical writings demonstrate non-reductive sophistication; Gronow reconstructed Kautsky's theory as a systematic and internally coherent — if philosophically distinct from Marx's own — account of capitalist development. Baring (2023) added a genealogical twist: the "vulgar Marxism" label is not an external imposition but an internal product of Kautsky's own instinct/consciousness distinction, making the Western Marxist critique parasitic on the very framework it condemns.

Yet neither pure dismissal nor uncritical rehabilitation resolves the genuinely unresolved questions that the literature surfaces. The modal status of Kautsky's "necessity" remains contested: whether his historical laws are universal covering-law regularities, conditional tendencies, or covertly teleological projections of a socialist endpoint onto a supposedly value-free scientific framework is not settled by appealing to his intentions. The gap between Kautsky's teleological substance — socialism as the direction of history — and his anti-teleological scientific self-image is documented in the secondary literature but inadequately theorized. Cohen's (1986) compatibilist reading of "conditional inevitability," on which historical laws explain rather than supplant revolutionary will, offers one reconstruction, but whether Kautsky's own formulations sustain that reading remains open. Similarly, the periodization on which rehabilitation depends — a sophisticated early Kautsky deteriorating after 1910 — is argued but not demonstrated with the granularity the 1927 *Geschichtsauffassung* would require if it were examined as a philosophical text in its own right rather than as a late symptom.

Two coverage gaps define where this project enters the literature. First, the 1927 synthesis is largely unstudied in English as philosophy: secondary analysis has been indirect, treating the work as a foil for Lukács or focusing on its political implications rather than its arguments about nature, technology, teleology, and the structure of historical explanation. Second, the naturalism–ethics interface — Kautsky's attempt in the 1907 *Ethics* to ground normative socialist commitments in a materialist-evolutionary framework, and his resistance to the neo-Kantian is/ought separation — has received attention but no sustained philosophical reconstruction that takes both his scientific premises and the Bernsteinian challenge seriously on their own terms.

The present project addresses both gaps by undertaking a focused philosophical reconstruction of Kautsky's philosophy of history, taking the teleology/anti-teleology tension and the determinism/agency problem as its analytic center and reading Kautsky comparatively against his contemporaries — Plekhanov, Luxemburg, Bernstein — in order to isolate what is distinctive, and what remains genuinely unresolved, in his account.
