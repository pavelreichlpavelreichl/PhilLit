## Section 4: The Revisionism Debate as a Conflict of Philosophies of History

The *Revisionismusstreit* of 1896–1899 is the moment at which Kautsky's philosophy of history passed from theoretical exposition to polemical defense. Forced by Bernstein's challenge to state precisely what he meant by historical necessity, scientific socialism, and the inevitability of capitalist breakdown, Kautsky revealed the deeper philosophical commitments of his historiosophy more clearly than in any of his systematic works. The debate was not primarily an exchange of economic predictions; at stake were rival conceptions of what historical tendencies are, how they relate to ethical commitment, and what kind of authority a "science" of history can claim.

### Subsection 4.1: Breakdown, Tendency, and the Structure of the Dispute

Bernstein's *Preconditions of Socialism* (1899) mounted a two-pronged attack on Kautsky's orthodoxy (Bernstein 1993). Empirically, he argued that capitalism's tendencies toward concentration, immiseration, and crisis were not being borne out; tendencies were just that — tendencies, not necessities, revisable in light of new evidence about cartels, credit, and trade unions. Philosophically, he insisted that no amount of scientific-historical analysis could generate the ethical "ought" that socialism required: the normative case for socialism depended on Kantian moral imperatives independent of any causal account of historical development. Both prongs targeted the same feature of Kautsky's position: the claim that structural laws of capitalist development ground both the prediction of breakdown and the obligation to pursue socialism.

Steger's reconstruction of Bernstein's intellectual development shows that the revisionist challenge was not an ad hoc response to unwelcome economic data but a deliberate philosophical reorientation, shaped by British Fabianism, neo-Kantian ethics (Hermann Cohen, Natorp), and Spencer's social evolutionism (Steger 1997). Bernstein displaced necessity with normative value-guidance: historical development was to be steered by ethical ideals rather than read off from structural laws. In a companion article, Steger argues that Bernstein's core move was to reframe historical materialism itself as an obstacle to socialist politics — a theoretical apparatus that excluded ethical evaluation from the analysis of social development (Steger 1992). The philosophical diagnosis was that Kautsky's conception of agency was inadequate: if the laws of history "explain revolutionary will," they cannot simultaneously supply the normative grounds for it.

Gronow's chapter-length analysis of the controversy locates Kautsky's reply in *Bernstein und das sozialdemokratische Programm* (1899) within the full structure of Kautsky's theory of capitalist development (Gronow 2016). Kautsky was not, Gronow argues, defending dogmatic fidelity to Marx but a specific theoretical claim: that capital centralisation, monopolisation, and immiseration were structural tendencies grounded in the logic of capital, not empirically contingent trends susceptible to indefinite revision. The *Zusammenbruchstheorie* functioned simultaneously as an economic prediction and as a philosophy-of-history thesis about the modal status of historical tendencies — the claim that these processes possess the kind of necessity that distinguishes scientific law from mere correlation. It is this dual status, Gronow argues, that made Kautsky's response philosophically rather than merely empirically motivated.

Luxemburg's *Social Reform or Revolution* (1900) — also directed against Bernstein — sharpened the philosophical point. As Vance (2017) reconstructs the Bernstein–Luxemburg exchange, the real question was not whether reforms were permissible but whether they could alter the underlying structural logic of capitalist development. From Luxemburg's standpoint, Bernstein's empirical fallibilism about tendencies was philosophically incoherent: it treated as revisable precisely those structural features that Marxist theory identified as necessary determinations of the mode of production. Kautsky and Luxemburg thus occupied a common philosophical position against Bernstein, even as they would later diverge sharply on the question of spontaneous agency.

Melrose's sociology-of-knowledge analysis adds a further dimension: the orthodoxy/revisionism split was not purely a dispute about philosophical or empirical truth but a contest over epistemic authority (Melrose 2016). Both Kautsky and Bernstein positioned themselves as authentic inheritors of Marx, making claims to "orthodox" knowledge of Marx's thought function as identity markers within the SPD. The philosophical debate was therefore also a struggle over who had the authority to determine what Marxism meant, which illuminates why Kautsky's reply was so preoccupied with questions of method and scientific standing.

### Subsection 4.2: The Instability of the Alternatives, and the Empirical Test

The revisionism debate did not resolve cleanly in favor of either party — philosophically or empirically. Dale's analysis of Bernstein's evolutionary socialism and Austro-Marxism identifies a structural irony: having repudiated Kautsky's economic determinism in favor of ethical autonomy, Bernstein's revisionist tradition simultaneously incorporated a quasi-fatalistic determinism about democratization (Dale 2014). The revisionist escape from necessity was therefore incomplete: it displaced one form of historical inevitability (the breakdown of capitalism) with another (the law-like expansion of democratic participation). This suggests that neither orthodoxy nor revisionism succeeded in resolving the underlying philosophical problem of how to combine evolutionary historical laws with genuine voluntarist agency.

Van Ree's longer-run account of German Social Democratic strategy complicates the binary further. He argues that the evolution from "permanent revolution" to parliamentary gradualism reflected a structural adaptation to parliamentary conditions that had already "evolutionized" Kautsky's conception of revolution before the revisionism debate had even begun (van Ree 2012). On this reading, Kautsky's defense of revolutionary necessity against Bernstein masked a prior and largely tacit reformulation of what "revolution" meant under parliamentary conditions — a redefinition that blurred the philosophical line he was trying to defend.

Kautsky's last major anti-revisionist intervention, *The Road to Power* (1909), attempted to hold the line against this absorption. Lih's analysis shows that the text argued for the necessity of a democratic republic as a prerequisite for socialism, making Kautsky neither simply reformist nor simply insurrectionary (Lih 2018). The dramatically divergent receptions of *Road to Power* in Germany (where it earned Kautsky hostility from both left and right of the SPD) and Russia (where Bolsheviks read it as endorsing their positions) are, on Lih's reading, a symptom of the philosophical instability of Kautsky's position: a historiosophy committed to structural necessity that could not unambiguously determine its own political implications.

The recent empirical work of Bartels, Kersting, and Wolf (2025) extends the test of the debate's economic claims into regional Prussian data. They find a mixed verdict: capital accumulation did produce increased capital share and income inequality, consistent with orthodox Marxist prediction; but it did not produce further capital concentration or increased socialist voting, consistent with Bernstein's critique. Trade union activity limited inequality and fostered socialist support, as revisionists argued. This mixed result illuminates the philosophical stakes directly: the predictions that Kautsky derived from his theory of structural tendency were partially confirmed, suggesting that the debate cannot be resolved by treating historical tendencies as either unconditionally necessary or as freely revisable empirical generalizations. The orthodox and revisionist philosophies of history shared a common difficulty in specifying what precisely follows, practically and normatively, from the partial truth of structural-tendency claims.

Taken together, the revisionism debate constitutes the sharpest available lens onto the fault-lines of Kautsky's historical thinking. His defense of structural necessity against Bernstein's empirical fallibilism exposed the dual burden he placed on historical law: it was simultaneously supposed to ground scientific prediction and provide the motivational and normative basis for socialist politics. Whether any philosophy of history can sustain both roles without either collapsing into fatalism or conceding the neo-Kantian is/ought gap is the question the debate leaves unresolved — and the question that motivates the analytical reconstruction this project undertakes.
