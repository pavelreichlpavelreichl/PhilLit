## 2016

The 2016 entries include modal metaphysics (Stang (2016)), the conceptualism and perception debate (Schulting (ed.) (2016)), Kant's racism (Allais (2016)), the highest good (Höwing (ed.) (2016)), the Cambridge Edition volume *Lectures and Drafts on Political Philosophy*, and work on the Opus postumum and pre-critical period.

- Stang (2016), *Kant's Modal Metaphysics*. Studies Kant's theory of possibility from the pre-Critical work of the 1750s and 1760s to the Critical system of the 1780s and early 1790s. Argues that the continuity and change between the two periods turn on Kant's transforming the ontological question about what all possible beings have in common into a transcendental question about how we represent possible objects a priori. (Relates to Chignell (2012) on modal metaphysics.)
- McLear (2016), *Kant on Perceptual Content*. Argues that Kant is widely read as endorsing the Content View, on which perceptual states have intentional content and represent ways the world might be, and that this reading faces significant problems. Proposes instead that Kant holds a form of acquaintance theory of perception. (Follows McLear (2011) and joins the naive-realist discussion: Gomes (2014), Stephenson (2015).)
- Schulting (ed.) (2016), *Kantian Nonconceptualism*. Collects essays by Kant scholars on whether, and in what sense, Kant allows nonconceptual content in intuition and perception. Includes Allais's survey of the recent debate, Heidemann on aesthetic nonconceptualism, and Onof and Tolley on space and cognition. (Continues the conceptualism debate: Hanna (2011), Tolley (2013), Onof and Schulting (2015).)
- Conant (2016), *Why Kant Is Not a Kantian*. Argues that Kant rejects the shared empiricist and rationalist assumption that sensibility and understanding are each self-standingly intelligible capacities, which Conant terms the Layer-Cake Conception of Human Mindedness. On his reading of the B Deduction, the two capacities must be made sense of in the light of each other. The note describes it as a statement of the conceptualist reading. (Cf. Griffith (2012).)
- Guyer (2016), *Virtues of Freedom: Selected Essays on Kant*. Collects essays on Kant's attempt to ground morality in the unconditional value of the human freedom to set one's own ends, which becomes autonomy when regulated by the equal value of everyone's freedom. The essays also treat Kant's proofs of freedom, the cultivation of moral feelings such as respect, love and self-esteem, and models of human community. They were first published between the 1980s and the 2010s.
- Höwing (ed.) (2016), *The Highest Good in Kant's Philosophy*. Collects essays re-evaluating Kant's doctrine of the highest good as a combination of virtue and happiness. The essays address its role in the moral argument for faith, the antinomy of pure practical reason, the primacy of practical reason, and Kant's views of the final end of nature and historical progress.
- Westphal (2016), *How Hume and Kant Reconstruct Natural Law: Justifying Strict Objectivity without Debating Moral Realism*. Argues that Hume and Kant share a constructivist method for identifying basic moral principles and justifying their strict objectivity without invoking moral realism or anti-realism. Basic principles of justice are artificial but not arbitrary, because they answer problems of social coordination with reasons that can be addressed to all parties. (Continues the constructivism debate: Stern (2011), O'Neill (2015).)
- Nassar (2016), *Analogical reflection as a source for the science of life: Kant and the possibility of the biological sciences*. Brings together historians of science and historians of philosophy on Kant and the life sciences, arguing that Kant's notion of reflective judgment contributed to the ontological concern with life as a distinctive object of study. Although Kant says biology cannot be a science, his more fundamental claim is that the study of living beings requires an essentially analogical mode of thought. (Continues the life-sciences strand: Mensch (2013), Goy and Watkins (eds.) (2014).)
- Allais (2016), *Kant's Racism*. Responds to Charles Mills's proposal that Kant can be made consistent on race by a threshold notion of moral personhood on which some races do not qualify under the categorical imperative. Argues that Kant cannot be made consistent and that his racism should instead be used to learn about the nature of racism, since his moral philosophy and moral psychology supply materials for understanding it. (Continues the race debate and answers Mills: see Mills (2014).)
- Dyck (2016), *Spontaneity before the Critical Turn: The Spontaneity of the Mind in Crusius, the Pre-Critical Kant, and Tetens*. Argues, against the view that the spontaneity of the understanding is an innovation of the Critique of Pure Reason, that eighteenth-century German thinkers had already developed a robust conception of mental spontaneity. Examines the accounts of Crusius, the pre-Critical Kant and Tetens as an interconnected and overlooked context for Kant's later doctrine. (Relates to Dyck (2014).)
- McNulty (2016), *Chemistry in Kant's Opus Postumum*. Asks whether Kant, who in the Opus postumum adopts a broadly Lavoisierian chemistry, still regards chemistry as an improper though rational science. Answers yes, arguing against Tuschling that mathematical construction keeps a role in the foundations of proper natural science and that the aether and the enumeration of the elements do not make chemistry a proper science. (Relates to Hall (2014) on the Opus postumum and McNulty (2015) on chemistry.)
- Matherne (2016), *Kantian Themes in Merleau-Ponty's Theory of Perception*. Challenges the picture of Kant as an intellectualist and Merleau-Ponty as his non-conceptualist challenger. Argues, through Kant's schematism, the neo-Kantian Lachièze-Rey and Merleau-Ponty's body schema, that Merleau-Ponty's theory of perception develops Kant's.
- Kant (2016), *Lectures and Drafts on Political Philosophy*. An edition/translation in the Cambridge Edition of the Works of Immanuel Kant of reflections Kant wrote while developing his political philosophy, including preparatory drafts for Theory and Practice, Toward Perpetual Peace, the Doctrine of Right and the Conflict of the Faculties. It also contains the only surviving student transcription of his course on Natural Right. The texts trace his political thought from his exposure to Rousseau in the 1760s to the 1790s.
- Eberhard (2016), *Preparation for Natural Theology: With Kant's Notes and the Danzig Rational Theology Transcript*. An edition/translation of Eberhard's 1781 textbook, which Kant used as the basis for his lectures on the philosophical doctrine of religion, together with Kant's notes and the Danzig rational theology transcript. The complete text makes it possible to separate Eberhard's words from Kant's own ideas in the lectures.

## 2017

The 2017 entries address cognition, perception and the self (Watkins and Willaschek (2017), Longuenesse (2017)), normativity (Pollok (2017)), laws of nature and mathematics (Massimi and Breitenbach (eds.) (2017)), global justice and colonialism (Flikschuh (2017)), Kant and women, and the late transition project.

- Longuenesse (2017), *I, Me, Mine: Back to Kant, and Back Again*. Analyses Kant's distinction between consciousness of oneself as subject and as object and its relevance to contemporary discussions of self-consciousness and the first person, in dialogue with recent philosophy of language and mind and with Sartre. Argues that Kant identified a specific kind of self-consciousness, awareness of one's activity of bringing rational unity to one's mental states, and analyses his critique of rational psychology in the Paralogisms. (Relates to Kitcher (2011) on apperception and to the Paralogisms: Proops (2010), Dyck (2014).)
- Pollok (2017), *Kant's Theory of Normativity: Exploring the Space of Reason*. Offers a book-length analysis of Kant's theory of normativity across theoretical and practical philosophy and aesthetics. Reads the critical turn as a normative turn: Kant departs from the perfectionism of early modern rationalism and arrives at synthetic a priori principles that fix the validity of our judgments. Relates reason's self-legislation to the natural-right tradition. (Relates to Ginsborg (2014) on normativity.)
- Watkins and Willaschek (2017), *Kant's Account of Cognition*. Sets out the structure of Kant's account of theoretical cognition (Erkenntnis) in the Critique of Pure Reason. Argues that cognition is a mental state that determines a given object by attributing general features to it, that givenness involves an immediate relation to an existing object, and that cognition is therefore distinct from knowledge (Wissen). The note describes it as a framework reading that feeds the naive-realist and nonconceptualist debates about intuition. (Continues the conceptualism and naive-realist discussion: Gomes (2014), Stephenson (2015), McLear (2016).)
- Gomes and Stephenson (eds.) (2017), *Kant and the Philosophy of Mind: Perception, Reason, and the Self*. Fourteen essays on the philosophy of mind in Kant's writings: mental processing in intuition, the object-dependence of intuitions, imagination and inner sense, and the nature of the self and self-consciousness. The volume also draws out the relevance of these topics to contemporary philosophy of mind. (Continues the conceptualism and naive-realist discussion: Gomes (2014), Stephenson (2015), McLear (2016).)
- O'Shea (ed.) (2017), *Kant's Critique of Pure Reason: A Critical Guide*. A Cambridge Critical Guide in which an international team of Kant scholars clarifies the terms and arguments of the Critique of Pure Reason and engages with its debates, including the interpretation of transcendental idealism, conceptualism and nonconceptual content in perception, and the soundness of transcendental arguments. (A volume-length survey of the Critique like Guyer (ed.) (2010).)
- Massimi and Breitenbach (eds.) (2017), *Kant and the Laws of Nature*. Collects essays exploring Kant's views on the laws of nature in the physical and life sciences. Topics include the separation of the physical and life sciences in Kant's treatment, the relation between universal and empirical laws, and the role of reason and understanding in imposing lawful unity on nature.
- Sutherland (2017), *Kant's Conception of Number*. Investigates Kant's conception of natural number, setting aside the role of intuition in arithmetic. Argues that the conception contains both cardinal and ordinal elements: the cardinal elements give the basis of a conception of number in general, and the ordinal elements specify the size of particular collections. (Continues the Kant-and-mathematics strand: Friedman (2012).)
- Kleingeld (2017), *Contradiction and Kant's Formula of Universal Law*. Argues that the standard reading of the Formula of Universal Law as requiring universalizability, marked by the distinction between contradictions in conception and in the will, overlooks the simultaneity condition in the formula. Proposes that the principle requires being able to will two things simultaneously without self-contradiction: that a maxim be one's own and that it be a universal law.
- Flikschuh (2017), *What Is Orientation in Global Thinking? A Kantian Inquiry*. Takes Kant's question "What is orientation in thinking?" to argue that global normative theory fails to acknowledge its own conceptual inadequacies and tends to apply domestic political experience to the global context. Proposes a form of global reasoning sensitive to the variability of contexts and to local reasons for action. (Continues the colonialism discussion: Flikschuh and Ypi (eds.) (2014).)
- Valdez (2017), *It's Not about Race: Good Wars, Bad Wars, and the Origins of Kant's Anti-Colonialism*. Interprets Kant's cosmopolitanism and anti-colonialism in Toward Perpetual Peace. On the historical evidence, Kant's concern is the damage European expansion and colonial rivalry do to the prospects for peace in Europe, which leads him to adjust his concept of antagonism. Concludes that his anti-colonialism co-exists with hierarchical views of race. (Continues the colonialism discussion: Flikschuh and Ypi (eds.) (2014).)
- Chaouli (2017), *Thinking with Kant's Critique of Judgment*. Invites readers to think through the Critique of Judgment about the force of aesthetic experience, the essence of art, and the relation between beauty and meaning. Each chapter unfolds the significance of a key concept of Kant's thought.
- Huneman (2017), *Kant's Concept of Organism Revisited: A Framework for a Possible Synthesis between Developmentalism and Adaptationism?*. Argues that Kant's concept of organism, usually seen as a precursor of developmentalism, is incomplete on that view: his regulative notion of purposiveness relies on a design criterion and an epigenesis criterion for natural purposes. Because the design criterion is met within the Modern Synthesis and the epigenesis criterion within Evo-Devo, Kant's notion could frame a synthesis of adaptationism and developmentalism. (Relates to Nassar (2016) and Goy and Watkins (eds.) (2014).)
- Varden (2017), *Kant and Women*. Argues that Kant's conception of women is complex and, on a sympathetic reading, not anti-feminist. Kant expects the traditional male-female distinction to persist but does not propose the traditional gender ideal as the moral ideal, so his moral works hold that all citizens, including women, have the right and should be encouraged to strive toward an active condition. (Continues the gender discussion: Papadaki (2010), Hay (2013).)
- Thorndike (2017), *Kant's Transition Project and Late Philosophy: Connecting the Opus postumum and Metaphysics of Morals*. Argues that Kant saw a need for a transition from the a priori to the empirical in both natural and practical philosophy: the transition from the metaphysical foundations of natural science to physics in the Opus postumum is paralleled by a transition from the metaphysics of morals to ethics applied to contingent circumstances. On Thorndike's account, moral feelings make the moral transition possible by serving as schemata of the moral law. (Relates to Hall (2014) and McNulty (2016) on the late work.) (Summary based on the NDPR review.) (Dated by its CrossRef print or online date; see YEAR NOTE.)
- Engelland (2017), *Heidegger's Shadow: Kant, Husserl, and the Transcendental Turn*. Contends that Heidegger recognizes transcendental philosophy as the necessary entry point to his thought yet strives to overcome it, and that he cannot abandon his own early work in transcendental philosophy. Traces Heidegger's readings of Kant and Husserl through Kant and the Problem of Metaphysics, Being and Time and Contributions. (Relates to Golob (2013) on Heidegger's reading of Kant.)
- Kant (2017), *Kant: The Metaphysics of Morals*. An edition/translation in the Cambridge Texts in the History of Philosophy: a revised edition of Mary Gregor's translation of the complete Metaphysics of Morals (Doctrine of Right and Doctrine of Virtue). It adds footnotes on controversial points in the translation, an introduction by Lara Denis that sets the work in context and presents interpretive debates, and guidance on further reading.

## 2018

The 2018 entries include work on the Transcendental Dialectic (Willaschek (2018)), Kant and animals (Korsgaard (2018)), the race debate (Mills (2018), Sandford (2018)), several Cambridge Elements, Baumgarten and Kant, an edition of the *Religion* and the XII Kant-Kongress proceedings.

- Willaschek (2018), *Kant on the Sources of Metaphysics: The Dialectic of Pure Reason*. Reconstructs and defends Kant's account of why rational beings must ask metaphysical questions about unconditioned objects such as souls, uncaused causes or God, and why answers appear rationally compelling. Interprets the Transcendental Dialectic so as to bring out its positive side and Kant's account of metaphysical thinking.
- Horstmann (2018), *Kant's Power of Imagination*. A Cambridge Element (Elements in the Philosophy of Immanuel Kant) that offers a reconstructive study of how the power of imagination contributes to cognition according to Kant, drawing only on Kant's own resources. One section treats the imagination in the A and B editions of the Critique of Pure Reason and another its role in the first part of the Critique of Judgment.
- Lu-Adler (2018), *Kant and the Science of Logic: A Historical and Philosophical Reconstruction*. Presents Kant's theory of logic as a turning point in the history of debates over whether logic is a science, an instrument, a standard of assessment or a mixture, and over how it relates to metaphysics and physics. Reconstructs Kant's distinctions between general and particular logic, pure and applied logic, and pure general and transcendental logic in their historical context.
- Merritt (2018a), *Kant on Reflection and Virtue*. Gives an account of reflection and the cognitive virtues in Kant, taking as its point of departure his claim that all judgments require reflection. Argues for a specification thesis, that moral virtue is a specification of general cognitive virtue, with reflection standing at the intersection of theoretical and practical reason. (Summary based on the NDPR review.)
- Sorensen and Williamson (eds.) (2018), *Kant and the Faculty of Feeling*. Collects essays on feeling, Kant's third mental faculty, which has received less attention than cognition and desire. The volume asks why feeling must be a separate faculty, how it relates to cognition and desire, and what respect, sublimity, enthusiasm and hope tell us about its nature.
- Papish (2018), *Kant on Evil, Self-Deception, and Moral Reform*. Explores the cognitive dimensions of evil and moral reform in Kant's mature ethics: what self-deception is, how it is connected to evil, and how we achieve self-knowledge in its place. Treats hedonism, character, moral weakness and strength, the alleged universality of evil, and the role of the ethical community, using Kant's logic lectures alongside the core ethical texts.
- Holtman (2018), *Kant on Civil Society and Welfare*. A Cambridge Element in the series Elements in the Philosophy of Immanuel Kant. Examines what justifies state-sponsored supports for individual welfare within a Kantian political system and their purpose, extent and form. Assesses minimalist and middle-ground interpretations of Kant's political theory and develops an alternative based on civic respect for each person as a participant in joint governance.
- Merritt (2018b), *The Sublime*. A Cambridge Element that considers Kant's account of the sublime in the context of Anglophone and German rationalist predecessors. Argues that strictly only the human vocation to perfect our rational capacity according to the standard of virtue is sublime, distinguishes respect and admiration as the two varieties of sublime feeling, and discusses the Stoic channel through Seneca.
- Korsgaard (2018), *Fellow Creatures: Our Obligations to the Other Animals*. Argues that we are obligated to treat all sentient animals as ends in themselves. Combines an Aristotelian theory of the good with a revised version of Kant's argument for the value of humanity, holding that rationality commits us to claiming standing as ends in ourselves both as autonomous lawmakers and as beings with a good. The note describes it as an extension and revision of Kant's formula of humanity that departs from Kant's own view of animals. (Joins the animals discussion: McLear (2011).)
- Mills (2018), *Black Radical Kantianism*. Tries to develop a black radical Kantianism, that is, a Kantianism informed by the black experience in modernity, after briefly considering socialist and feminist appropriations of Kant. The note places it as a reconstruction of Kantian moral and political theory informed by the critique of Kant's racism. (Continues the race debate; Allais (2016) answers Mills's earlier proposal; cf. Mills (2014).) (Summary based on a PhilPapers snippet.)
- Sandford (2018), *Kant, race, and natural history*. Argues that Kant's treatment of the systematic unity of nature and knowledge in the first and third Critiques can be traced back to a methodological problem in the natural history of his period, the possibility of a natural system of nature. His theory of race, worked out in response to the problem of unity in diversity within the human species, is presented as the earliest developed model of teleological judgment in his work. (Takes the systematic-link side of the race debate; cf. Mills (2014), Allais (2016).)
- Fugate and Hymers (eds.) (2018), *Baumgarten and Kant on Metaphysics*. Eleven essays on Alexander Baumgarten's Metaphysics and its relation to Kant's philosophy. Early chapters set Baumgarten's work in its historical context, including Pietism and rationalism, and later chapters explore how Kant criticized and built on the Metaphysics.
- Nisenbaum (2018), *For the Love of Metaphysics: Nihilism and the Conflict of Reason from Kant to Rosenzweig*. Proposes that the conflict of reason in the Critique of Pure Reason, and Kant's solution through the primacy of the practical, is the problem shaping German Idealism. Jacobi and Maimon set the stage for the reception of critical philosophy, and the rise and fall of German Idealism is told as a story of interpretations and radicalizations of Kant's prioritizing of the practical.
- Kant (2018), *Kant: Religion within the Boundaries of Mere Reason: And Other Writings*. An edition/translation in the Cambridge Texts in the History of Philosophy, a revised edition with slightly revised translations, a revised introduction and guidance on further reading. The work spells out a religion grounded in moral reason and treats original sin, justification and the proper role of a church.
- Waibel et al. (eds.) (2018), *Natur und Freiheit: Akten des XII. Internationalen Kant-Kongresses*. Congress proceedings that collect the plenary, main and session lectures of the Twelfth International Kant Congress, "Nature and Freedom", held at the University of Vienna from 21 to 25 September 2015, on two fundamental concepts of Kant's critical works. The contributions are multilingual and span the subfields. (Belongs to the Kant-Kongress series: Bacin et al. (eds.) (2013).)
