## 2010

The 2010 entries include commentary on the Critique of Pure Reason and arguments for transcendental idealism (Guyer (ed.) (2010), Allais (2010)), work on the Metaphysics of Morals, virtue and Right (Baxley (2010), Byrd and Hruschka (2010)), and a Critical Guide on the second Critique.

- Guyer (ed.) (2010), *The Cambridge Companion to Kant's Critique of Pure Reason*. A Cambridge Companion devoted to a single text, with seventeen chapters by an international team, arranged under the background, the arguments and the impact of the work. The opening chapters place the Critique against continental rationalism and British empiricism.
- Proops (2010), *Kant's First Paralogism*. Examines how, in the First Paralogism, transcendental illusion leads rational psychologists to the doctrine that the soul is a substance. Argues that Kant identifies the source of the fallacy as a confusion about the nature of conceivability.
- Allais (2010), *Kant's Argument for Transcendental Idealism in the Transcendental Aesthetic*. Reads the argument of the Transcendental Aesthetic as turning on how synthetic a priori propositions can have objects, not on the source of their justification, against a common reading. Takes intuition to involve the presence to consciousness of the object represented, and concludes that a priori intuition cannot present a mind-independent feature of reality.
- Sturm and Wunderlich (2010), *Kant and the scientific study of consciousness*. Argues that Kant's views on consciousness, the mind-body problem and the status of psychology as a science differ from the way these topics are combined in present debates about a science of consciousness. Kant did not use the concept of consciousness for phenomenal qualia, his mind-body problem concerns substances, and he required quantification rather than mechanistic explanation of psychology.
- Baxley (2010), *Kant's Theory of Virtue: The Value of Autocracy*. Argues that Kantian virtue is to be understood as autocracy, a form of moral self-government in which reason rules sensibility. Holds that the moral psychology of the later ethical works, including the duty to cultivate certain emotional states, yields a modern, egalitarian conception of virtue that differs from Aristotelian virtue ethics.
- Reath and Timmermann (eds.) (2010), *Kant's Critique of Practical Reason: A Critical Guide*. A Critical Guide with essays on the principal arguments of the second Critique and their relation to the critical philosophy as a whole. The topics are the genesis of the work, the authority of the moral law as a fact of reason, the metaphysics of free agency, respect as the moral motive, and the primacy of practical reason and the postulates.
- Denis (ed.) (2010), *Kant's Metaphysics of Morals: A Critical Guide*. A Critical Guide with essays on the Metaphysics of Morals (Doctrine of Right and Doctrine of Virtue), treating its relation to Kant's earlier works and themes such as right and virtue, property, punishment and moral feeling. The NDPR review notes a chapter that questions Allen Wood's claim that Kant derives duties mainly from the Formula of Humanity rather than the Formula of Universal Law.
- Pallikkathayil (2010), *Deriving Morality from Politics: Rethinking the Formula of Humanity*. Argues against Korsgaard's and O'Neill's interpretation of what it is to treat someone merely as a means under the Formula of Humanity. Develops an alternative on which the content of many moral duties depends on the results of political philosophy and of actual political decision making.
- Byrd and Hruschka (2010), *Kant's Doctrine of Right: A Commentary*. A commentary that analyses Kant's system of individual rights, from the innate right to external freedom to property, contract and family claims. It explains his terminology, method and ideas in light of his intellectual environment, including Achenwall's natural-law text. The SEP entry on Kant's social and political philosophy discusses the authors' reading of Kant as favouring representative democracy.
- Flikschuh (2010), *Kant's Sovereignty Dilemma: A Contemporary Analysis*. Discusses the distinction between international and cosmopolitan Right as elements of a complete Kantian theory of global justice, and the dilemma that Kant's theory of Right both requires and prohibits the juridical compulsion of states. (Summary based on a Wiley Online Library page snippet.)
- Crowther (2010), *The Kantian Aesthetic: From Knowledge to the Avant-Garde*. Explains the kind of perceptual knowledge involved in aesthetic judgments by linking Kant's aesthetics to a critically upgraded account of his theory of knowledge. Shows that such judgments must involve categories and fundamental schemata.
- Papadaki (2010), *Kantian Marriage and Beyond: Why It Is Worth Thinking about Kant on Marriage*. Argues that Kant's account of monogamous marriage as the only relationship in which sexual use is compatible with humanity survives the charges of being flawed, paradoxical and at odds with feminism. Drawing on Kant's conception of friendship, suggests that Kant may have overlooked another context in which sex is morally permissible.
- Heis (2010), *"Critical philosophy begins at the very point where logistic leaves off": Cassirer's Response to Frege and Russell*. Traces the reception of Frege's and Russell's logic and logicism among the Marburg Neo-Kantians. Natorp and Cassirer reject their conception of logic on the basis of a Marburg philosophy of logic taken from Cohen, while Cassirer sees the logic as vindicating some Neo-Kantian theses about space, time and mathematical proof.

## 2011

The 2011 entries address nonconceptual content and animal consciousness (Hanna (2011), McLear (2011)), Groundwork commentary, dignity and a German-English edition (Allison (2011)), Kant, race, geography and cosmopolitanism (Kleingeld (2011)), and constructivism against realism.

- Kitcher (2011), *Kant's Thinker*. Examines the Critique of Pure Reason's account of cognition and its relation to self-consciousness. Argues that Kant's theory of the interplay between self-consciousness and consciousness of objects handles the self-ascription of beliefs and the tracking of objects over time better than more recent theories, and can suggest avenues for cognitive science. NDPR places the book in the Strawsonian tradition while noting disagreements with Strawson.
- Hanna (2011), *Beyond the Myth of the Myth: A Kantian Theory of Non-Conceptual Content*. Argues that a broadly Kantian strategy for explaining the existence, semantic structure and psychological function of essentially non-conceptual content also yields a bottom-up theory of the foundations of rationality in minded animals. On this theory, non-conceptual content is the substructure from which Sellars's logical space of reasons grows. The same journal issue carries a conceptualist reply by Bowman.
- McLear (2011), *Kant on Animal Consciousness*. Argues that Kant's claims about animal representation and consciousness do not rule out objective perceptual consciousness in animals. A nonconceptualist reading can endorse this while denying that animals can take cognitive attitudes towards, and self-ascribe, their own representational states. Addresses the problem that the conceptualist reading raises for non-rational animals.
- Schulting and Verburgt (eds.) (2011), *Kant's Idealism: New Interpretations of a Controversial Doctrine*. Collects essays on Kant's doctrine of idealism, including contributions by Ameriks and Baum, and treating the relation between Kant's logic and idealism, the thing in itself and the chapter on the Transcendental Ideal. It includes an essay by Allais on transcendental idealism and the transcendental deduction. (Summary based on a publisher description.)
- Allison (2011), *Kant's Groundwork for the Metaphysics of Morals: A Commentary*. A commentary on the Groundwork (1785) that pays attention to the structure of the work and to its historical and intellectual context, covering the whole text including the deduction in the third chapter. SEP's entry on Kant's moral philosophy cites the book on the claim that the formulas of the categorical imperative generate the same duties.
- Sensen (2011), *Kant on Human Dignity*. Studies all passages in which Kant uses the term dignity and the main arguments in the Kant literature for a value of human beings. Argues that respect for human beings does not follow from human dignity, since that would violate autonomy, but is an unconditional imperative of reason, yielding a unified account of Kant's moral philosophy.
- Stern (2011), *Understanding Moral Obligation: Kant, Hegel, Kierkegaard*. Challenges the argument from autonomy by which Kant is said to have turned to constructivism, arguing that Kant did not hold it and that the threat to autonomy comes from the obligatoriness of morality. Traces how Kant (obligation located in ourselves), Hegel (in others) and Kierkegaard (in God) respond. NDPR reports that Stern argues Kant was a value realist.
- Kleingeld (2011), *Kant and Cosmopolitanism: The Philosophical Ideal of World Citizenship*. Presents an account of Kant's cosmopolitanism (moral, political, legal, economic, cultural and psychological aspects), contrasting it with the views of his German contemporaries and arguing that it changed in the mid-1790s into a philosophically stronger theory. Discusses patriotism, states, an international federation, cultural pluralism, race and global economic justice. The North American Kant Society prize-winners page lists it as the 2014 Book Prize winner.
- Ginsborg (2011), *Primitive Normativity and Skepticism about Rules*. Invokes Kant's account of aesthetic judgment as the basis for a response to the skepticism about rules and meaning that Kripke attributes to Wittgenstein. Kant is the source of the proposed response rather than the sole subject of the paper. (Summary based on the SEP description; the retrieved abstract text covers only the article's opening on Kripke's argument.)
- DiCenso (2011), *Kant, Religion, and Politics*. Examines the place of religion in Kant's principal writings, arguing that his theory of religion emerges from his epistemology, ethics and political theory. Argues that it serves projects of restructuring institutions and moving political attitudes towards greater autonomy, against the view that Kant reduces religion to moral lessons.
- Louden (2011), *Kant's Human Being: Essays on His Theory of Human Nature*. Collects essays on Kant's empirical work on human nature and its connection to his ethical theory, analysing Kant's answer to the question "What is the human being?". Its parts treat virtue and human nature, Kant's popular lecture courses in anthropology, geography and education, and national character. The NDPR review comments on the closing treatment of Kant's remarks on national character.
- Elden and Mendieta (eds.) (2011), *Reading Kant's Geography*. Collects an introductory essay by Elden and seventeen essays in five parts on Kant's lectures on physical geography. The volume presents the lectures as a source for his thinking on anthropology, race theory, space and time, history, the environment and the public. (Summary based on a publisher description.)
- Massimi (2011), *Kant's dynamical theory of matter in 1755, and its debt to speculative Newtonian experimentalism*. Explores the scientific sources of Kant's early dynamical theory of matter in the Universal Natural History and On Fire (1755). Argues that speculative Newtonian experimentalism (from Newton's Opticks through Boerhaave's Elementa chemiae and Hales's Vegetable Staticks), rather than the Principia, was central, qualifying the picture of 1755 as Kant's conversion to Newton.
- Rockmore (2011), *Kant and Phenomenology*. Argues that phenomenology originates in Kant, whose second-edition constructivist theory of knowledge challenged representational epistemology. Holds that phenomenology is a form of constructivism traced through Plato, Kant and Hegel rather than through Husserl. The NDPR review objects that the constructivist reading does not engage rival interpretations such as Allison's dual-aspect theory.
- Kant (2011), *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals: A German-English Edition*. An edition/translation, edited by Gregor and Timmermann, that presents an edited German text alongside a revised version of Mary Gregor's translation of the Groundwork, with editorial notes and a bilingual index. Per NDPR, the 1786 second edition is the base text.

## 2012

The 2012 entries include conceptualist and two-aspect readings (Griffith (2012), Allison (2012)), the Cambridge Edition volumes *Natural Science* and *Lectures on Anthropology* (Kant (2012a), Kant (2012b)), reception in German idealism and early analytic philosophy, and political and moral essays.

- Ameriks (2012), *Kant's Elliptical Path*. Collects essays tracing the stages and key concepts of Kant's Critical philosophy from the early 1760s to the 1790s as a defence of the conception of humanity's fundamental end that Kant reached after reading Rousseau. Includes essays on each Critique, a defence of Ameriks's "moderate" interpretation of transcendental idealism, and chapters on will and the fact of reason, theodicy, religion and post-Kantian developments.
- Allison (2012), *Essays on Kant*. Collects seventeen previously published essays plus an addendum, covering epistemology, metaphysics, moral theory, teleology, political philosophy, philosophy of history and religion. The essays centre on transcendental idealism, freedom of the will and the purposiveness of nature, with a comparative dimension relating Kant to predecessors, successors and present-day philosophers. (Continues the two-aspect line of 2011: Schulting and Verburgt (eds.) (2011).)
- Chignell (2012), *Kant, Real Possibility, and the Threat of Spinoza*. Reconstructs Kant's pre-critical existence proof and the theory of modality behind his departure from Leibniz. Argues that the being it yields resembles Spinoza's extended natura naturans more than a personal creator-God, and that although transcendental idealism lets Kant avoid this conclusion in the critical period, one version of the Spinozistic threat remains.
- Griffith (2012), *Perception and the Categories: A Conceptualist Reading of Kant's Critique of Pure Reason*. Argues against the nonconceptualist readings of Kant given by Hanna and Allais and, from the A and B Transcendental Deductions, for a conceptualist reading. Because Kant's notion of empirical intuition makes essential reference to the categories, no empirical intuition can be given in sensibility independently of the understanding and its categories. (Continues the conceptualism/nonconceptualism debate of 2011: Hanna (2011), McLear (2011).)
- Friedman (2012), *Kant on geometry and spatial intuition*. Uses recent work on Kant and diagrammatic reasoning to reconsider aspects of Kant's philosophy of geometry and its relation to spatial intuition, in particular the relations between geometrical concepts and their schemata and between pure and empirical intuition. The SEP entry on Kant's philosophy of mathematics records that Friedman argues against diagrammatic interpretations of Kantian intuition. (Summary based on a PhilPapers snippet.)
- Hill (2012), *Virtue, Rules, and Justice: Kantian Aspirations*. Collects essays interpreting and extending Kant's moral theory, introducing themes of Kantian ethics and applying them to questions about revolution, prison reform and forcible humanitarian intervention. The NDPR review notes that all but one of the fifteen chapters were previously published.
- Sensen (ed.) (2012), *Kant on Moral Autonomy*. Collects essays on Kant's conception of autonomy, its history, its influence on contemporary conceptions and its moral significance, with an introduction by the editor and a postscript by Onora O'Neill. The NDPR review counts fourteen essays offered in honour of O'Neill.
- Ellis (ed.) (2012), *Kant's Political Theory: Interpretations and Applications*. Collects nine essays that aim to introduce Kant's political thought and to show the fruitfulness of a broadly Kantian approach. Five (O'Neill, Ripstein, Pogge, Hodgson, Taylor) read the Doctrine of Right and political essays, and four (Ferguson, Hunter, LaVaque-Manty, Laursen) draw on Kant's other writings or historical context. (Summary based on the NDPR review.)
- Teufel (2012), *What Does Kant Mean by 'Power of Judgement' in his Critique of the Power of Judgement?*. Argues that the power of judgement in the title of the third Critique refers only to the reflecting power of judgement, because Kant attempts a Critical justification of that power alone. Argues that Kant's discussion in sections 76-77 addresses the problem of synthesizing unsynthesized intuitions, so that the systematic ambitions of the third Critique are appropriate.
- Gray (2012), *Kant's Race Theory, Forster's Counter, and the Metaphysics of Color*. Argues that Kant's race theory is part of his idea of nature in the Critique of the Power of Judgment, examining the 1785-86 debate between Kant and Georg Forster over race. Shows that Kant developed his teleological conception of nature partly to give his race theory a unifying basis that could withstand Forster's empiricism. (Continues the Kant-and-race discussion of 2011: Kleingeld (2011), Louden (2011), Elden and Mendieta (eds.) (2011).)
- Parsons (2012), *From Kant to Husserl: Selected Essays*. The first of two volumes collecting Parsons's interpretive work: essays on Kant's philosophy of arithmetic, the relation of his arithmetical concepts to the categories, early reactions by Schultz and Bolzano, the first Critique and the Metaphysical Foundations of Natural Science, and a commentary on the Transcendental Aesthetic. These are followed by essays on Frege, Brentano and Husserl.
- Förster (2012), *The Twenty-Five Years of Philosophy: A Systematic Reconstruction*. Follows the Kantian idea that philosophy can be scientific and complete from the Critique of Pure Reason (1781) to Hegel (1806). Argues that Kant's three Critiques yield a distinction between intellectual intuition and intuitive understanding, which explains two post-Kantian lines of development, Fichte's Wissenschaftslehre and Goethe's transformation of intuitive understanding in light of Spinoza, that are joined in Hegel.
- Sedgwick (2012), *Hegel's Critique of Kant: From Dichotomy to Identity*. Presents Hegel's critique of Kant's theoretical philosophy, arguing that Hegel offers a critique of and alternative to the conception of cognition Kant defended in the critical period. Explores Hegel's claim to derive from Kantian doctrines the clues to a superior form of idealism.
- Kant (2012a), *Lectures on Anthropology*. An edition/translation, edited by Louden and Wood, of selections from student transcriptions of Kant's anthropology lectures of 1772-1789, which precede the published Anthropology from a Pragmatic Point of View (1798). It presents Anthropology Friedländer (1772) and Anthropology Mrongovius (1786) in full, with selections from the other transcriptions and parts of the Menschenkunde, and shows Kant's empirical theory of human nature.
- Kant (2012b), *Natural Science*. An edition/translation in the Cambridge Edition of the Works of Immanuel Kant, edited by Eric Watkins, of Kant's early natural-science writings. It includes Thoughts on the True Estimation of Living Forces, the Universal Natural History and Theory of the Heavens (1755) and the whole of Physical Geography, with editorial introductions, explanatory notes and a glossary. NDPR notes that some texts appear in English for the first time.
