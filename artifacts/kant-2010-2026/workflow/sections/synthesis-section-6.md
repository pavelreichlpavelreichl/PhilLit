## 2022

The 2022 entries include metaphysical readings of Kant's idealism (Schafer and Stang (eds.) (2022)), work on the Opus postumum (Basile and Lyssy (2022)), Kant and race and labour (Lu-Adler (2022), Pascoe (2022)), Kant and artificial intelligence, a Critical Guide to the *Metaphysical Foundations*, and a critical edition of the *Träume*.

- Schafer and Stang (eds.) (2022), *The Sensible and Intelligible Worlds: New Essays on Kant's Metaphysics and Epistemology*. Collects essays on the metaphysical presuppositions of Kant's philosophy and on how they square with his critique of dogmatic metaphysics, set against an earlier tendency in analytic Kant scholarship to read him as anti-metaphysical. The chapters draw on Leibnizian and Aristotelian backgrounds and on links to German Idealism, and cover transcendental idealism, modal metaphysics, the continuum, the epistemology of the a priori and the foundations of Kant's metaethical views. (Continues the metaphysical-reading line: Jauernig (2021), Willaschek (2018).)
- Gomes et al. (2022), *On the Necessity of the Categories*. Asks whether Kant leaves open the possibility of discursive cognizers whose categories differ from ours, as he leaves open creatures with different pure forms of sensibility. Argues that textual and systematic considerations do not settle the question, and that Kant's wider views on the limits of our knowledge of mind allow him to stay neutral, within unexpected constraints. The result is an asymmetry between other forms of sensibility and other categories, in which Kantian humility takes a distinctive character.
- Pendlebury (2022), *The Shape of the Kantian Mind*. Argues that conceptualist and nonconceptualist readers alike wrongly assume that concepts are involved in intuition only as particular concepts applied in particular exercises of sensibility. On the author's view no concepts are applied in exercises of sensibility, yet the understanding is teleologically internal to sensibility, whose exercises are directed to providing objects to which the categories apply. The picture rests on a distinction between capacities and acts and is said to be demanded by Kant's account of a priori knowledge in the Transcendental Deduction. (Continues the conceptualism debate: McLear (2016), Conant (2016), Watkins and Willaschek (2017).)
- McNulty (ed.) (2022), *Kant's Metaphysical Foundations of Natural Science: A Critical Guide*. A Critical Guide to the 1786 *Metaphysical Foundations*, which accounts for an acting-at-a-distance gravitational force, demonstrates the infinite divisibility of matter and derives analogues to Newton's laws of motion. Its chapters treat matter, motion, the mathematization of nature, space and natural laws, relate the metaphysics of nature to the critical philosophy, and describe the historical context of the work. The volume presents the text as under-studied. (Relates to Friedman (2013).)
- Wilson (2022), *Kant and the Naturalistic Turn of 18th Century Philosophy*. Argues that the critical philosophy was meant as a bulwark against radical naturalism (determinism, atheism, materialism) associated with the radical Enlightenment of Locke, La Mettrie, Buffon, Hume, Herder and others, while leaving room for empirical work in physics, chemistry and the life sciences. Presents Kant as no more than a proponent of a moderate Enlightenment and holds that, on topics such as science, mind-body relations, animals, women and non-Europeans, the more progressive positions were held by his opponents. The NDPR reviewer, Fugate, describes the book as a sharp attack on Kant scholarship and on Kant himself.
- Timmons (2022), *Kant's Doctrine of Virtue: A Guide*. A reader's guide to the Doctrine of Virtue, Part II of Kant's 1797 *Metaphysics of Morals*, in five parts. It covers the background and the general introduction to the *Metaphysics of Morals*, then the introduction to the Doctrine of Virtue (ends that are also duties, radical evil and the nature of virtue, the science of ethics), duties to oneself, duties of love, vices and friendship, and finally moral education and practice. (Relates to Trampota et al. (eds.) (2013) on the Doctrine of Virtue.)
- Herman (2022), *Kantian Commitments: Essays on Moral Theory and Practice*. Ten essays that rethink fundamentals of Kant's ethics. Part One revisits the formulas of the categorical imperative, the idea of making exceptions, and the contrast between Kant's project and other deontologies, especially recent contractualism, reading Kant as giving a theory of moral reasoning with standards of validity and soundness. Part Two takes up Kant's moralized view of history, social pluralism, moral psychology and normal human development, the argument about duties to animals, and the highest good in a morally good life.
- Kim and Schönecker (eds.) (2022), *Kant and Artificial Intelligence*. Asks how artificial intelligence, and the strong claims of its philosophical advocates, look from a Kantian perspective, and conversely what AI and its functions can teach about the validity claims of Kantian philosophy. The volume treats the self, spirit and self-consciousness, ethics, law and aesthetics.
- Pascoe (2022), *Kant's Theory of Labour*. A Cambridge Element that examines Kant's account of labour in his political philosophy and develops an intersectional analysis of Kant. Argues that his treatments of slavery, citizenship and sex developed in linked ways, so that his account of independence is built on his theory of labour and is continuous with his anthropological accounts of race and gender, giving a systematic justification for the dependency of women and non-whites. Adds that Kant's eventual rejection of slavery may owe more to his changing conception of labour than to his theory of race. (Continues the gender-and-citizenship discussion: Sabourin (2021).)
- Stone and Hasan (2022), *What Is Provisional Right?*. Resolves the apparent contradiction in Kant's claim that property claims are possible but merely provisional in the state of nature and conclusive only in a civil condition. Argues that provisional right is not a special kind of right but marks the imperfection of an action, that of acquiring ordinary rights, where public authorization is lacking. On this reading it works as a methodological device in a sequential exposition of the moral basis of public law, developed from Kant's two-step account of having and acquiring. (Relates to Byrd and Hruschka (2010) on private right.)
- Geiger (2022), *Kant and the Claims of the Empirical World: A Transcendental Reading of the Critique of the Power of Judgment*. Argues that a principal concern of the third Critique is to complete the account of the transcendental conditions of empirical experience and knowledge, covering Kant's analysis of natural beauty and his teleological judgments of organisms and of nature generally, so that the third Critique forms a unified whole. The NDPR review adds that the thread of the reading is the principle of the logical purposiveness of nature, and notes that some of its claims are controversial. (Relates to Ypi (2021) on purposiveness.)
- Lu-Adler (2022), *Kant on Lazy Savagery, Racialized*. Argues that Kant develops a concept of savagery, partly characterized by laziness, for a programme of human progress, and racializes it by treating native Americans as literal savages with a hereditary physiological laziness. Because perfection must be reached through industry, which Kant denies to the supposed savages, the racialization blocks any prospect of their fulfilling their perfections despite their having the same germs as Europeans. The case is said to bear on the debated relation between Kant's moral universalism and his racism. (Continues the race debate: Allais (2016), Sandford (2018).)
- Rosen (2022), *The Shadow of God: Kant, Hegel, and the Passage from Heaven to History*. Argues that many apparently secular modern ideas were shaped by religion, with the German Idealists as the thinkers seeking to reconcile faith and reason. For Kant, pursuing the moral law means working with others in an invisible church toward justice in history; later Idealists, dropping personal immortality, put historical immortality through social projects at the centre. The NDPR review calls the book a display of erudition and finds in it in effect two books.
- Basile and Lyssy (2022), *Perspectives on Kant's Opus postumum*. An edited volume on the theoretical elements of the Opus postumum, which it reads as central to the relation between Kant's transcendental philosophy and his natural philosophy, practical philosophy, philosophy of religion and metaphysics, and as showing Kant correcting and developing the critical philosophy. Chapters address the transition from the *Metaphysical Foundations* to physics, the ether and its transcendental deduction, self-affection and the self-positing of the subject, and the idea of God in the highest standpoint of transcendental philosophy. (Continues the transition-project discussion: Hall (2014), Thorndike (2017), Howard (2019).)
- Kant (2022), *Träume eines Geistersehers, erläutert durch Träume der Metaphysik. Historisch-kritische Edition*. A German-language edition of Kant's 1766 *Dreams of a Spirit-Seer*, with introduction and notes by Kreimendahl and Oberhausen (editors as named in web search results for the Meiner edition). The publisher describes it as presenting the text in historical-critical form, with German translations of the Swedenborg passages Kant refers to and commentary linking the treatise to contemporary philosophy and to Kant's pre-critical and critical works. The treatise is read as turning a critique of Swedenborg into a rejection of traditional metaphysics and as already containing basic features of Kant's moral philosophy.

## 2023

The 2023 entries include a book-length treatment of Kant, race and racism (Lu-Adler (2023)), work on freedom and rational agency (Kohl (2023)), the formulas of the categorical imperative (Fahmy (2023), Kleingeld (2023)), the unity of the third Critique and natural history, the late philosophy of nature, sex, love and friendship, and Heidegger's reading of Kant.

- Schafer (2023), *Kant's Reason: The Unity of Reason and the Limits of Comprehension in Kant*. Develops an interpretation of Kant's conception of reason on which theoretical and practical reason are two manifestations of one capacity for theoretical and practical understanding, which the book calls comprehension, both governed by a version of the Principle of Sufficient Reason and both essentially autonomous. The resulting position, rational constitutivism, treats neither substantive reasons or values nor merely structural rationality as fundamental. It contrasts with reasons fundamentalism and with the agency-first constitutivism that has dominated Kantian metaethics. (Follows Schafer (2019) and Schafer (2021).)
- Stang (2023), *Kant's Schematism of the Categories: An Interpretation and Defence*. Reconstructs the argument of the Schematism chapter of the *Critique of Pure Reason*, whose task is to solve the problem of the inhomogeneity of intuitions and categories. The reconstruction rests on two premises, Subsumption Sufficiency and Real Possibility, which with a modal link entail that objects can be subsumed under categories. The author reads transcendental schemata as time-determinations, understood as rules for reflexively representing the temporal relations among our own representational states, and concludes that on this reading the argument is sound.
- Cooper (2023), *Kant and the Transformation of Natural History*. A study of Kant's account of natural history that argues that Kant contributed to the introduction of time into the frame in which explanations are sought and justified in natural science. It challenges the claim that his theory of science rules out a developmental conception of nature, argues that it supplies a method by which natural historians can dispute historical claims, and uses that method to expose flaws in Kant's own historical reasoning, including the formation and defence of his racist views. The NDPR review says the book rejects the standard reading on which natural history was an early interest later sidelined by the critical philosophy. (Has links to the race debate: Sandford (2018).)
- Kohl (2023), *Kant on Freedom and Rational Agency*. Interprets Kant's doctrine of freedom on the view that absolute transcendental freedom, or rational autonomy, is the necessary presupposition of all norm-governed human agency in its moral, epistemic and aesthetic dimensions. The doctrine emerges as a critique of a naturalistic worldview, culminating in the argument that naturalistic cognizers must presuppose freedom in devising rationally valid theories. The NDPR review reports that the book argues against non-metaphysical readings of the practical standpoint and takes moral freedom to depend on the freedom of cognitive faculties. (Relates to Allison (2020) on freedom.)
- Fahmy (2023), *Never Merely as a Means: Rethinking the Role and Relevance of Consent*. Argues that consent-based criteria for using another merely as a means, built over several decades on the Groundwork false-promising example, yield counter-intuitive and un-Kantian verdicts in relatively simple cases. Holds that the example involves a consent-sensitive duty and so encourages too narrow an understanding, since the prohibition covers both consent-sensitive and consent-insensitive duties. Offers a duty-based account that reflects this. (Continues the humanity-formula discussion: Kleingeld (2020), Pallikkathayil (2010).)
- Kleingeld (2023), *A Defense and Development of the Volitional Self-Contradiction Interpretation of Kant's Formula of Universal Law*. Replies to comments by Timmons, Walschots, Romero and Lo Re on the author's 2017 reading of the Formula of Universal Law as requiring that one can will, without volitional self-contradiction, both the maxim as one's own action principle and its becoming a universal law. The reply expands the interpretation, holding that Kant also constructs the diagnostic contradiction between willing a maxim to become a universal law and willing what finite rational beings necessarily will, namely the means to their actual and possible ends. It contrasts the view with the Practical Contradiction and Logical Contradiction interpretations. (Follows Kleingeld (2017).)
- Davies (2023), *Kant on Civil Self-Sufficiency*. Argues that Kant attributes civil self-sufficiency, the qualification for active citizenship and political participation, to those who are not under the authority of any private individual for their survival. The author holds this reading to be more textually grounded than the dominant reading, which understands self-sufficiency in terms of economic relations alone. Adds that Kant's interest in relations of authority reflects a concern to eliminate certain forms of corruption in public lawgiving. (Relates to Pascoe (2022) on passive citizenship.)
- Sweet (2023), *Kant on Freedom, Nature, and Judgment: The Territory of the Third Critique*. Treats the *Critique of Judgment* as examining an independent sphere of human life rather than as serving practical or theoretical interests, and argues that it answers the question what we may hope for. The answer lies in Kant's account of territory, a region of experience that underlies and mediates between freedom and nature. The NDPR reviewer notes that commentators have tended to treat the third Critique as two projects, aesthetics and philosophy of biology, and that the literature stressing its unity is sparse. (Relates to Geiger (2022) on the unity of the third Critique.)
- Clewis (2023), *The Origins of Kant's Aesthetics*. Examines the sources and development of Kant's aesthetics through his publications, correspondence, handwritten notes and university lectures, in eight chapters on aesthetic judgment and normativity, formal beauty, partly conceptual beauty, genius, the fine arts, the sublime, ugliness and disgust, and humor. Considers how Kant's thought was shaped by authors including Wolff, Baumgarten, Meier, Mendelssohn, Sulzer, Herder, Hutcheson, Hume, Burke, Home, Batteux, Rousseau and Voltaire.
- Chignell (2023), *Demoralization and Hope: A Psychological Reading of Kant's Moral Argument*. Examines three varieties of Kant's moral proof under the doctrine of the primacy of the practical, and argues that the third, a moral-psychological argument based on the need to sustain moral hope and avoid demoralization, has advantages over the others. It starts from a premise that coheres better with Kant's mature account of moral motivation and invokes empirical-psychological theses to support a commitment to the full-blown classical deity. (Relates to Höwing (ed.) (2016) on moral faith.)
- Lu-Adler (2023), *Kant, Race, and Racism: Views from Somewhere*. Challenges the assumptions that Kant's racism is personal prejudice that can be disentangled from his core philosophy and that it contradicts his moral theory. Argues that Kant's raciology, divided into racialism and racism, is integral to his philosophical system, and treats racism as an ideological formation in which Kant took part as a scholar who developed a scientific theory of race and as an educator who transmitted denigrating depictions of racialized others. Urges Kant scholars and teachers to engage in antiracist work in a color-conscious way. (Continues Lu-Adler (2022), as the note states, and the race debate.)
- Rinne and Brecher (eds.) (2023), *Kant on Sex, Love, and Friendship*. Collects essays on how sex, love and friendship function in Kant's philosophy, individually and in conjunction. The essays examine the theory of sexuality, marriage (including same-sex marriage), morality and sexual objectification, love and autonomy, love of human beings, the conceptual structure of love, friendship, misanthropy and the highest good. The volume sets this against what it calls common prejudices about Kant's contribution to these topics. (Relates to Papadaki (2010) and Varden (2020).)
- Howard (2023), *Kant's Late Philosophy of Nature*. An Element, according to its abstract, on the Opus postumum, Kant's final drafts attempting a transition from the metaphysical foundations of natural science to physics. It outlines a framework combining the systematic ambition of early twentieth-century readings with the rigor of recent studies, sets aside the question which gap in the earlier philosophy the project was meant to fill, and directs attention to fascicles X and XI and to the arrival point of the transition, Kant's question what physics is. (Follows Howard (2019) and relates to Basile and Lyssy (2022).)
- Lambeth (2023), *Heidegger's Interpretation of Kant: The Violence and the Charity*. Gives an account of Heidegger's method of interpreting Kant, arguing that it is more promising than usually recognized, a failure of recognition that has limited its uptake in anglophone Kant scholarship. On this account Heidegger locates Kant's insights in moments of tension where Kant struggles to put a fresh point into words, so that the interpreter must disentangle competing strands of argument and decide which to favour. The book traces this method through Heidegger's reading of the *Critique of Pure Reason*, situates it against other post-Kantian readings, and shows how the engagement bears on Heidegger's own views. (Relates to Golob (2013), Engelland (2017).)
- Baiasu and Timmons (2023), *The Kantian Mind*. An edited reference volume (CrossRef lists the two editors as authors) in the Routledge Philosophical Minds series, with forty-five chapters in four parts: Background to the Critical Philosophy, Transcendental Philosophy (Critique and Doctrine), Posthumous Writings and Lectures, and Kant and Contemporary Kantians. Besides the main works it covers Kant's views on logic, mathematics, the natural sciences, anthropology, religion, politics and education, and his influence on contemporary analytic and continental philosophy; each chapter has suggestions for further reading. (Relates to Wuerth (ed.) (2021) on reference works.) (Summary based on a publisher description.) (Dated by its CrossRef print or online date; see YEAR NOTE.)

## 2024

In the tercentenary year of Kant's birth, the 2024 entries include the *Oxford Handbook of Kant* (Gomes and Stephenson (eds.) (2024)), imagination and intuition (Matherne (2024)), dignity and Kant's racism (Ameriks (2024)), race, radical evil and the Haitian Revolution (Papish (2024)), Baumgarten and practical philosophy, two-aspect arguments from Kant's language, and a source-materials volume for the second Critique.

- Smyth (2024), *Intuition in Kant: The Boundlessness of Sense*. Offers an overview of Kant's conception of intuition in all its species (divine, receptive, sensible and human). Kant treats sense perception as the paradigm of intuition yet holds that infinities can be represented in intuition; the book argues that the features Kant ascribes to intuition remedy specific cognitive shortcomings arising from the discursivity of our intellect, so that intuition acts as the intellect's cognitive partner. It reconstructs the role of this conception in Kant's philosophy of mind, epistemology and philosophy of mathematics. (Relates to Allais (2010) on the Transcendental Aesthetic.)
- Rosefeldt (2024), *"In Itself": A New Investigation of Kant's Adverbial Wording of Transcendental Idealism*. Investigates the linguistic forms in which Kant expresses transcendental idealism, arguing that Prauss's case for reading *in itself* as an adverbial expression modifying verbs of philosophical reflection is flawed, so that exegetical evidence for methodological two-aspect interpretations is poor. Kant's adverbial uses of *in itself* largely reduce to a standard use modifying verbs of predicative being such as the copula. The paper also discusses how this fits two-object and ontological two-aspect interpretations. (Continues the two-aspect versus two-object debate: Stang (2014), Marshall (2013).)
- Matherne (2024), *Seeing More: Kant's Theory of Imagination*. Defends a systematic interpretation of Kant's theory of imagination, on which imagination is a flexible cognitive capacity exercised as much in relation to real and present objects as to absent or unreal ones. The book traces its exercise in perception and ordinary experience, in the appreciation of beauty and sublimity, in art, and in the pursuit of happiness and of morality, and holds that one unified theory underlies these uses. The NDPR review describes it as answering what imagination is for Kant, against skeptics who assign it little weight in his system. (Relates to Horstmann (2018) on imagination.)
- Russell (2024), *Kant's Fantasy*. Reconstructs Kant's conception of fantasy (Phantasie), the unintentional, unstructured, obscure and pleasurable imaginative activity that his anthropology describes and criticizes, and asks whether it might play a positive role in experience. Kant distinguishes the lawless freedom of imagination in fantasy from the free lawfulness of imagination in aesthetic judgment. Departing from Kant and drawing on Susan Sontag, the paper argues that certain modernist works invite fantasy, so that fantasy belongs to proper aesthetic appreciation while remaining normatively ambiguous.
- Guyer (2024a), *Kant's Impact on Moral Philosophy*. Traces the response to the moral philosophy of Kant's Groundwork, second Critique, *Religion* and *Metaphysics of Morals* from Fichte, Schelling and Hegel through T. H. Green, Josiah Royce and Nietzsche to Rawls, O'Neill, Korsgaard and Parfit. It argues that the responses that develop Kant's ideal of maximal but equal freedom as the inner worth of the world answer charges of empty formalism and of leaving no room for happiness, for responsibility for evil or for moral feeling.
- Ameriks (2024), *Kantian Dignity and its Difficulties*. A systematic and historical study of Kant's conception of dignity. It defends dignity as rooted in a basic capacity of reason for morality and as an unconditional, inviolable feature of all human beings that deserves universal respect, then examines how the notion was clarified, misunderstood or neglected in five contexts (the Baltics and Herder, Berlin and the rise of Fascism, Philadelphia, London and Richard Price, Washington and Thomas Mann), arguing that Kant held racist and anti-Semitic prejudices and a general old-world elitism in conflict with his own principles. (Relates to Sensen (2011) on dignity.)
- Vilhauer (2024), *Kant on Rational Sympathy*. An Element explaining Kant's distinction between rational sympathy, regulated by practical reason and necessary for adopting as our own those ends of others that are contingent from the standpoint of practical rationality, and natural sympathy, which is passive and can prompt affect and dispose us to act wrongly. On the account given, sympathy is a function of a posteriori productive imagination, and in rational sympathy we freely use the imagination to step into the perspectives of others. (Relates to Ware (2014) and Sorensen and Williamson (eds.) (2018) on moral feeling.)
- Guyer (2024b), *The Moral Foundation of Right*. An Element arguing that Kant's Universal Principle of Right follows straightforwardly from the foundational idea of his moral philosophy as a whole. Right is the coercively enforceable part of morality, contrasted with Ethics as the part that is not coercively enforceable, and the author sets this against those who have questioned whether Right is part of morality.
- Papish (2024), *Kant on race and the radical evil in the human species*. Reads the *Religion within the Boundaries of Mere Reason* alongside Kant's natural history writings on the concept of race and the development of the human races, a pairing that the article says the literature has neglected. Argues that this clarifies the claim of a radical evil in the human species: that evil is not a static property but a dynamic, changing defect in human nature that increasingly threatens aspirations to collective moral perfection. (Relates to Papish (2018), Merritt (2021) on radical evil, and Sandford (2018) on race and natural history.)
- Huseyinzadegan (2024), *Conjectures on Kant and the Haitian Revolution*. Puts forward, as a suspicion only, that Kant never thought Black lives had dignity but only price. Following Michel-Rolph Trouillot's argument that the Haitian Revolution was unthinkable for Enlightenment philosophers, it conjectures what Kant could or should have said about the revolution, drawing on his writings on the American and French Revolutions. If the suspicion is right, Kantianism cannot accommodate an antiracist programme, which the article says should change how racism, slavery and colonialism are discussed in Kant scholarship. (Takes the negative side of the systematic-link question: Sandford (2018), Mills (2014).)
- Fugate and Hymers (eds.) (2024), *Baumgarten and Kant on the Foundations of Practical Philosophy*. Thirteen chapters on the historical background and philosophical content of Baumgarten's 1760 *Elements* and its relation to Kant's work. The volume follows the editors' earlier *Kant and Baumgarten on Metaphysics* (2018) and builds on their English translations of Baumgarten's *Elements* and Kant's *Reflections on Moral Philosophy* (2020). It treats the Baumgarten connection as a neglected aspect of classical German philosophy. (Follows Fugate and Hymers (eds.) (2018).)
- Aigner (2024), *Kant and Technics: From the Critique of Pure Reason to the Opus Postumum*. Answers the claim, associated with Heidegger, Simondon and Stiegler, that Kant's philosophy is incapable of thinking technics. Argues that an explicit concept of technics is at work in Kant's philosophy and that, from the *Critique of Pure Reason* to the posthumously published *Opus Postumum*, transcendental philosophy is at once constituted against, reliant upon and proceeding from technics, so that Kant cannot be treated as a techno-oblivious thinker. (Relates to Kim and Schönecker (eds.) (2022) on technology and Howard (2023) on late work.)
- Kinzel (2024), *Neo-Kantian conceptualism: between scientific experience and everyday perception*. Reconstructs the transformations in the Marburg neo-Kantian account of experience by focusing on the problem of conceptualism, linking four issues in the Marburg philosophers' transcendental projects: the interpretation of Kant, the critique of experiential givenness, objective cognition in science, and the relation of scientific and pre-scientific experience. It identifies two shifts: from Cohen's conceptualist answer to subjectivism to Cassirer's functionalist answer to empiricism, and from Cassirer's conceptualism about scientific experience to his symbolic non-conceptualism about everyday perception. (Relates to Heis (2010), Biagioli (2020) on Marburg neo-Kantianism and Pendlebury (2022) on conceptualism.) (Dated by its CrossRef print or online date; see YEAR NOTE.)
- Walschots (2024), *Kant's Critique of Practical Reason: Background Source Materials*. An edition/translation volume of source materials: English translations of texts by Kant's predecessors and contemporaries that he read and responded to in the second Critique, together with several later reactions to it, in eleven chapters with editorial material on each author and text. The NDPR review lists selections from Wolff's German Ethics and Crusius's guide to living rationally, reviews of the Groundwork by Flatt, Tittel and Pistorius, and Wizenmann's 1787 essay, among others. The texts are translations of eighteenth-century sources, not Kant's own works. (Relates to Kant (2015).)
- Gomes and Stephenson (eds.) (2024), *The Oxford Handbook of Kant*. A handbook of commissioned essays that present where the study of Kant's philosophy stands in the twenty-first century, organized around Kant's four questions (what can I know, what ought I to do, what may I hope, what is the human being). CrossRef chapter records show chapters on transcendental idealism, logic, mathematics, the special sciences, autonomy and freedom, virtue, right, religion, evil, hope, education, race, and sex and gender. The entry's note places its publication in the tercentenary year (CrossRef print date 31 October 2024).
