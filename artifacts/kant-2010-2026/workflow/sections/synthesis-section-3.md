## 2013

The 2013 entries include journal articles on one-world (two-aspect) against two-world readings and on nonconceptualism (Marshall (2013), Tolley (2013)), work on the empirical and scientific side of Kant (Friedman (2013), Frierson (2013), Mensch (2013)), source texts on race, a commentary on the Doctrine of Virtue, and Kant-Kongress proceedings.

- Marshall (2013), *Kant's Appearances and Things in Themselves as Qua-Objects*. Proposes understanding the appearance/thing-in-itself distinction through an Aristotelian qua-object (following Kit Fine), which gives a sense in which appearances and things in themselves can be the same things while differing in many features. The proposal is offered to the one-world interpretation, which faces problems arising from the different features Kant assigns to the two. (Continues the two-aspect line: Schulting and Verburgt (eds.) (2011), Allison (2012).)
- Tolley (2013), *The Non-Conceptuality of the Content of Intuitions: A New Approach*. Argues that Kant takes intuitions to have content in the sense of Frege's Sinn (a mode of presentation of an object or property) and that this content is distinct in kind from the content of concepts. Holds that the conceptualism debate had stalled for lack of agreement on what content means, and shows how this nonconceptualist account answers objections to earlier nonconceptualist readings. (Continues the conceptualism debate: Hanna (2011), McLear (2011), Griffith (2012).)
- Friedman (2013), *Kant's Construction of Nature: A Reading of the Metaphysical Foundations of Natural Science*. Reads the Metaphysical Foundations of Natural Science (1786), reconstructing its main argument and explaining its relationship to Newton's Principia and to eighteenth-century scientific thinkers such as Euler and Lambert. Situating the text against Kant's pre-critical writings and the changes to the second edition of the Critique of Pure Reason, it argues for a perspective on the meaning and development of the critical philosophy as a whole.
- Grenberg (2013), *Kant's Defense of Common Moral Experience: A Phenomenological Account*. Argues that the substance of Kant's moral philosophy emerges from reflection on the common experience of the conflict between happiness and morality. Reading the Groundwork and the Critique of Practical Reason, it finds a phenomenological notion of experience in the Fact of Reason and gives feeling an epistemic role in grounding a priori morality, against readings that give feeling only a motivational role and against Fichtean readings. NDPR also reports an interpretation of the failure of the Groundwork III argument for moral obligation.
- Wuerth (2013), *Sense and Sensibility in Kant's Practical Agent: Against the Intellectualism of Korsgaard and Sidgwick*. Presents Kant's account of practical agency as embodied agency, drawing on his empirical-psychological and metaphysical descriptions, and argues against the intellectualized interpretations of Korsgaard and Sidgwick. On this reading the agent chooses from a reflective distance but not from the complete affective distance of reason alone, and its sensibility (sense, feeling, desire) belongs to the agent that chooses.
- Trampota et al. (eds.) (2013), *Kant's "Tugendlehre": A Comprehensive Commentary*. A running commentary on the Doctrine of Virtue of the Metaphysics of Morals by 18 scholars from Germany, Italy, Britain and the United States. It discusses topics such as suicide, truthfulness, moral perfection, beneficence, gratitude, sympathy, respect and friendship, as well as Kant's moral psychology. (Relates to Baxley (2010) and Denis (ed.) (2010) on the Doctrine of Virtue.)
- Hay (2013), *Kantianism, Liberalism, and Feminism: Resisting Oppression*. Argues that the moral and political frameworks of Kantianism and liberalism are indispensable for addressing the concerns of contemporary feminism. NDPR reports that the first chapters set out a Kantian feminist theory of oppression as group-specific, systemic harm and that the later chapters apply it to sexual oppression. The book is a constructive Kantian account rather than an exegesis, and NDPR raises questions of Kant interpretation and suggests giving the Doctrine of Right a larger role in it. (Continues the gender discussion: Papadaki (2010).)
- Mensch (2013), *Kant's Organicism: Epigenesis and the Development of Critical Philosophy*. Argues that epigenesis, a theory of biological formation, was not merely a metaphor for Kant but guided his critical philosophy, especially the relationship between reason and the categories of the understanding. Combines a study of Kant's theory of cognition with intellectual history and biography, placing the epigenesis of reason within eighteenth-century theories of generation, genealogy and classification. (Summary based on the publisher description.)
- Matherne (2013), *The Inclusive Interpretation of Kant's Aesthetic Ideas*. Offers an inclusive interpretation of the aesthetic ideas of the Critique of the Power of Judgment, on which they can present empirical concepts and emotions related to ordinary experience as well as moral and purely rational concepts, as restrictive readings hold. Argues that this neglected class matters for Kant's views of artistic production, of the experience of art and of the value of art for ordinary experience.
- Insole (2013), *Kant and the Creation of Freedom: A Theological Problem*. Argues that Kant struggled with how to conceive God's creative action in relation to human freedom and concluded that freedom can be protected only if God withdraws in certain ways from the created world. His rejection of the medieval and early modern account of divine concursus is said to shape transcendental idealism and his theory of freedom.
- Frierson (2013), *What Is the Human Being?*. An introduction to Kant's philosophy of human nature that explains three ways Kant addresses the question (the transcendental, the empirical and the pragmatic). It then considers later theorists of human nature, among them Hegel, Marx, Darwin, Nietzsche, Freud, Sartre, Heidegger, Foucault and E. O. Wilson, who challenge or enrich his views. (Relates to Louden (2011) and Kant (2012a) on anthropology and human nature.)
- Mikkelsen (ed.) (2013), *Kant and the Concept of Race: Late Eighteenth-Century Writings*. An edition/translation of Kant's essays on race, including the first race essay in its two versions of 1775 and 1777, presented in chronological order. It adds previously untranslated texts by four German contemporaries of Kant. (Continues the Kant-and-race discussion: Kleingeld (2011), Elden and Mendieta (eds.) (2011), Gray (2012).)
- Watkins (2013), *The Early Kant's (Anti-) Newtonianism*. Argues that the pre-critical Kant, besides using Newtonian principles in the Universal Natural History, developed a criticism of Newton based on metaphysical issues about God, with implications for how the formation and constitution of the heavenly bodies should be scientifically explained. The abstract adds that Kant remained interested in these issues in the Critical period.
- Golob (2013), *Heidegger on Kant, Time and the "Form" of Intentionality*. Analyses Heidegger's 1927/8 lecture course on the Critique of Pure Reason and his 1929 *Kant and the Problem of Metaphysics*. Argues that Heidegger's reading of Kant turns on two questions: how Heidegger's Kant understands the transcendental, answered through Cassam's model of a self-directed transcendental argument, and what role the horizon plays, answered through Kant's view that pure general logic abstracts from semantic content and Hume's attack on metaphysics.
- Bacin et al. (eds.) (2013), *Kant und die Philosophie in weltbürgerlicher Absicht: Akten des XI. Kant-Kongresses 2010*. Five-volume proceedings of the Eleventh International Kant Congress (Pisa, 2010), containing 25 plenary talks and 341 papers in 14 sections, from Kant's concept of philosophy to Kant's heritage. The note records the congress as held in 2010 and the print date as 2013.

## 2014

The 2014 entries include anthropology and empirical psychology (Frierson (2014)), the life sciences, Kant and colonialism and the race debate (Flikschuh and Ypi (eds.) (2014)), one-object against two-object readings and naive-realist perception (Stang (2014)), moral realism against constructivist readings (Wuerth (2014)), the Opus postumum, and a genealogy of neo-Kantianism.

- Stang (2014), *The Non-Identity of Appearances and Things in Themselves*. Targets the Metaphysical One Object reading of transcendental idealism, on which an appearance is numerically identical to the thing in itself that appears as it, and raises indiscernibility arguments: because an appearance has different spatiotemporal and modal properties than a thing in itself, no appearance can be identical to one. Notes the parallel with arguments against monism about material objects and the matter constituting them. (Continues the two-aspect line: Marshall (2013), Allison (2012).)
- Gomes (2014), *Kant on Perception: Naïve Realism, Non-Conceptualism, and the B-Deduction*. Argues that nonconceptualism cannot provide an account of the Transcendental Deduction and so should be rejected. Holds that this has no bearing on whether Kant endorsed a relational account of perceptual experience. (Continues the conceptualism debate: Tolley (2013), Griffith (2012), McLear (2011).)
- Dyck (2014), *Kant and Rational Psychology*. Examines Kant's critique of rational psychology in the Critique of Pure Reason within its eighteenth-century German context. Argues that Christian Wolff's rational psychology, which stressed empirical foundations, rather than Descartes or Leibniz, was Kant's primary target in the Paralogisms, and that Kant's criticism exposes how rational psychologists treat the soul as given in inner experience. (Returns to the Paralogisms: Proops (2010).) (Summary based on the publisher page description.)
- Frierson (2014), *Kant's Empirical Psychology*. Uses close analysis of texts, including unpublished lectures and notes, to show how Kant explains human action, choice and thought in empirical terms. Argues that understanding his empirical psychology sheds light on concepts such as the moral law, moral responsibility, weakness of will and cognitive error, and applies Kant's accounts of mental illness to contemporary philosophical issues. (Follows Frierson (2013).)
- Wuerth (2014), *Kant on Mind, Action, and Ethics*. Interprets Kant's ontology of the mind, transcendental idealism, account of the mind's powers and theory of action, and from these develops a moral realist account of Kant's ethics. NDPR adds an account of the soul as substance that rejects those of Kitcher, Ameriks and Allison, and a rejection of constructivism in favour of moral realism. It won the North American Kant Society Book Prize 2015, per the prize-winners page. (Continues the constructivism-realism debate: Stern (2011).)
- Ware (2014), *Kant on Moral Sensibility and Moral Motivation*. Takes up the problem that, in the Critique of Practical Reason, recognition of the moral law's authority must elicit both painful and pleasurable feelings, yet it is unclear how these effects could motivate action from duty. Reconstructs Kant's theory of moral sensibility in a way that overcomes this sceptical threat.
- Denis and Sensen (eds.) (2014), *Kant's Lectures on Ethics: A Critical Guide*. A Critical Guide with fifteen essays on four sets of student notes of Kant's lectures on ethics (Herder, Collins, Mrongovius and Vigilantius), companion to the Cambridge Edition volume. The essays treat the relation to the Baumgarten textbooks and topics such as obligation, virtue, love, the highest good, freedom, the categorical imperative, moral motivation and religion. (Dated by its CrossRef print or online date; see YEAR NOTE.)
- Maliks (2014), *Kant's Politics in Context*. Reads Kant's political philosophy in the light of public controversies between conservative and radical observers of the French Revolution. Argues that his theory of equal freedom explains both his condemnation of paternalistic government and of radical democracy and his defence of republican constitutionalism, and that Kant developed his philosophy in public discourse with figures such as Fichte, Erhard, Bergk, Rehberg and Gentz. (Dated by its CrossRef print or online date; see YEAR NOTE.)
- Flikschuh and Ypi (eds.) (2014), *Kant and Colonialism: Historical and Critical Perspectives*. Examines Kant's position on colonialism within his wider cosmopolitan commitments and traces his maturing opposition to European conquest and colonization. The volume offers no single overall position; contributions treat the role of race and trade in his teleological thinking, permissible and impermissible colonial settlement, land rights of non-state peoples, the relation of war to colonial conquest and prospects for Kantian restorative international law. (Continues the race and colonialism discussion: Elden and Mendieta (eds.) (2011), Mikkelsen (ed.) (2013), Kleingeld (2011), Gray (2012).)
- Goy and Watkins (eds.) (2014), *Kant's Theory of Biology*. Fifteen interpretive essays by specialists on Kant's theory of biology, covering seventeenth- and eighteenth-century biological theories, the development of the philosophy of biology in Kant's writings, the theory of organisms in the Critique of the Power of Judgment and current perspectives on the teleology of nature. (Continues the life-sciences strand: Mensch (2013).)
- Ginsborg (2014), *The Normativity of Nature: Essays on Kant's Critique of Judgement*. A collection of essays, nearly all previously published, on the Critique of Judgement, grouped under aesthetics, cognition and teleology. It connects questions about viewing nature through teleology with puzzles about aesthetic judgment and about concept acquisition. (Follows Ginsborg (2011) on normativity.) (Dated by its CrossRef print or online date; see YEAR NOTE.)
- Cohen (ed.) (2014), *Kant's Lectures on Anthropology: A Critical Guide*. A Critical Guide with essays by an international team on Kant's anthropology lectures, which underlie the 1798 *Anthropology from a Pragmatic Point of View*, and on their evolution and relation to the critical philosophy. Topics include cognition, the senses, self-knowledge, freedom, passion, desire, morality, culture, education and cosmopolitanism. (Companion to Kant (2012a), as the note states.)
- Mills (2014), *Kant and Race, Redux*. Replies to critiques of his earlier essay on Kant's Untermenschen by Kleingeld, Bernasconi and Fleischacker, and defends the claim that Kant's racial views affected his ethico-political theory centrally. On this claim, not all biological humans are full moral persons for Kant, sub-persons being a radically different and inferior kind. (Continues the race and colonialism discussion: Elden and Mendieta (eds.) (2011), Mikkelsen (ed.) (2013), Kleingeld (2011), Gray (2012).)
- Hall (2014), *The Post-Critical Kant: Understanding the Critical Philosophy through the Opus Postumum*. Explores the gap Kant announced in letters of 1798, a needed transition from the metaphysical foundations of natural science to physics, by turning to the Opus postumum. Argues that only by understanding the Opus postumum can Kant's mature view and the critical project be fully comprehended, and that the transition project can solve certain perennial problems of the critical philosophy.
- Beiser (2014), *The Genesis of Neo-Kantianism, 1796-1880*. A history of the neo-Kantian movement from the 1790s to 1880, following philosophers who faced challenges from scientific explanation (Darwinism, materialism, the physiology of perception) to religion, aesthetics and philosophy itself. NDPR stresses that for Beiser neo-Kantian philosophy is not only a doctrine but a strategy to establish the independence, relevance and power of philosophy. (Relates to Heis (2010) on Marburg neo-Kantianism.) (Summary based on the NDPR review.)

## 2015

The 2015 entries include book-length treatments of transcendental idealism and the Deduction (Allais (2015), Allison (2015)), a naive-realist article (Stephenson (2015)), a constructivist reading of reason (O'Neill (2015)), a Groundwork commentary, and reference and edition work (the Kant-Lexikon, a revised *Critique of Practical Reason*, *Reading Kant's Lectures*).

- Allais (2015), *Manifest Reality: Kant's Idealism and His Realism*. Interprets transcendental idealism as having epistemological and metaphysical components: a genuine but non-phenomenalist idealism about things as they appear, and an aspect of reality that grounds mind-dependent spatiotemporal objects and cannot be cognized, without asserting distinct non-spatiotemporal objects. NDPR reports a survey of deflationary, metaphysical and phenomenalist readings and a defence of a nonconceptualist account of intuition. A web search lists it as the North American Kant Society Book Prize 2016 winner. (Continues the two-aspect line: Marshall (2013), Stang (2014).)
- Allison (2015), *Kant's Transcendental Deduction: An Analytical-Historical Commentary*. A commentary arguing that the Transcendental Deduction addresses a problem about the role of a priori concepts stemming from the nature of the understanding in grounding objectivity, rather than an older sceptical challenge. It traces four developments in Kant's thought (sensibility and understanding, their cooperation, the unity of apperception, the mediating role of imagination) and defends a non-contingency thesis and the inseparability of the argument from transcendental idealism. (Summary based on the publisher page description.)
- Ferrarin (2015), *The Powers of Pure Reason: Kant and the Idea of Cosmic Philosophy*. Presents an account of Kant's critical works under the rubric of the idea of cosmic philosophy, aiming to uncover the basic intentions animating his efforts in the final critical decades through close reading, developmental history and analytic reconstruction. NDPR finds in it oscillating efforts to give due weight to competing tendencies within core insights rather than a steady progression toward clarity. (Summary based on the NDPR review.)
- Anderson (2015), *The Poverty of Conceptual Truth: Kant's Analytic/Synthetic Distinction and the Limits of Metaphysics*. Argues that Kant's analytic/synthetic distinction underwrites an argument against Leibnizian-Wolffian metaphysics: mathematics, the foundations of natural science, empirical knowledge and the principles of metaphysics are essentially synthetic and cannot be restated in the containment idiom of conceptual truth. Grounds the distinction in traditional logic and traces Kant's path to it; the epilogue draws consequences for conceptualism.
- Stephenson (2015), *Kant on the Object-Dependence of Intuition and Hallucination*. Argues, against a view described as currently popular in the literature, that Kant was not a naive realist about perceptual experience. Undermines the evidence for attributing the strong object-dependence of experience that naive realism entails to Kant, and argues that Kant's account of hallucination is unavailable to someone who holds that veridical perception is object-dependent in that sense. (Continues the naive-realist reading: Gomes (2014).)
- Onof and Schulting (2015), *Space as Form of Intuition and as Formal Intuition: On the Note to B160 in Kant's Critique of Pure Reason*. Examines Kant's distinction in the B-Deduction between space as form of intuition and as formal intuition, arguing against a recent reading that fuses the two so that space is first generated by the understanding's synthesis of the imagination. On the authors' account, a characteristic of space as a form of intuition is its nonconceptual unity, which is independent of determination by the understanding. (Continues the conceptualism debate: Tolley (2013), Gomes (2014).)
- McNulty (2015), *Rehabilitating the Regulative Use of Reason: Kant on Empirical and Chemical Laws*. Addresses interpretive disagreement over the nature and source of the necessity of empirical laws of nature in Kant's system and develops an ideational account, on which ideas of reason necessitate the laws of some non-physical sciences. Chemical laws, for instance, are grounded on ideas of the elements. The essay won the North American Kant Society Markus Herz Student Essay Prize 2014, per the prize-winners page.
- Schönecker and Wood (2015), *Immanuel Kant's "Groundwork for the Metaphysics of Morals": A Commentary*. A short commentary on the Groundwork aiming at an accurate reading. NDPR describes it as a slightly revised translation of the authors' German commentary of 2001, in which Wood's views on the Categorical Imperative shape the treatment of Groundwork II and Schönecker's account of the structure of the deduction shapes Groundwork III. (A Groundwork commentary like Allison (2011).)
- O'Neill (2015), *Constructing Authorities: Reason, Politics and Interpretation in Kant's Philosophy*. Collects essays developed over many years, arguing against the claim that Kant's critique of reason collapses into an argument from authority: Kant held that we must construct, not assume, the authority of reason, by ensuring that what we offer as reasons can be followed by others. She presents this constructivist view of reasoning as the clue to Kant's claims about knowledge, ethics and politics, including autonomy, the social contract, cosmopolitan justice and scriptural interpretation. (Continues the constructivism debate: Stern (2011), Wuerth (2014).)
- Friedlander (2015), *Expressions of Judgment: An Essay on Kant's Aesthetics*. A reappraisal of the Critique of Judgment that emphasizes the internal connection of judgment and meaning and shows how the pleasure in judging is related to our capacity to draw meaning from our encounter with beauty. The NDPR review describes the essay as speculative and criticizes its lack of reconstruction of Kant's lines of argument.
- Palmquist (2015), *Comprehensive Commentary on Kant's Religion within the Bounds of Bare Reason*. A sentence-level commentary on the Religion that includes a revised version of Pluhar's translation of the full text, identifies the changes (several hundred in all) that Kant made to the second (1794) edition, and argues that Kant's arguments in the Religion are cogent and have practical applications to religious practice. It also includes a glossary justifying translations of terms. (Relates to DiCenso (2011) and Insole (2013) on the Religion.) (Summary based on the publisher description.) (Dated by its CrossRef print or online date; see YEAR NOTE.)
- Gardner and Grist (eds.) (2015), *The Transcendental Turn*. An edited volume on the relations of European thinkers to the transcendental tradition that goes back to Kant's idea of a special transcendental knowledge. By its table of contents it has chapters on Kant's transcendental turn and idealism, Fichte, Hegel, Nietzsche, Husserl and phenomenology, arising from a research project organized by the late Mark Sacks. (Summary based on the NDPR review.)
- Kant (2015), *Critique of Practical Reason*. An edition/translation: a revised edition of the Cambridge Texts translation of the second Critique (1788), containing Mary Gregor's translation, with Andrews Reath's introduction placing the main themes of the Critique in the context of Kant's moral theory and critical system. The introduction has been revised and the guide to secondary reading updated. (The Cambridge Texts edition of the text treated in Reath and Timmermann (eds.) (2010).)
- Willaschek et al. (eds.) (2015), *Kant-Lexikon*. A lexical reference work giving modern readers access to Kant's largely novel terminology on the basis of present-day editions, and taking into account twentieth-century and contemporary research and advances in lexicology. The note describes it as a German-language lexicon of Kant's concepts across all subfields.
- Clewis (ed.) (2015), *Reading Kant's Lectures*. A collection of more than twenty original essays on Kant's teaching in relation to his published works, covering the student-note records of his lectures in metaphysics, logic, moral philosophy, anthropology, pedagogy, philosophical encyclopedia, natural rights, natural theology, mathematics, physics and physical geography. The abstract ties the volume to the Academy edition's continuing publication of the lectures.
