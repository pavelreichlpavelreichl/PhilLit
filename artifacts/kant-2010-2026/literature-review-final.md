## Introduction

This review lists selected scholarship on Kant published from 2010 through 30 September 2026: books, edited volumes, editions and translations, and journal articles, mainly English-language, with a few German-language reference works, proceedings and editions. Entries are organised by year, each with a short summary.

An entry had to be verified in tool output (Semantic Scholar, CrossRef, PhilPapers via Brave search, CORE, NDPR, SEP and IEP), be centred on Kant (or, for reception works, have him as a principal interlocutor), carry a publication year within the period (CrossRef print year preferred), not duplicate another entry, and meet at least one recorded significance indicator. The indicators are data, not verdicts: citation counts relative to year and subfield (retrieved 30 September to 1 October 2026), publisher and venue, NDPR and journal reviews, prizes confirmed by a source page, mention in SEP or IEP, status as a new edition or translation, and a documented role in a debate. The selection is not exhaustive: 251 entries (13 in 2010 and 2026, 14 in 2016, 16 in 2017, 15 in every other year); other works were screened out on indicators or the per-year cap.

Each year opens with a short overview, then lists entries by subfield; "continues the debate of" notes appear only where the annotations support them.

The list has limits. Twenty-six entries lack a retrievable abstract, so their summaries rest on NDPR reviews, publisher or journal pages, or snippets. For 2025 and 2026 most citation counts lie between 0 and 6, so significance rests on publisher, venue, reviews and author record; no NDPR review dated 2026 was found. OpenAlex was unavailable and not used for discovery. Education and disability have no entry; animals have three; the Opus postumum has eight, none in 2010-2013, 2015, 2018, 2020, 2021 or 2026; editions and translations number 13, none in 2010, 2014, 2020, 2021, 2023 or 2026. Dates follow the CrossRef print date, so some works, such as Denis and Sensen (eds.) (2014), appear a year earlier than conventionally cited.

## 2010

The 2010 entries include commentary on the Critique of Pure Reason and arguments for transcendental idealism (Guyer (ed.) (2010), Allais (2010)), work on the Metaphysics of Morals, virtue and Right (Baxley (2010), Byrd and Hruschka (2010)), and a Critical Guide on the second Critique.

- Guyer (ed.) (2010), *The Cambridge Companion to Kant's Critique of Pure Reason*. A Cambridge Companion devoted to a single text, with seventeen chapters by an international team, arranged under the background, the arguments and the impact of the work. The opening chapters place the Critique against continental rationalism and British empiricism.
- Proops (2010), *Kant's First Paralogism*. Examines how, in the First Paralogism, transcendental illusion leads rational psychologists to the doctrine that the soul is a substance. Argues that Kant identifies the source of the fallacy as a confusion about the nature of conceivability.
- Allais (2010), *Kant's Argument for Transcendental Idealism in the Transcendental Aesthetic*. Reads the argument of the Transcendental Aesthetic as turning on how synthetic a priori propositions can have objects, not on the source of their justification, against a common reading. Takes intuition to involve the presence to consciousness of the object represented, and concludes that a priori intuition cannot present a mind-independent feature of reality.
- Sturm and Wunderlich (2010), *Kant and the scientific study of consciousness*. Argues that Kant's views on consciousness, the mind-body problem and the status of psychology as a science differ from the way these topics are combined in present debates about a science of consciousness. Kant did not use the concept of consciousness for phenomenal qualia, his mind-body problem concerns substances, and he required quantification rather than mechanistic explanation of psychology.
- Baxley (2010), *Kant's Theory of Virtue: The Value of Autocracy*. Argues that Kantian virtue is to be understood as autocracy, a form of moral self-government in which reason rules sensibility. Holds that the moral psychology of the later ethical works, including the duty to cultivate certain emotional states, yields a modern, egalitarian conception of virtue that differs from Aristotelian virtue ethics.
- Reath and Timmermann (eds.) (2010), *Kant's Critique of Practical Reason: A Critical Guide*. A Critical Guide with essays on the principal arguments of the second Critique and their relation to the critical philosophy as a whole. The topics are the genesis of the work, the authority of the moral law as a fact of reason, the metaphysics of free agency, respect as the moral motive, and the primacy of practical reason and the postulates.
- Denis (ed.) (2010), *Kant's Metaphysics of Morals: A Critical Guide*. A Critical Guide with essays on the Metaphysics of Morals (Doctrine of Right and Doctrine of Virtue), treating its relation to Kant's earlier works and themes such as right and virtue, property, punishment and moral feeling. The NDPR review notes a chapter that questions Allen Wood's claim that Kant derives duties mainly from the Formula of Humanity rather than the Formula of Universal Law.
- Pallikkathayil (2010), *Deriving Morality from Politics: Rethinking the Formula of Humanity*. Argues against Korsgaard's and O'Neill's interpretation of what it is to treat someone merely as a means under the Formula of Humanity. Develops an alternative on which the content of many moral duties depends on the results of political philosophy and of actual political decision making.
- Byrd and Hruschka (2010), *Kant's Doctrine of Right: A Commentary*. A commentary that analyses Kant's system of individual rights, from the innate right to external freedom to property, contract and family claims. It explains his terminology, method and ideas in light of his intellectual environment, including Achenwall's natural-law text. The SEP entry on Kant's social and political philosophy discusses the authors' reading of Kant as favouring representative democracy.
- Flikschuh (2010), *Kant's Sovereignty Dilemma: A Contemporary Analysis*. Discusses the distinction between international and cosmopolitan Right as elements of a complete Kantian theory of global justice, and the dilemma that Kant's theory of Right both requires and prohibits the juridical compulsion of states. (Summary based on a Wiley Online Library page snippet.)
- Crowther (2010), *The Kantian Aesthetic: From Knowledge to the Avant-Garde*. Explains the kind of perceptual knowledge involved in aesthetic judgments by linking Kant's aesthetics to a critically upgraded account of his theory of knowledge. Shows that such judgments must involve categories and fundamental schemata.
- Papadaki (2010), *Kantian Marriage and Beyond: Why It Is Worth Thinking about Kant on Marriage*. Argues that Kant's account of monogamous marriage as the only relationship in which sexual use is compatible with humanity survives the charges of being flawed, paradoxical and at odds with feminism. Drawing on Kant's conception of friendship, suggests that Kant may have overlooked another context in which sex is morally permissible.
- Heis (2010), *"Critical philosophy begins at the very point where logistic leaves off": Cassirer's Response to Frege and Russell*. Traces the reception of Frege's and Russell's logic and logicism among the Marburg Neo-Kantians. Natorp and Cassirer reject their conception of logic on the basis of a Marburg philosophy of logic taken from Cohen, while Cassirer sees the logic as vindicating some Neo-Kantian theses about space, time and mathematical proof.

## 2011

The 2011 entries address nonconceptual content and animal consciousness (Hanna (2011), McLear (2011)), Groundwork commentary, dignity and a German-English edition (Allison (2011)), Kant, race, geography and cosmopolitanism (Kleingeld (2011)), and constructivism against realism.

- Kitcher (2011), *Kant's Thinker*. Examines the Critique of Pure Reason's account of cognition and its relation to self-consciousness. Argues that Kant's theory of the interplay between self-consciousness and consciousness of objects handles the self-ascription of beliefs and the tracking of objects over time better than more recent theories, and can suggest avenues for cognitive science. NDPR places the book in the Strawsonian tradition while noting disagreements with Strawson.
- Hanna (2011), *Beyond the Myth of the Myth: A Kantian Theory of Non-Conceptual Content*. Argues that a broadly Kantian strategy for explaining the existence, semantic structure and psychological function of essentially non-conceptual content also yields a bottom-up theory of the foundations of rationality in minded animals. On this theory, non-conceptual content is the substructure from which Sellars's logical space of reasons grows. The same journal issue carries a conceptualist reply by Bowman.
- McLear (2011), *Kant on Animal Consciousness*. Argues that Kant's claims about animal representation and consciousness do not rule out objective perceptual consciousness in animals. A nonconceptualist reading can endorse this while denying that animals can take cognitive attitudes towards, and self-ascribe, their own representational states. Addresses the problem that the conceptualist reading raises for non-rational animals.
- Schulting and Verburgt (eds.) (2011), *Kant's Idealism: New Interpretations of a Controversial Doctrine*. Collects essays on Kant's doctrine of idealism, including contributions by Ameriks and Baum, and treating the relation between Kant's logic and idealism, the thing in itself and the chapter on the Transcendental Ideal. It includes an essay by Allais on transcendental idealism and the transcendental deduction. (Summary based on a publisher description.)
- Allison (2011), *Kant's Groundwork for the Metaphysics of Morals: A Commentary*. A commentary on the Groundwork (1785) that pays attention to the structure of the work and to its historical and intellectual context, covering the whole text including the deduction in the third chapter. SEP's entry on Kant's moral philosophy cites the book on the claim that the formulas of the categorical imperative generate the same duties.
- Sensen (2011), *Kant on Human Dignity*. Studies all passages in which Kant uses the term dignity and the main arguments in the Kant literature for a value of human beings. Argues that respect for human beings does not follow from human dignity, since that would violate autonomy, but is an unconditional imperative of reason, yielding a unified account of Kant's moral philosophy.
- Stern (2011), *Understanding Moral Obligation: Kant, Hegel, Kierkegaard*. Challenges the argument from autonomy by which Kant is said to have turned to constructivism, arguing that Kant did not hold it and that the threat to autonomy comes from the obligatoriness of morality. Traces how Kant (obligation located in ourselves), Hegel (in others) and Kierkegaard (in God) respond. NDPR reports that Stern argues Kant was a value realist.
- Kleingeld (2011), *Kant and Cosmopolitanism: The Philosophical Ideal of World Citizenship*. Presents an account of Kant's cosmopolitanism (moral, political, legal, economic, cultural and psychological aspects), contrasting it with the views of his German contemporaries and arguing that it changed in the mid-1790s into a philosophically stronger theory. Discusses patriotism, states, an international federation, cultural pluralism, race and global economic justice. The North American Kant Society prize-winners page lists it as the 2014 Book Prize winner.
- Ginsborg (2011), *Primitive Normativity and Skepticism about Rules*. Invokes Kant's account of aesthetic judgment as the basis for a response to the skepticism about rules and meaning that Kripke attributes to Wittgenstein. Kant is the source of the proposed response rather than the sole subject of the paper. (Summary based on the SEP description; the retrieved abstract text covers only the article's opening on Kripke's argument.)
- DiCenso (2011), *Kant, Religion, and Politics*. Examines the place of religion in Kant's principal writings, arguing that his theory of religion emerges from his epistemology, ethics and political theory. Argues that it serves projects of restructuring institutions and moving political attitudes towards greater autonomy, against the view that Kant reduces religion to moral lessons.
- Louden (2011), *Kant's Human Being: Essays on His Theory of Human Nature*. Collects essays on Kant's empirical work on human nature and its connection to his ethical theory, analysing Kant's answer to the question "What is the human being?". Its parts treat virtue and human nature, Kant's popular lecture courses in anthropology, geography and education, and national character. The NDPR review comments on the closing treatment of Kant's remarks on national character.
- Elden and Mendieta (eds.) (2011), *Reading Kant's Geography*. Collects an introductory essay by Elden and seventeen essays in five parts on Kant's lectures on physical geography. The volume presents the lectures as a source for his thinking on anthropology, race theory, space and time, history, the environment and the public. (Summary based on a publisher description.)
- Massimi (2011), *Kant's dynamical theory of matter in 1755, and its debt to speculative Newtonian experimentalism*. Explores the scientific sources of Kant's early dynamical theory of matter in the Universal Natural History and On Fire (1755). Argues that speculative Newtonian experimentalism (from Newton's Opticks through Boerhaave's Elementa chemiae and Hales's Vegetable Staticks), rather than the Principia, was central, qualifying the picture of 1755 as Kant's conversion to Newton.
- Rockmore (2011), *Kant and Phenomenology*. Argues that phenomenology originates in Kant, whose second-edition constructivist theory of knowledge challenged representational epistemology. Holds that phenomenology is a form of constructivism traced through Plato, Kant and Hegel rather than through Husserl. The NDPR review objects that the constructivist reading does not engage rival interpretations such as Allison's dual-aspect theory.
- Kant (2011), *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals: A German-English Edition*. An edition/translation, edited by Gregor and Timmermann, that presents an edited German text alongside a revised version of Mary Gregor's translation of the Groundwork, with editorial notes and a bilingual index. Per NDPR, the 1786 second edition is the base text.

## 2012

The 2012 entries include conceptualist and two-aspect readings (Griffith (2012), Allison (2012)), the Cambridge Edition volumes *Natural Science* and *Lectures on Anthropology* (Kant (2012a), Kant (2012b)), reception in German idealism and early analytic philosophy, and political and moral essays.

- Ameriks (2012), *Kant's Elliptical Path*. Collects essays tracing the stages and key concepts of Kant's Critical philosophy from the early 1760s to the 1790s as a defence of the conception of humanity's fundamental end that Kant reached after reading Rousseau. Includes essays on each Critique, a defence of Ameriks's "moderate" interpretation of transcendental idealism, and chapters on will and the fact of reason, theodicy, religion and post-Kantian developments.
- Allison (2012), *Essays on Kant*. Collects seventeen previously published essays plus an addendum, covering epistemology, metaphysics, moral theory, teleology, political philosophy, philosophy of history and religion. The essays centre on transcendental idealism, freedom of the will and the purposiveness of nature, with a comparative dimension relating Kant to predecessors, successors and present-day philosophers. (Continues the two-aspect line of 2011: Schulting and Verburgt (eds.) (2011).)
- Chignell (2012), *Kant, Real Possibility, and the Threat of Spinoza*. Reconstructs Kant's pre-critical existence proof and the theory of modality behind his departure from Leibniz. Argues that the being it yields resembles Spinoza's extended natura naturans more than a personal creator-God, and that although transcendental idealism lets Kant avoid this conclusion in the critical period, one version of the Spinozistic threat remains.
- Griffith (2012), *Perception and the Categories: A Conceptualist Reading of Kant's Critique of Pure Reason*. Argues against the nonconceptualist readings of Kant given by Hanna and Allais and, from the A and B Transcendental Deductions, for a conceptualist reading. Because Kant's notion of empirical intuition makes essential reference to the categories, no empirical intuition can be given in sensibility independently of the understanding and its categories. (Continues the conceptualism/nonconceptualism debate of 2011: Hanna (2011), McLear (2011).)
- Friedman (2012), *Kant on geometry and spatial intuition*. Uses recent work on Kant and diagrammatic reasoning to reconsider aspects of Kant's philosophy of geometry and its relation to spatial intuition, in particular the relations between geometrical concepts and their schemata and between pure and empirical intuition. The SEP entry on Kant's philosophy of mathematics records that Friedman argues against diagrammatic interpretations of Kantian intuition. (Summary based on a PhilPapers snippet.)
- Hill (2012), *Virtue, Rules, and Justice: Kantian Aspirations*. Collects essays interpreting and extending Kant's moral theory, introducing themes of Kantian ethics and applying them to questions about revolution, prison reform and forcible humanitarian intervention. The NDPR review notes that all but one of the fifteen chapters were previously published.
- Sensen (ed.) (2012), *Kant on Moral Autonomy*. Collects essays on Kant's conception of autonomy, its history, its influence on contemporary conceptions and its moral significance, with an introduction by the editor and a postscript by Onora O'Neill. The NDPR review counts fourteen essays offered in honour of O'Neill.
- Ellis (ed.) (2012), *Kant's Political Theory: Interpretations and Applications*. Collects nine essays that aim to introduce Kant's political thought and to show the fruitfulness of a broadly Kantian approach. Five (O'Neill, Ripstein, Pogge, Hodgson, Taylor) read the Doctrine of Right and political essays, and four (Ferguson, Hunter, LaVaque-Manty, Laursen) draw on Kant's other writings or historical context. (Summary based on the NDPR review.)
- Teufel (2012), *What Does Kant Mean by 'Power of Judgement' in his Critique of the Power of Judgement?*. Argues that the power of judgement in the title of the third Critique refers only to the reflecting power of judgement, because Kant attempts a Critical justification of that power alone. Argues that Kant's discussion in sections 76-77 addresses the problem of synthesizing unsynthesized intuitions, so that the systematic ambitions of the third Critique are appropriate.
- Gray (2012), *Kant's Race Theory, Forster's Counter, and the Metaphysics of Color*. Argues that Kant's race theory is part of his idea of nature in the Critique of the Power of Judgment, examining the 1785-86 debate between Kant and Georg Forster over race. Shows that Kant developed his teleological conception of nature partly to give his race theory a unifying basis that could withstand Forster's empiricism. (Continues the Kant-and-race discussion of 2011: Kleingeld (2011), Louden (2011), Elden and Mendieta (eds.) (2011).)
- Parsons (2012), *From Kant to Husserl: Selected Essays*. The first of two volumes collecting Parsons's interpretive work: essays on Kant's philosophy of arithmetic, the relation of his arithmetical concepts to the categories, early reactions by Schultz and Bolzano, the first Critique and the Metaphysical Foundations of Natural Science, and a commentary on the Transcendental Aesthetic. These are followed by essays on Frege, Brentano and Husserl.
- Förster (2012), *The Twenty-Five Years of Philosophy: A Systematic Reconstruction*. Follows the Kantian idea that philosophy can be scientific and complete from the Critique of Pure Reason (1781) to Hegel (1806). Argues that Kant's three Critiques yield a distinction between intellectual intuition and intuitive understanding, which explains two post-Kantian lines of development, Fichte's Wissenschaftslehre and Goethe's transformation of intuitive understanding in light of Spinoza, that are joined in Hegel.
- Sedgwick (2012), *Hegel's Critique of Kant: From Dichotomy to Identity*. Presents Hegel's critique of Kant's theoretical philosophy, arguing that Hegel offers a critique of and alternative to the conception of cognition Kant defended in the critical period. Explores Hegel's claim to derive from Kantian doctrines the clues to a superior form of idealism.
- Kant (2012a), *Lectures on Anthropology*. An edition/translation, edited by Louden and Wood, of selections from student transcriptions of Kant's anthropology lectures of 1772-1789, which precede the published Anthropology from a Pragmatic Point of View (1798). It presents Anthropology Friedländer (1772) and Anthropology Mrongovius (1786) in full, with selections from the other transcriptions and parts of the Menschenkunde, and shows Kant's empirical theory of human nature.
- Kant (2012b), *Natural Science*. An edition/translation in the Cambridge Edition of the Works of Immanuel Kant, edited by Eric Watkins, of Kant's early natural-science writings. It includes Thoughts on the True Estimation of Living Forces, the Universal Natural History and Theory of the Heavens (1755) and the whole of Physical Geography, with editorial introductions, explanatory notes and a glossary. NDPR notes that some texts appear in English for the first time.

## 2013

The 2013 entries include journal articles on one-world (two-aspect) against two-world readings and on nonconceptualism (Marshall (2013), Tolley (2013)), work on the empirical and scientific side of Kant (Friedman (2013), Frierson (2013), Mensch (2013)), source texts on race, a commentary on the Doctrine of Virtue, and Kant-Kongress proceedings.

- Marshall (2013), *Kant's Appearances and Things in Themselves as Qua-Objects*. Proposes understanding the appearance/thing-in-itself distinction through an Aristotelian qua-object (following Kit Fine), which gives a sense in which appearances and things in themselves can be the same things while differing in many features. The proposal is offered to the one-world interpretation, which faces problems arising from the different features Kant assigns to the two. (Continues the two-aspect line: Schulting and Verburgt (eds.) (2011), Allison (2012).)
- Tolley (2013), *The Non-Conceptuality of the Content of Intuitions: A New Approach*. Argues that Kant takes intuitions to have content in the sense of Frege's Sinn (a mode of presentation of an object or property) and that this content is distinct in kind from the content of concepts. Holds that the conceptualism debate had stalled for lack of agreement on what content means, and shows how this nonconceptualist account answers objections to earlier nonconceptualist readings. (Continues the conceptualism debate: Hanna (2011), McLear (2011), Griffith (2012).)
- Friedman (2013), *Kant's Construction of Nature: A Reading of the Metaphysical Foundations of Natural Science*. Reads the Metaphysical Foundations of Natural Science (1786), reconstructing its main argument and explaining its relationship to Newton's Principia and to eighteenth-century scientific thinkers such as Euler and Lambert. Situating the text against Kant's pre-critical writings and the changes to the second edition of the Critique of Pure Reason, it argues for a perspective on the meaning and development of the critical philosophy as a whole.
- Grenberg (2013), *Kant's Defense of Common Moral Experience: A Phenomenological Account*. Argues that the substance of Kant's moral philosophy emerges from reflection on the common experience of the conflict between happiness and morality. Reading the Groundwork and the Critique of Practical Reason, it finds a phenomenological notion of experience in the Fact of Reason and gives feeling an epistemic role in grounding a priori morality, against readings that give feeling only a motivational role and against Fichtean readings. NDPR also reports an interpretation of the failure of the Groundwork III argument for moral obligation.
- Wuerth (2013), *Sense and Sensibility in Kant's Practical Agent: Against the Intellectualism of Korsgaard and Sidgwick*. Presents Kant's account of practical agency as embodied agency, drawing on his empirical-psychological and metaphysical descriptions, and argues against the intellectualized interpretations of Korsgaard and Sidgwick. On this reading the agent chooses from a reflective distance but not from the complete affective distance of reason alone, and its sensibility (sense, feeling, desire) belongs to the agent that chooses.
- Trampota et al. (eds.) (2013), *Kant's "Tugendlehre": A Comprehensive Commentary*. A running commentary on the Doctrine of Virtue of the Metaphysics of Morals by 18 scholars from Germany, Italy, Britain and the United States. It discusses topics such as suicide, truthfulness, moral perfection, beneficence, gratitude, sympathy, respect and friendship, as well as Kant's moral psychology. (Relates to Baxley (2010) and Denis (ed.) (2010) on the Doctrine of Virtue.)
- Hay (2013), *Kantianism, Liberalism, and Feminism: Resisting Oppression*. Argues that the moral and political frameworks of Kantianism and liberalism are indispensable for addressing the concerns of contemporary feminism. NDPR reports that the first chapters set out a Kantian feminist theory of oppression as group-specific, systemic harm and that the later chapters apply it to sexual oppression. The book is a constructive Kantian account rather than an exegesis, and NDPR raises questions of Kant interpretation and suggests giving the Doctrine of Right a larger role in it. (Continues the gender discussion: Papadaki (2010).)
- Mensch (2013), *Kant's Organicism: Epigenesis and the Development of Critical Philosophy*. Argues that epigenesis, a theory of biological formation, was not merely a metaphor for Kant but guided his critical philosophy, especially the relationship between reason and the categories of the understanding. Combines a study of Kant's theory of cognition with intellectual history and biography, placing the epigenesis of reason within eighteenth-century theories of generation, genealogy and classification. (Summary based on the publisher description.)
- Matherne (2013), *The Inclusive Interpretation of Kant's Aesthetic Ideas*. Offers an inclusive interpretation of the aesthetic ideas of the Critique of the Power of Judgment, on which they can present empirical concepts and emotions related to ordinary experience as well as moral and purely rational concepts, as restrictive readings hold. Argues that this neglected class matters for Kant's views of artistic production, of the experience of art and of the value of art for ordinary experience.
- Insole (2013), *Kant and the Creation of Freedom: A Theological Problem*. Argues that Kant struggled with how to conceive God's creative action in relation to human freedom and concluded that freedom can be protected only if God withdraws in certain ways from the created world. His rejection of the medieval and early modern account of divine concursus is said to shape transcendental idealism and his theory of freedom.
- Frierson (2013), *What Is the Human Being?*. An introduction to Kant's philosophy of human nature that explains three ways Kant addresses the question (the transcendental, the empirical and the pragmatic). It then considers later theorists of human nature, among them Hegel, Marx, Darwin, Nietzsche, Freud, Sartre, Heidegger, Foucault and E. O. Wilson, who challenge or enrich his views. (Relates to Louden (2011) and Kant (2012a) on anthropology and human nature.)
- Mikkelsen (ed.) (2013), *Kant and the Concept of Race: Late Eighteenth-Century Writings*. An edition/translation of Kant's essays on race, including the first race essay in its two versions of 1775 and 1777, presented in chronological order. It adds previously untranslated texts by four German contemporaries of Kant. (Continues the Kant-and-race discussion: Kleingeld (2011), Elden and Mendieta (eds.) (2011), Gray (2012).)
- Watkins (2013), *The Early Kant's (Anti-) Newtonianism*. Argues that the pre-critical Kant, besides using Newtonian principles in the Universal Natural History, developed a criticism of Newton based on metaphysical issues about God, with implications for how the formation and constitution of the heavenly bodies should be scientifically explained. The abstract adds that Kant remained interested in these issues in the Critical period.
- Golob (2013), *Heidegger on Kant, Time and the "Form" of Intentionality*. Analyses Heidegger's 1927/8 lecture course on the Critique of Pure Reason and his 1929 *Kant and the Problem of Metaphysics*. Argues that Heidegger's reading of Kant turns on two questions: how Heidegger's Kant understands the transcendental, answered through Cassam's model of a self-directed transcendental argument, and what role the horizon plays, answered through Kant's view that pure general logic abstracts from semantic content and Hume's attack on metaphysics.
- Bacin et al. (eds.) (2013), *Kant und die Philosophie in weltbürgerlicher Absicht: Akten des XI. Kant-Kongresses 2010*. Five-volume proceedings of the Eleventh International Kant Congress (Pisa, 2010), containing 25 plenary talks and 341 papers in 14 sections, from Kant's concept of philosophy to Kant's heritage. The note records the congress as held in 2010 and the print date as 2013.

## 2014

The 2014 entries include anthropology and empirical psychology (Frierson (2014)), the life sciences, Kant and colonialism and the race debate (Flikschuh and Ypi (eds.) (2014)), one-object against two-object readings and naive-realist perception (Stang (2014)), moral realism against constructivist readings (Wuerth (2014)), the Opus postumum, and a genealogy of neo-Kantianism.

- Stang (2014), *The Non-Identity of Appearances and Things in Themselves*. Targets the Metaphysical One Object reading of transcendental idealism, on which an appearance is numerically identical to the thing in itself that appears as it, and raises indiscernibility arguments: because an appearance has different spatiotemporal and modal properties than a thing in itself, no appearance can be identical to one. Notes the parallel with arguments against monism about material objects and the matter constituting them. (Continues the two-aspect line: Marshall (2013), Allison (2012).)
- Gomes (2014), *Kant on Perception: Naïve Realism, Non-Conceptualism, and the B-Deduction*. Argues that nonconceptualism cannot provide an account of the Transcendental Deduction and so should be rejected. Holds that this has no bearing on whether Kant endorsed a relational account of perceptual experience. (Continues the conceptualism debate: Tolley (2013), Griffith (2012), McLear (2011).)
- Dyck (2014), *Kant and Rational Psychology*. Examines Kant's critique of rational psychology in the Critique of Pure Reason within its eighteenth-century German context. Argues that Christian Wolff's rational psychology, which stressed empirical foundations, rather than Descartes or Leibniz, was Kant's primary target in the Paralogisms, and that Kant's criticism exposes how rational psychologists treat the soul as given in inner experience. (Returns to the Paralogisms: Proops (2010).) (Summary based on the publisher page description.)
- Frierson (2014), *Kant's Empirical Psychology*. Uses close analysis of texts, including unpublished lectures and notes, to show how Kant explains human action, choice and thought in empirical terms. Argues that understanding his empirical psychology sheds light on concepts such as the moral law, moral responsibility, weakness of will and cognitive error, and applies Kant's accounts of mental illness to contemporary philosophical issues. (Follows Frierson (2013).)
- Wuerth (2014), *Kant on Mind, Action, and Ethics*. Interprets Kant's ontology of the mind, transcendental idealism, account of the mind's powers and theory of action, and from these develops a moral realist account of Kant's ethics. NDPR adds an account of the soul as substance that rejects those of Kitcher, Ameriks and Allison, and a rejection of constructivism in favour of moral realism. It won the North American Kant Society Book Prize 2015, per the prize-winners page. (Continues the constructivism-realism debate: Stern (2011).)
- Ware (2014), *Kant on Moral Sensibility and Moral Motivation*. Takes up the problem that, in the Critique of Practical Reason, recognition of the moral law's authority must elicit both painful and pleasurable feelings, yet it is unclear how these effects could motivate action from duty. Reconstructs Kant's theory of moral sensibility in a way that overcomes this sceptical threat.
- Denis and Sensen (eds.) (2014), *Kant's Lectures on Ethics: A Critical Guide*. A Critical Guide with fifteen essays on four sets of student notes of Kant's lectures on ethics (Herder, Collins, Mrongovius and Vigilantius), companion to the Cambridge Edition volume. The essays treat the relation to the Baumgarten textbooks and topics such as obligation, virtue, love, the highest good, freedom, the categorical imperative, moral motivation and religion. (Dated by its CrossRef print or online date; see YEAR NOTE.)
- Maliks (2014), *Kant's Politics in Context*. Reads Kant's political philosophy in the light of public controversies between conservative and radical observers of the French Revolution. Argues that his theory of equal freedom explains both his condemnation of paternalistic government and of radical democracy and his defence of republican constitutionalism, and that Kant developed his philosophy in public discourse with figures such as Fichte, Erhard, Bergk, Rehberg and Gentz. (Dated by its CrossRef print or online date; see YEAR NOTE.)
- Flikschuh and Ypi (eds.) (2014), *Kant and Colonialism: Historical and Critical Perspectives*. Examines Kant's position on colonialism within his wider cosmopolitan commitments and traces his maturing opposition to European conquest and colonization. The volume offers no single overall position; contributions treat the role of race and trade in his teleological thinking, permissible and impermissible colonial settlement, land rights of non-state peoples, the relation of war to colonial conquest and prospects for Kantian restorative international law. (Continues the race and colonialism discussion: Elden and Mendieta (eds.) (2011), Mikkelsen (ed.) (2013), Kleingeld (2011), Gray (2012).)
- Goy and Watkins (eds.) (2014), *Kant's Theory of Biology*. Fifteen interpretive essays by specialists on Kant's theory of biology, covering seventeenth- and eighteenth-century biological theories, the development of the philosophy of biology in Kant's writings, the theory of organisms in the Critique of the Power of Judgment and current perspectives on the teleology of nature. (Continues the life-sciences strand: Mensch (2013).)
- Ginsborg (2014), *The Normativity of Nature: Essays on Kant's Critique of Judgement*. A collection of essays, nearly all previously published, on the Critique of Judgement, grouped under aesthetics, cognition and teleology. It connects questions about viewing nature through teleology with puzzles about aesthetic judgment and about concept acquisition. (Follows Ginsborg (2011) on normativity.) (Dated by its CrossRef print or online date; see YEAR NOTE.)
- Cohen (ed.) (2014), *Kant's Lectures on Anthropology: A Critical Guide*. A Critical Guide with essays by an international team on Kant's anthropology lectures, which underlie the 1798 *Anthropology from a Pragmatic Point of View*, and on their evolution and relation to the critical philosophy. Topics include cognition, the senses, self-knowledge, freedom, passion, desire, morality, culture, education and cosmopolitanism. (Companion to Kant (2012a), as the note states.)
- Mills (2014), *Kant and Race, Redux*. Replies to critiques of his earlier essay on Kant's Untermenschen by Kleingeld, Bernasconi and Fleischacker, and defends the claim that Kant's racial views affected his ethico-political theory centrally. On this claim, not all biological humans are full moral persons for Kant, sub-persons being a radically different and inferior kind. (Continues the race and colonialism discussion: Elden and Mendieta (eds.) (2011), Mikkelsen (ed.) (2013), Kleingeld (2011), Gray (2012).)
- Hall (2014), *The Post-Critical Kant: Understanding the Critical Philosophy through the Opus Postumum*. Explores the gap Kant announced in letters of 1798, a needed transition from the metaphysical foundations of natural science to physics, by turning to the Opus postumum. Argues that only by understanding the Opus postumum can Kant's mature view and the critical project be fully comprehended, and that the transition project can solve certain perennial problems of the critical philosophy.
- Beiser (2014), *The Genesis of Neo-Kantianism, 1796-1880*. A history of the neo-Kantian movement from the 1790s to 1880, following philosophers who faced challenges from scientific explanation (Darwinism, materialism, the physiology of perception) to religion, aesthetics and philosophy itself. NDPR stresses that for Beiser neo-Kantian philosophy is not only a doctrine but a strategy to establish the independence, relevance and power of philosophy. (Relates to Heis (2010) on Marburg neo-Kantianism.) (Summary based on the NDPR review.)

## 2015

The 2015 entries include book-length treatments of transcendental idealism and the Deduction (Allais (2015), Allison (2015)), a naive-realist article (Stephenson (2015)), a constructivist reading of reason (O'Neill (2015)), a Groundwork commentary, and reference and edition work (the Kant-Lexikon, a revised *Critique of Practical Reason*, *Reading Kant's Lectures*).

- Allais (2015), *Manifest Reality: Kant's Idealism and His Realism*. Interprets transcendental idealism as having epistemological and metaphysical components: a genuine but non-phenomenalist idealism about things as they appear, and an aspect of reality that grounds mind-dependent spatiotemporal objects and cannot be cognized, without asserting distinct non-spatiotemporal objects. NDPR reports a survey of deflationary, metaphysical and phenomenalist readings and a defence of a nonconceptualist account of intuition. A web search lists it as the North American Kant Society Book Prize 2016 winner. (Continues the two-aspect line: Marshall (2013), Stang (2014).)
- Allison (2015), *Kant's Transcendental Deduction: An Analytical-Historical Commentary*. A commentary arguing that the Transcendental Deduction addresses a problem about the role of a priori concepts stemming from the nature of the understanding in grounding objectivity, rather than an older sceptical challenge. It traces four developments in Kant's thought (sensibility and understanding, their cooperation, the unity of apperception, the mediating role of imagination) and defends a non-contingency thesis and the inseparability of the argument from transcendental idealism. (Summary based on the publisher page description.)
- Ferrarin (2015), *The Powers of Pure Reason: Kant and the Idea of Cosmic Philosophy*. Presents an account of Kant's critical works under the rubric of the idea of cosmic philosophy, aiming to uncover the basic intentions animating his efforts in the final critical decades through close reading, developmental history and analytic reconstruction. NDPR finds in it oscillating efforts to give due weight to competing tendencies within core insights rather than a steady progression toward clarity. (Summary based on the NDPR review.)
- Anderson (2015), *The Poverty of Conceptual Truth: Kant's Analytic/Synthetic Distinction and the Limits of Metaphysics*. Argues that Kant's analytic/synthetic distinction underwrites an argument against Leibnizian-Wolffian metaphysics: mathematics, the foundations of natural science, empirical knowledge and the principles of metaphysics are essentially synthetic and cannot be restated in the containment idiom of conceptual truth. Grounds the distinction in traditional logic and traces Kant's path to it; the epilogue draws consequences for conceptualism.
- Stephenson (2015), *Kant on the Object-Dependence of Intuition and Hallucination*. Argues, against a view described as currently popular in the literature, that Kant was not a naive realist about perceptual experience. Undermines the evidence for attributing the strong object-dependence of experience that naive realism entails to Kant, and argues that Kant's account of hallucination is unavailable to someone who holds that veridical perception is object-dependent in that sense. (Continues the naive-realist reading: Gomes (2014).)
- Onof and Schulting (2015), *Space as Form of Intuition and as Formal Intuition: On the Note to B160 in Kant's Critique of Pure Reason*. Examines Kant's distinction in the B-Deduction between space as form of intuition and as formal intuition, arguing against a recent reading that fuses the two so that space is first generated by the understanding's synthesis of the imagination. On the authors' account, a characteristic of space as a form of intuition is its nonconceptual unity, which is independent of determination by the understanding. (Continues the conceptualism debate: Tolley (2013), Gomes (2014).)
- McNulty (2015), *Rehabilitating the Regulative Use of Reason: Kant on Empirical and Chemical Laws*. Addresses interpretive disagreement over the nature and source of the necessity of empirical laws of nature in Kant's system and develops an ideational account, on which ideas of reason necessitate the laws of some non-physical sciences. Chemical laws, for instance, are grounded on ideas of the elements. The essay won the North American Kant Society Markus Herz Student Essay Prize 2014, per the prize-winners page.
- Schönecker and Wood (2015), *Immanuel Kant's "Groundwork for the Metaphysics of Morals": A Commentary*. A short commentary on the Groundwork aiming at an accurate reading. NDPR describes it as a slightly revised translation of the authors' German commentary of 2001, in which Wood's views on the Categorical Imperative shape the treatment of Groundwork II and Schönecker's account of the structure of the deduction shapes Groundwork III. (A Groundwork commentary like Allison (2011).)
- O'Neill (2015), *Constructing Authorities: Reason, Politics and Interpretation in Kant's Philosophy*. Collects essays developed over many years, arguing against the claim that Kant's critique of reason collapses into an argument from authority: Kant held that we must construct, not assume, the authority of reason, by ensuring that what we offer as reasons can be followed by others. She presents this constructivist view of reasoning as the clue to Kant's claims about knowledge, ethics and politics, including autonomy, the social contract, cosmopolitan justice and scriptural interpretation. (Continues the constructivism debate: Stern (2011), Wuerth (2014).)
- Friedlander (2015), *Expressions of Judgment: An Essay on Kant's Aesthetics*. A reappraisal of the Critique of Judgment that emphasizes the internal connection of judgment and meaning and shows how the pleasure in judging is related to our capacity to draw meaning from our encounter with beauty. The NDPR review describes the essay as speculative and criticizes its lack of reconstruction of Kant's lines of argument.
- Palmquist (2015), *Comprehensive Commentary on Kant's Religion within the Bounds of Bare Reason*. A sentence-level commentary on the Religion that includes a revised version of Pluhar's translation of the full text, identifies the changes (several hundred in all) that Kant made to the second (1794) edition, and argues that Kant's arguments in the Religion are cogent and have practical applications to religious practice. It also includes a glossary justifying translations of terms. (Relates to DiCenso (2011) and Insole (2013) on the Religion.) (Summary based on the publisher description.) (Dated by its CrossRef print or online date; see YEAR NOTE.)
- Gardner and Grist (eds.) (2015), *The Transcendental Turn*. An edited volume on the relations of European thinkers to the transcendental tradition that goes back to Kant's idea of a special transcendental knowledge. By its table of contents it has chapters on Kant's transcendental turn and idealism, Fichte, Hegel, Nietzsche, Husserl and phenomenology, arising from a research project organized by the late Mark Sacks. (Summary based on the NDPR review.)
- Kant (2015), *Critique of Practical Reason*. An edition/translation: a revised edition of the Cambridge Texts translation of the second Critique (1788), containing Mary Gregor's translation, with Andrews Reath's introduction placing the main themes of the Critique in the context of Kant's moral theory and critical system. The introduction has been revised and the guide to secondary reading updated. (The Cambridge Texts edition of the text treated in Reath and Timmermann (eds.) (2010).)
- Willaschek et al. (eds.) (2015), *Kant-Lexikon*. A lexical reference work giving modern readers access to Kant's largely novel terminology on the basis of present-day editions, and taking into account twentieth-century and contemporary research and advances in lexicology. The note describes it as a German-language lexicon of Kant's concepts across all subfields.
- Clewis (ed.) (2015), *Reading Kant's Lectures*. A collection of more than twenty original essays on Kant's teaching in relation to his published works, covering the student-note records of his lectures in metaphysics, logic, moral philosophy, anthropology, pedagogy, philosophical encyclopedia, natural rights, natural theology, mathematics, physics and physical geography. The abstract ties the volume to the Academy edition's continuing publication of the lectures.

## 2016

The 2016 entries include modal metaphysics (Stang (2016)), the conceptualism and perception debate (Schulting (ed.) (2016)), Kant's racism (Allais (2016)), the highest good (Höwing (ed.) (2016)), the Cambridge Edition volume *Lectures and Drafts on Political Philosophy*, and work on the Opus postumum and pre-critical period.

- Stang (2016), *Kant's Modal Metaphysics*. Studies Kant's theory of possibility from the pre-Critical work of the 1750s and 1760s to the Critical system of the 1780s and early 1790s. Argues that the continuity and change between the two periods turn on Kant's transforming the ontological question about what all possible beings have in common into a transcendental question about how we represent possible objects a priori. (Relates to Chignell (2012) on modal metaphysics.)
- McLear (2016), *Kant on Perceptual Content*. Argues that Kant is widely read as endorsing the Content View, on which perceptual states have intentional content and represent ways the world might be, and that this reading faces significant problems. Proposes instead that Kant holds a form of acquaintance theory of perception. (Follows McLear (2011) and joins the naive-realist discussion: Gomes (2014), Stephenson (2015).)
- Schulting (ed.) (2016), *Kantian Nonconceptualism*. Collects essays by Kant scholars on whether, and in what sense, Kant allows nonconceptual content in intuition and perception. Includes Allais's survey of the recent debate, Heidemann on aesthetic nonconceptualism, and Onof and Tolley on space and cognition. (Continues the conceptualism debate: Hanna (2011), Tolley (2013), Onof and Schulting (2015).)
- Conant (2016), *Why Kant Is Not a Kantian*. Argues that Kant rejects the shared empiricist and rationalist assumption that sensibility and understanding are each self-standingly intelligible capacities, which Conant terms the Layer-Cake Conception of Human Mindedness. On his reading of the B Deduction, the two capacities must be made sense of in the light of each other. The note describes it as a statement of the conceptualist reading. (Cf. Griffith (2012).)
- Guyer (2016), *Virtues of Freedom: Selected Essays on Kant*. Collects essays on Kant's attempt to ground morality in the unconditional value of the human freedom to set one's own ends, which becomes autonomy when regulated by the equal value of everyone's freedom. The essays also treat Kant's proofs of freedom, the cultivation of moral feelings such as respect, love and self-esteem, and models of human community. They were first published between the 1980s and the 2010s.
- Höwing (ed.) (2016), *The Highest Good in Kant's Philosophy*. Collects essays re-evaluating Kant's doctrine of the highest good as a combination of virtue and happiness. The essays address its role in the moral argument for faith, the antinomy of pure practical reason, the primacy of practical reason, and Kant's views of the final end of nature and historical progress.
- Westphal (2016), *How Hume and Kant Reconstruct Natural Law: Justifying Strict Objectivity without Debating Moral Realism*. Argues that Hume and Kant share a constructivist method for identifying basic moral principles and justifying their strict objectivity without invoking moral realism or anti-realism. Basic principles of justice are artificial but not arbitrary, because they answer problems of social coordination with reasons that can be addressed to all parties. (Continues the constructivism debate: Stern (2011), O'Neill (2015).)
- Nassar (2016), *Analogical reflection as a source for the science of life: Kant and the possibility of the biological sciences*. Brings together historians of science and historians of philosophy on Kant and the life sciences, arguing that Kant's notion of reflective judgment contributed to the ontological concern with life as a distinctive object of study. Although Kant says biology cannot be a science, his more fundamental claim is that the study of living beings requires an essentially analogical mode of thought. (Continues the life-sciences strand: Mensch (2013), Goy and Watkins (eds.) (2014).)
- Allais (2016), *Kant's Racism*. Responds to Charles Mills's proposal that Kant can be made consistent on race by a threshold notion of moral personhood on which some races do not qualify under the categorical imperative. Argues that Kant cannot be made consistent and that his racism should instead be used to learn about the nature of racism, since his moral philosophy and moral psychology supply materials for understanding it. (Continues the race debate and answers Mills: see Mills (2014).)
- Dyck (2016), *Spontaneity before the Critical Turn: The Spontaneity of the Mind in Crusius, the Pre-Critical Kant, and Tetens*. Argues, against the view that the spontaneity of the understanding is an innovation of the Critique of Pure Reason, that eighteenth-century German thinkers had already developed a robust conception of mental spontaneity. Examines the accounts of Crusius, the pre-Critical Kant and Tetens as an interconnected and overlooked context for Kant's later doctrine. (Relates to Dyck (2014).)
- McNulty (2016), *Chemistry in Kant's Opus Postumum*. Asks whether Kant, who in the Opus postumum adopts a broadly Lavoisierian chemistry, still regards chemistry as an improper though rational science. Answers yes, arguing against Tuschling that mathematical construction keeps a role in the foundations of proper natural science and that the aether and the enumeration of the elements do not make chemistry a proper science. (Relates to Hall (2014) on the Opus postumum and McNulty (2015) on chemistry.)
- Matherne (2016), *Kantian Themes in Merleau-Ponty's Theory of Perception*. Challenges the picture of Kant as an intellectualist and Merleau-Ponty as his non-conceptualist challenger. Argues, through Kant's schematism, the neo-Kantian Lachièze-Rey and Merleau-Ponty's body schema, that Merleau-Ponty's theory of perception develops Kant's.
- Kant (2016), *Lectures and Drafts on Political Philosophy*. An edition/translation in the Cambridge Edition of the Works of Immanuel Kant of reflections Kant wrote while developing his political philosophy, including preparatory drafts for Theory and Practice, Toward Perpetual Peace, the Doctrine of Right and the Conflict of the Faculties. It also contains the only surviving student transcription of his course on Natural Right. The texts trace his political thought from his exposure to Rousseau in the 1760s to the 1790s.
- Eberhard (2016), *Preparation for Natural Theology: With Kant's Notes and the Danzig Rational Theology Transcript*. An edition/translation of Eberhard's 1781 textbook, which Kant used as the basis for his lectures on the philosophical doctrine of religion, together with Kant's notes and the Danzig rational theology transcript. The complete text makes it possible to separate Eberhard's words from Kant's own ideas in the lectures.

## 2017

The 2017 entries address cognition, perception and the self (Watkins and Willaschek (2017), Longuenesse (2017)), normativity (Pollok (2017)), laws of nature and mathematics (Massimi and Breitenbach (eds.) (2017)), global justice and colonialism (Flikschuh (2017)), Kant and women, and the late transition project.

- Longuenesse (2017), *I, Me, Mine: Back to Kant, and Back Again*. Analyses Kant's distinction between consciousness of oneself as subject and as object and its relevance to contemporary discussions of self-consciousness and the first person, in dialogue with recent philosophy of language and mind and with Sartre. Argues that Kant identified a specific kind of self-consciousness, awareness of one's activity of bringing rational unity to one's mental states, and analyses his critique of rational psychology in the Paralogisms. (Relates to Kitcher (2011) on apperception and to the Paralogisms: Proops (2010), Dyck (2014).)
- Pollok (2017), *Kant's Theory of Normativity: Exploring the Space of Reason*. Offers a book-length analysis of Kant's theory of normativity across theoretical and practical philosophy and aesthetics. Reads the critical turn as a normative turn: Kant departs from the perfectionism of early modern rationalism and arrives at synthetic a priori principles that fix the validity of our judgments. Relates reason's self-legislation to the natural-right tradition. (Relates to Ginsborg (2014) on normativity.)
- Watkins and Willaschek (2017), *Kant's Account of Cognition*. Sets out the structure of Kant's account of theoretical cognition (Erkenntnis) in the Critique of Pure Reason. Argues that cognition is a mental state that determines a given object by attributing general features to it, that givenness involves an immediate relation to an existing object, and that cognition is therefore distinct from knowledge (Wissen). The note describes it as a framework reading that feeds the naive-realist and nonconceptualist debates about intuition. (Continues the conceptualism and naive-realist discussion: Gomes (2014), Stephenson (2015), McLear (2016).)
- Gomes and Stephenson (eds.) (2017), *Kant and the Philosophy of Mind: Perception, Reason, and the Self*. Fourteen essays on the philosophy of mind in Kant's writings: mental processing in intuition, the object-dependence of intuitions, imagination and inner sense, and the nature of the self and self-consciousness. The volume also draws out the relevance of these topics to contemporary philosophy of mind. (Continues the conceptualism and naive-realist discussion: Gomes (2014), Stephenson (2015), McLear (2016).)
- O'Shea (ed.) (2017), *Kant's Critique of Pure Reason: A Critical Guide*. A Cambridge Critical Guide in which an international team of Kant scholars clarifies the terms and arguments of the Critique of Pure Reason and engages with its debates, including the interpretation of transcendental idealism, conceptualism and nonconceptual content in perception, and the soundness of transcendental arguments. (A volume-length survey of the Critique like Guyer (ed.) (2010).)
- Massimi and Breitenbach (eds.) (2017), *Kant and the Laws of Nature*. Collects essays exploring Kant's views on the laws of nature in the physical and life sciences. Topics include the separation of the physical and life sciences in Kant's treatment, the relation between universal and empirical laws, and the role of reason and understanding in imposing lawful unity on nature.
- Sutherland (2017), *Kant's Conception of Number*. Investigates Kant's conception of natural number, setting aside the role of intuition in arithmetic. Argues that the conception contains both cardinal and ordinal elements: the cardinal elements give the basis of a conception of number in general, and the ordinal elements specify the size of particular collections. (Continues the Kant-and-mathematics strand: Friedman (2012).)
- Kleingeld (2017), *Contradiction and Kant's Formula of Universal Law*. Argues that the standard reading of the Formula of Universal Law as requiring universalizability, marked by the distinction between contradictions in conception and in the will, overlooks the simultaneity condition in the formula. Proposes that the principle requires being able to will two things simultaneously without self-contradiction: that a maxim be one's own and that it be a universal law.
- Flikschuh (2017), *What Is Orientation in Global Thinking? A Kantian Inquiry*. Takes Kant's question "What is orientation in thinking?" to argue that global normative theory fails to acknowledge its own conceptual inadequacies and tends to apply domestic political experience to the global context. Proposes a form of global reasoning sensitive to the variability of contexts and to local reasons for action. (Continues the colonialism discussion: Flikschuh and Ypi (eds.) (2014).)
- Valdez (2017), *It's Not about Race: Good Wars, Bad Wars, and the Origins of Kant's Anti-Colonialism*. Interprets Kant's cosmopolitanism and anti-colonialism in Toward Perpetual Peace. On the historical evidence, Kant's concern is the damage European expansion and colonial rivalry do to the prospects for peace in Europe, which leads him to adjust his concept of antagonism. Concludes that his anti-colonialism co-exists with hierarchical views of race. (Continues the colonialism discussion: Flikschuh and Ypi (eds.) (2014).)
- Chaouli (2017), *Thinking with Kant's Critique of Judgment*. Invites readers to think through the Critique of Judgment about the force of aesthetic experience, the essence of art, and the relation between beauty and meaning. Each chapter unfolds the significance of a key concept of Kant's thought.
- Huneman (2017), *Kant's Concept of Organism Revisited: A Framework for a Possible Synthesis between Developmentalism and Adaptationism?*. Argues that Kant's concept of organism, usually seen as a precursor of developmentalism, is incomplete on that view: his regulative notion of purposiveness relies on a design criterion and an epigenesis criterion for natural purposes. Because the design criterion is met within the Modern Synthesis and the epigenesis criterion within Evo-Devo, Kant's notion could frame a synthesis of adaptationism and developmentalism. (Relates to Nassar (2016) and Goy and Watkins (eds.) (2014).)
- Varden (2017), *Kant and Women*. Argues that Kant's conception of women is complex and, on a sympathetic reading, not anti-feminist. Kant expects the traditional male-female distinction to persist but does not propose the traditional gender ideal as the moral ideal, so his moral works hold that all citizens, including women, have the right and should be encouraged to strive toward an active condition. (Continues the gender discussion: Papadaki (2010), Hay (2013).)
- Thorndike (2017), *Kant's Transition Project and Late Philosophy: Connecting the Opus postumum and Metaphysics of Morals*. Argues that Kant saw a need for a transition from the a priori to the empirical in both natural and practical philosophy: the transition from the metaphysical foundations of natural science to physics in the Opus postumum is paralleled by a transition from the metaphysics of morals to ethics applied to contingent circumstances. On Thorndike's account, moral feelings make the moral transition possible by serving as schemata of the moral law. (Relates to Hall (2014) and McNulty (2016) on the late work.) (Summary based on the NDPR review.) (Dated by its CrossRef print or online date; see YEAR NOTE.)
- Engelland (2017), *Heidegger's Shadow: Kant, Husserl, and the Transcendental Turn*. Contends that Heidegger recognizes transcendental philosophy as the necessary entry point to his thought yet strives to overcome it, and that he cannot abandon his own early work in transcendental philosophy. Traces Heidegger's readings of Kant and Husserl through Kant and the Problem of Metaphysics, Being and Time and Contributions. (Relates to Golob (2013) on Heidegger's reading of Kant.)
- Kant (2017), *Kant: The Metaphysics of Morals*. An edition/translation in the Cambridge Texts in the History of Philosophy: a revised edition of Mary Gregor's translation of the complete Metaphysics of Morals (Doctrine of Right and Doctrine of Virtue). It adds footnotes on controversial points in the translation, an introduction by Lara Denis that sets the work in context and presents interpretive debates, and guidance on further reading.

## 2018

The 2018 entries include work on the Transcendental Dialectic (Willaschek (2018)), Kant and animals (Korsgaard (2018)), the race debate (Mills (2018), Sandford (2018)), several Cambridge Elements, Baumgarten and Kant, an edition of the *Religion* and the XII Kant-Kongress proceedings.

- Willaschek (2018), *Kant on the Sources of Metaphysics: The Dialectic of Pure Reason*. Reconstructs and defends Kant's account of why rational beings must ask metaphysical questions about unconditioned objects such as souls, uncaused causes or God, and why answers appear rationally compelling. Interprets the Transcendental Dialectic so as to bring out its positive side and Kant's account of metaphysical thinking.
- Horstmann (2018), *Kant's Power of Imagination*. A Cambridge Element (Elements in the Philosophy of Immanuel Kant) that offers a reconstructive study of how the power of imagination contributes to cognition according to Kant, drawing only on Kant's own resources. One section treats the imagination in the A and B editions of the Critique of Pure Reason and another its role in the first part of the Critique of Judgment.
- Lu-Adler (2018), *Kant and the Science of Logic: A Historical and Philosophical Reconstruction*. Presents Kant's theory of logic as a turning point in the history of debates over whether logic is a science, an instrument, a standard of assessment or a mixture, and over how it relates to metaphysics and physics. Reconstructs Kant's distinctions between general and particular logic, pure and applied logic, and pure general and transcendental logic in their historical context.
- Merritt (2018a), *Kant on Reflection and Virtue*. Gives an account of reflection and the cognitive virtues in Kant, taking as its point of departure his claim that all judgments require reflection. Argues for a specification thesis, that moral virtue is a specification of general cognitive virtue, with reflection standing at the intersection of theoretical and practical reason. (Summary based on the NDPR review.)
- Sorensen and Williamson (eds.) (2018), *Kant and the Faculty of Feeling*. Collects essays on feeling, Kant's third mental faculty, which has received less attention than cognition and desire. The volume asks why feeling must be a separate faculty, how it relates to cognition and desire, and what respect, sublimity, enthusiasm and hope tell us about its nature.
- Papish (2018), *Kant on Evil, Self-Deception, and Moral Reform*. Explores the cognitive dimensions of evil and moral reform in Kant's mature ethics: what self-deception is, how it is connected to evil, and how we achieve self-knowledge in its place. Treats hedonism, character, moral weakness and strength, the alleged universality of evil, and the role of the ethical community, using Kant's logic lectures alongside the core ethical texts.
- Holtman (2018), *Kant on Civil Society and Welfare*. A Cambridge Element in the series Elements in the Philosophy of Immanuel Kant. Examines what justifies state-sponsored supports for individual welfare within a Kantian political system and their purpose, extent and form. Assesses minimalist and middle-ground interpretations of Kant's political theory and develops an alternative based on civic respect for each person as a participant in joint governance.
- Merritt (2018b), *The Sublime*. A Cambridge Element that considers Kant's account of the sublime in the context of Anglophone and German rationalist predecessors. Argues that strictly only the human vocation to perfect our rational capacity according to the standard of virtue is sublime, distinguishes respect and admiration as the two varieties of sublime feeling, and discusses the Stoic channel through Seneca.
- Korsgaard (2018), *Fellow Creatures: Our Obligations to the Other Animals*. Argues that we are obligated to treat all sentient animals as ends in themselves. Combines an Aristotelian theory of the good with a revised version of Kant's argument for the value of humanity, holding that rationality commits us to claiming standing as ends in ourselves both as autonomous lawmakers and as beings with a good. The note describes it as an extension and revision of Kant's formula of humanity that departs from Kant's own view of animals. (Joins the animals discussion: McLear (2011).)
- Mills (2018), *Black Radical Kantianism*. Tries to develop a black radical Kantianism, that is, a Kantianism informed by the black experience in modernity, after briefly considering socialist and feminist appropriations of Kant. The note places it as a reconstruction of Kantian moral and political theory informed by the critique of Kant's racism. (Continues the race debate; Allais (2016) answers Mills's earlier proposal; cf. Mills (2014).) (Summary based on a PhilPapers snippet.)
- Sandford (2018), *Kant, race, and natural history*. Argues that Kant's treatment of the systematic unity of nature and knowledge in the first and third Critiques can be traced back to a methodological problem in the natural history of his period, the possibility of a natural system of nature. His theory of race, worked out in response to the problem of unity in diversity within the human species, is presented as the earliest developed model of teleological judgment in his work. (Takes the systematic-link side of the race debate; cf. Mills (2014), Allais (2016).)
- Fugate and Hymers (eds.) (2018), *Baumgarten and Kant on Metaphysics*. Eleven essays on Alexander Baumgarten's Metaphysics and its relation to Kant's philosophy. Early chapters set Baumgarten's work in its historical context, including Pietism and rationalism, and later chapters explore how Kant criticized and built on the Metaphysics.
- Nisenbaum (2018), *For the Love of Metaphysics: Nihilism and the Conflict of Reason from Kant to Rosenzweig*. Proposes that the conflict of reason in the Critique of Pure Reason, and Kant's solution through the primacy of the practical, is the problem shaping German Idealism. Jacobi and Maimon set the stage for the reception of critical philosophy, and the rise and fall of German Idealism is told as a story of interpretations and radicalizations of Kant's prioritizing of the practical.
- Kant (2018), *Kant: Religion within the Boundaries of Mere Reason: And Other Writings*. An edition/translation in the Cambridge Texts in the History of Philosophy, a revised edition with slightly revised translations, a revised introduction and guidance on further reading. The work spells out a religion grounded in moral reason and treats original sin, justification and the proper role of a church.
- Waibel et al. (eds.) (2018), *Natur und Freiheit: Akten des XII. Internationalen Kant-Kongresses*. Congress proceedings that collect the plenary, main and session lectures of the Twelfth International Kant Congress, "Nature and Freedom", held at the University of Vienna from 21 to 25 September 2015, on two fundamental concepts of Kant's critical works. The contributions are multilingual and span the subfields. (Belongs to the Kant-Kongress series: Bacin et al. (eds.) (2013).)

## 2019

The 2019 entries address autonomy, constructivism and constitutivism (Kleingeld and Willaschek (2019), Schafer (2019)), laws of nature (Watkins (2019)), modality and the metaphysics lectures (Abacı (2019)), nonideal theory and cosmopolitanism, how to deal with Kant's sexism and racism, and a Yale edition of the Groundwork.

- Watkins (2019), *Kant on Laws*. Studies Kant's concept of law in general, arguing that he has one generic concept of law (necessity prescribed to a domain by the act of a spontaneous faculty) that is instantiated differently in laws of nature and in the moral law. Argues that the necessity of transcendental laws is grounded in the legislative authority of the understanding and reason rather than in the natures of objects. (Relates to Massimi and Breitenbach (eds.) (2017) on laws of nature.) (Summary based on the NDPR review.)
- Abacı (2019), *Kant's Revolutionary Theory of Modality*. Argues that Kant's precritical views on possibility, existence and necessity remain within the rationalist paradigm, whereas in the critical period modal notions become subjective, relational features of our discursivity rather than fundamental ontological predicates. Claims that this theory is constitutive of the critical turn, which Kant first formulates as a shift from an ontological to an epistemological question about possibility. (Follows Stang (2016) on Kant's modal metaphysics, according to the NDPR review.)
- Kraus (2019), *The Parity and Disparity between Inner and Outer Experience in Kant*. Interprets Kant's inner experience as empirical cognition of mental states but not of a persistent mental substance, so that the schema of persistence is replaced by the regulative idea of the soul. Sets this reading against the parity view (inner experience as cognition of a mental object on a par with outer experience) and the disparity view (inner experience is not empirical cognition).
- Kleingeld and Willaschek (2019), *Autonomy Without Paradox: Kant, Self-Legislation and the Moral Law*. Challenges the assumption that Kantian autonomy is the self-legislation of the principle of morality. Argues on textual and philosophical grounds that autonomy concerns only substantive moral laws (such as the law against lying) and that, so understood, it lacks the paradoxical features usually attributed to it. (Continues the constructivism/realism debate: Stern (2011), Wuerth (2014), O'Neill (2015), Westphal (2016).)
- Schafer (2019), *Kant: constitutivism as capacities-first philosophy*. Asks whether and in what sense Kant is a constitutivist about meta-normative questions, and answers by placing the question within Kant's general methodology for philosophy. Argues that Kant's constitutivism is one instance of a broader capacities-first approach that treats our basic, self-conscious rational capacities as fundamental. (Continues the constructivism/realism debate: Stern (2011), Wuerth (2014), O'Neill (2015), Westphal (2016).)
- Formosa and Sticker (2019), *Kant and the demandingness of the virtue of beneficence*. Interprets Kant's beneficence as a midpoint between overdemandingness and undemandingness through four claims. The ground of the duty to benefit others also permits beneficence to ourselves, we may prioritise our own ends, it is more virtuous to do more rather than less in helping others, and indifference to others is vicious.
- Ameriks (2019), *Kantian Subjects: Critical Philosophy and Late Modernity*. Collects essays in two parts: the first reads the Critical philosophy's conception of subjectivity (freedom, autonomy, law, necessity, final ends, human vocation, intentionality, idealism), including readings of the Groundwork in relation to Sartre, Murdoch, O'Neill, Prauss and Brandom, and the second treats post-Kantians, especially Hegel, Schelling and Hölderlin. The NDPR review describes it as continuing Ameriks's line of interpreting Kant against sweeping readings of him as destroying metaphysics. (Continues Ameriks (2012).)
- Huseyinzadegan (2019), *Kant's Nonideal Theory of Politics*. Extends the scholarship that takes Kant's empirical writings seriously to his political theory. Argues that Kant's politics has two guiding threads, an ideal theory of right that deduces political goals a priori and a nonideal theory concerned with contingent features of history, culture and geography, which proceeds by the hypothetical, regulative and teleological use of reason. (Summary based on review text retrieved by the abstract resolver.)
- Valdez (2019), *Transnational Cosmopolitanism: Kant, Du Bois, and Justice as a Political Craft*. Reconstructs W. E. B. Du Bois's post-World War I writings and political action as the basis of a normative account of transnational cosmopolitanism. Identifies limits in Kant's cosmopolitanism through a contextual account of Perpetual Peace, argues that these limits persist in neo-Kantian scholarship, and proposes a cosmopolitanism that is more radical, democratic and transnational. (Continues Valdez (2017) and Flikschuh and Ypi (eds.) (2014).)
- Fisher (2019), *Organisms and the form of freedom in Kant's third Critique*. Interprets the argument in the Appendix to the Critique of Teleological Judgment that human freedom is the final end of creation: judgments of freedom within nature are possible as instances of teleological judgment, so that nature as a whole is regarded as given laws from a supersensible ground, namely freedom. On this reading mechanism, teleology and freedom are unified in the supersensible, which also explains how the third Critique bridges the gulf between nature and freedom.
- Insole (2019), *Free Belief: The Medieval Heritage in Kant's Moral Faith*. Argues that in describing faith as a free choice Kant aligns with the medieval tradition and with his mediating sources, Leibniz and Locke. Kant departs from that tradition only by holding that divine action would destroy genuine human freedom, which explains his insistence that grace follows rather than precedes moral conversion. (Relates to Insole (2013).)
- Kleingeld (2019), *On Dealing with Kant's Sexism and Racism*. Asks how current readers should deal with Kant's sexist and (until late in his life) racist views alongside his universalist moral theory. Rejects both setting these views aside and translating his egalitarian principles into inegalitarian ones, argues for highlighting the tensions that pervade Kant's theory, and states preconditions for using his principles to criticise sexism and racism. (Joins the race and gender debates: Mills (2018), Sandford (2018), Varden (2017).)
- Howard (2019), *The transition within the transition: the Übergang from the Selbstsetzungslehre to the ether proofs in Kant's Opus postumum*. Argues against readings such as Förster's that treat the ether proofs and the Selbstsetzungslehre as the core of the Opus postumum, holding that the drafts of fascicles X and XI, written between them, are of primary importance. Through a close reading of a page of fascicle XI, shows Kant trying to resolve problems internal to both by presenting a transition from one to the other. (Relates to Hall (2014) and Thorndike (2017) on the transition project.)
- Kant (2019), *Groundwork for the Metaphysics of Morals*. An edition/translation of the Groundwork, edited by Wood, offering an annotated translation together with four essays: Schneewind on the historical relation of Kantian ethics to earlier and later ethical thought, Baron on moral motivation, and Kagan and Wood advancing contrasting interpretations of Kantian ethics and its practical implications. (Relates to Kant (2011), Allison (2011) and Schönecker and Wood (2015) on Groundwork editions and commentaries.) (Summary based on the publisher abstract.)
- Fugate (ed.) (2019), *Kant's Lectures on Metaphysics*. A Critical Guide whose ten chapters analyse the transcripts of Kant's lectures on metaphysics, following their structure (prolegomena, ontology, cosmology, empirical psychology, rational psychology, natural theology). The contributors examine what the transcripts show about Kant's conception of transcendental philosophy and critical theism and discuss how to use these primary sources responsibly.

## 2020

The 2020 entries cover Kant and animals (Callanan and Allais (eds.) (2020)), sex, love and gender (Varden (2020)), inner sense and self-knowledge (Kraus (2020)), the philosophy of mathematics (Posy and Rechter (eds.) (2020)), freedom, religion, race and nonideal theory, and reception through Cassirer, Husserl and McDowell.

- Kraus (2020), *Kant on Self-Knowledge and Self-Formation: The Nature of Inner Experience*. Develops Kant's account of psychological personhood by analysing empirical self-knowledge through the capacity for inner experience. Defends the thesis that inner experience is empirical cognition of oneself not as a mere object but as a psychological person; per the NDPR review, self-cognition depends on the cooperation of sensibility, understanding and reason, with the regulative idea of the soul filling the gap between self-reference in thought and the deliverances of inner sense. (Follows Kraus (2019).)
- Cohen (2020), *A Kantian Account of Emotions as Feelings*. Extracts from Kant's writings an account of the emotions, although Kant neither uses the term nor treats them systematically. Reads Kant as defining emotions first and foremost as feelings, which are non-cognitive states with a derived intentionality, and argues that the faculty of feeling makes us aware of how our faculties relate to each other and to the world, so that feelings are affective appraisals of our agency with an orientational function.
- de Boer (2020), *Kant's Reform of Metaphysics: The Critique of Pure Reason Reconsidered*. Interprets the Critique of Pure Reason in view of Kant's efforts to turn Christian Wolff's metaphysics into a science, rather than primarily through epistemological questions. Situates the Critique in eighteenth-century German philosophy, analyses the Transcendental Deduction, the Schematism, the Appendix to the Transcendental Analytic and the Architectonic, and reconstructs the outline of the system of pure reason that the Critique was to prepare.
- Møller (2020), *Kant's Tribunal of Reason: Legal Metaphor and Normativity in the Critique of Pure Reason*. Gives a book-length study of the legal metaphors in the first Critique, arguing that they are central to Kant's account of reason: reason has a structure mirroring a legal system in a natural-right framework, and its systematicity is legal systematicity. Kant aims to make cognisers resemble authorised judges by proving the legitimacy of laws and the conditions of valid judgment. (Relates to Pollok (2017) on normativity.)
- Posy and Rechter (eds.) (2020), *Kant's Philosophy of Mathematics: Volume 1, The Critical Philosophy and Its Roots*. Collects essays surveying contemporary scholarship on Kant's mathematical thinking, covering geometry, arithmetic, logic and metaphysics, with attention to the development of his philosophy of mathematics, its place in the critical philosophy, its historical background and the Kantian themes that influenced later mathematics. A web search result states that the volume has twelve essays. (Relates to Friedman (2012) and Sutherland (2017).) (Summary based on a publisher description.)
- Allison (2020), *Kant's Conception of Freedom: A Developmental and Critical Analysis*. Traces Kant's views on free will from his discussions in the 1750s to his accounts in the 1790s. Argues that Kant's path to viewing freedom as a transcendental power both radically distinct from and compatible with natural causality was a winding one, and that the concept stayed central because it unites his theoretical and practical philosophy. The NDPR review notes that the ten chapters divide evenly between pre-critical and post-1781 writings.
- Kleingeld (2020), *How to Use Someone "Merely as a Means"*. Re-examines Kant's texts to develop an account of the conditions under which someone is used merely as a means, a prohibition in Kant's moral theory widely thought impossible to specify precisely. Argues that the account has textual support and philosophical advantages over alternative conceptions. (Continues the humanity-formula discussion: Pallikkathayil (2010).)
- Loriaux (2020), *Kant and Global Distributive Justice*. Argues that although Kant's political thought does not address global poverty and inequality directly, it supplies conceptual and normative resources for global socioeconomic duties. Develops the Kantian duty to enter a rightful condition beyond the state, derives a duty of right to assist states unable to fulfil core state functions, and draws implications for just transnational trade and immigration regulation. The publisher abstract calls the work an Element.
- Halper (2020), *Rethinking Kant's distinction between the beauty of art and the beauty of nature*. Argues that Kant presents two different accounts of beauty, one applying properly to art and one to nature. The judgment of beauty proper to nature can be free and made without concepts, whereas a work of art is judged beautiful when it expresses aesthetic ideas, a distinction used to explain problematic passages in Kant's text, including cases where the conception of artistic beauty is applied to natural objects.
- Wood (2020), *Kant and Religion*. Offers an interpretation of Kant's Religion within the Boundaries of Mere Reason that explores his treatment of the idea of God, his views on evil, and the moral grounds for faith in God. Places religion's meaning within the history of human culture and ties Kant's stance on religion to his theoretical, moral, political and religious positions. (Relates to DiCenso (2011) and Palmquist (2015) on Religion.)
- Varden (2020), *Sex, Love, and Gender: A Kantian Theory*. Presents an account of sex, love and gender built on Kant, interpreting his scattered remarks in ethics, legal-political thought, aesthetics, teleology, history, religion and anthropology as one coherent approach. Then rehabilitates the theory by correcting Kant's own errors and limitations, and applies it to sexual orientation, abortion, gender identity, marriage, erotica, sexual oppression and trade in sexual services. (Continues the gender discussion: Papadaki (2010), Hay (2013), Varden (2017).)
- Callanan and Allais (eds.) (2020), *Kant and Animals*. An edited collection on the role of animals in Kant's philosophy across theoretical and practical philosophy. It covers Kant's model of animal minds, whether his philosophy of mind allows animals intentional representations of spatiotemporal objects, animal nature in humans and non-humans, how his scientific theory accommodated animals, and whether Kantian moral theory gives the right account of the moral status of animals, to which Kant denied direct obligations. (Continues the animals discussion: McLear (2011), Korsgaard (2018).)
- Basevich (2020), *Reckoning With Kant on Race*. Develops Kant's theory of reform into a theory of racial justice reform, reading his philosophy of race as part of his nonideal theory of justice, a racist pragmatic anthropology that assumes some racial groups failed to develop their innate capacity for legislative reason. Argues for an alternative Kantian nonideal theory that extends Kant's model of public reason, including an ideal of interracial civic fellowship. (Continues the race debate: Mills (2018), Kleingeld (2019); and the nonideal-theory strand: Huseyinzadegan (2019).)
- Biagioli (2020), *Ernst Cassirer's transcendental account of mathematical reasoning*. Examines how Cassirer tried to reconcile the Kantian explanation of the possibility of knowledge with nineteenth and early twentieth-century conceptual changes in science, in his philosophy of mathematics. Argues that he addressed the applicability of abstract mathematical concepts to empirical reality by a transcendental account on which the formation of mathematical concepts explains the extensibility of mathematical knowledge, placing him within the Marburg neo-Kantian debate of 1902-1910. (Relates to Heis (2010) on Cassirer.)
- van Mazijk (2020), *Perception and Reality in Kant, Husserl, and McDowell*. Engages critically with John McDowell's conceptualist account of how perception gives access to external reality, comparing his views on perception and reality with those of Kant and Husserl. Across six chapters it examines the three thinkers' theories of perception within their broader views of mind and reality, and yields a critique of McDowell's conceptualism that combines analytic philosophy, Kantian philosophy and phenomenology. (Continues the conceptualism debate: Matherne (2016).)

## 2021

The 2021 entries address the two-world/two-aspect debate and the Dialectic (Jauernig (2021), Proops (2021)), the philosophy of mathematics (Sutherland (2021)), the law of war, women, enlightenment and race, and two reference works, the *Cambridge Kant Lexicon* (Wuerth (ed.) (2021)) and the 13th Kant Congress proceedings.

- Jauernig (2021), *The World According to Kant: Appearances and Things in Themselves in Critical Idealism*. Interprets Kant's critical idealism as an ontological position comprising transcendental idealism, empirical realism and further basic ontological theses. On the proposed reading the world has a transcendental level of mind-independent things in themselves and an empirical level of mind-dependent appearances, which are intentional objects of experience that nonetheless genuinely exist because they are grounded in things in themselves; Kant is thus both a genuine idealist and a genuine realist about empirical objects, minds, space and time. (Continues the two-world versus two-aspect debate: Marshall (2013), Stang (2014), Allais (2015).)
- Proops (2021), *The Fiery Test of Critique: A Reading of Kant's Dialectic*. Studies the Transcendental Dialectic, arguing that Kant conceives critique as a winnowing exercise modelled on the metallurgical assay called cupellation. Critique therefore has a positive side: besides eliminating dogmatic metaphysics it uncovers the indirect proof of transcendental idealism through the resolution of the Antinomies and the defence of theoretically grounded doctrinal beliefs in a wise originator and an afterlife. (Relates to Proops (2010) and Willaschek (2018) on the Dialectic.)
- Ypi (2021), *The Architectonic of Reason: Purposiveness and Systematic Unity in Kant's Critique of Pure Reason*. Takes the Architectonic of Pure Reason as the entry point to the unity of reason and to Kant's question of what the human being is. Argues that the first Critique's concept of purposiveness is purposiveness as intelligent design, which differs from the purposiveness as normativity of the later works, anchored in reflective judgment and grounded on transcendental freedom, and draws consequences for the passage from nature to freedom, faith and knowledge, progress in history and religion.
- Sutherland (2021), *Kant's Mathematical World: Mathematics, Cognition, and Experience*. Reconstructs Kant's project of explaining both mathematical cognition and cognition of the world through our basic cognitive capacities, situating him in a mathematical tradition rooted in Euclid's Elements, from before the nineteenth-century arithmetization of mathematics. Argues that he treated mathematics as the science of magnitudes and their measurement, with all objects of experience being extensive magnitudes whose real properties have intensive magnitudes. (Relates to Sutherland (2017) and Posy and Rechter (eds.) (2020).)
- Hogan (2021), *Handedness, Idealism, and Freedom*. Seeks an adequate understanding of Kant's idealist argument from incongruent counterparts (pairs of objects such as left and right hands that cannot be enclosed in the same spatial limits despite exact similarity), which Kant takes to demand the demotion of space and time to forms of sensory intuition. Its central finding is that the argument rests essentially on Kant's theory of freedom, which links the pillars of transcendental idealism and points to a unified reading of his system of idealist arguments.
- Schafer (2021), *Transcendental Philosophy As Capacities-First Philosophy*. Proposes that the critical Kant can be understood as working within a capacities-first framework, in which our basic rational or cognitive capacities play both an explanatorily and an epistemically fundamental role. Examines the relation between these two roles, sketches how the reading illuminates the foundations of his theoretical and practical philosophy, and considers Kant's reasons for finding the approach attractive and its contemporary relevance. (Follows Schafer (2019).)
- Schulting (2021), *Apperception and Self-Consciousness in Kant and German Idealism*. Examines reflexivity, self-consciousness, representation and apperception in Kant and German Idealism, arguing that all human experience is irreversibly self-referential, which is the self-reflexivity called transcendental apperception. Follows the theme from Kant's historical roots to Reinhold, Fichte and Hegel, examining the cognitive role of self-consciousness and its relation to idealism.
- Ware (2021), *Kant's Justification of Ethics*. Defends the view that Kant's mature writings on ethics share a unified commitment to the primacy of the moral law, against commentators who detect a rift between the Groundwork and the Critique of Practical Reason. Through close analysis and historical contextualisation it rejects a paradigmatic way of reading Kant's arguments for morality and freedom and places them within his critical methodology. (Relates to Ware (2014).)
- Ripstein (2021), *Kant and the Law of War*. Brings Kant's explicit discussions of war into the contemporary debate on the law and morality of war. Kant holds that a special morality governs the permissible use of force because of war's distinctive immorality: war is barbaric because might makes right, and the very thing that makes war wrongful also supplies the standard for evaluating its conduct and the only basis for law governing war. (Relates to Byrd and Hruschka (2010) on the Doctrine of Right.)
- Makkai (2021), *Kant's Critique of Taste: The Feeling of Life*. Argues for a rediscovery of Kant's aesthetics, whose centrepiece, the investigation of the judgment of taste, presents our relationships with works of art we love as scenes of aesthetic encounter in which one feels oneself animated by an object. Tracing Kant's insight that to judge is to reveal one's sense of what bears judging, and so of what matters, it places the aesthetics within Kant's study, begun in the first Critique, of judgment's role in the life of the mind.
- Merritt (2021), *Nature, corruption, and freedom: Stoic ethics in Kant's Religion*. Argues that Kant's account of radical evil in the 1793 Religion, usually read as a reworking of the Augustinian doctrine of original sin, is better read as an answer to an old Stoic problem. Kant takes as premise a version of the Stoic idea that nature gives us uncorrupted starting points and must then explain the origin of human badness, which requires a conception of freedom not available in his Stoic sources. (Relates to Papish (2018) on radical evil.)
- Sabourin (2021), *Kant's Enlightenment and Women's Peculiar Immaturity*. Takes up Kant's claim in What is Enlightenment? that no women are currently enlightened and argues that the exclusion is due to legal restrictions guiding his conception of enlightenment, which is meant to take place in society. His twofold conception of citizenship and the subordination built into restricting the private use of reason clarify those restrictions, so that Kant seems unlikely to have intended women to take an active part in enlightenment. (Continues the gender discussion: Varden (2017).)
- Yab (2021), *Kant and the Politics of Racism: Towards Kant's Racialised Form of Cosmopolitan Right*. Proposes an account of the place of the theory of race in Kant's thought as a central part of the philosophical anthropology in his political system. A web-search summary of the Springer page adds that it addresses the impact of Kant's racial theory on his philosophy and political thought, the influence of his racial hierarchy on cosmopolitan right, and whether Kant is racist. (Continues the race and cosmopolitan-right discussion: Kleingeld (2011), Valdez (2019).) (Summary based on a publisher description retrieved from a retailer page and a web-search summary of the publisher page.)
- Wuerth (ed.) (2021), *The Cambridge Kant Lexicon*. A lexicon with entries by 130 Kant scholars in two parts. Part I covers Kant's philosophical concepts; Part II gives entries on each of his published works and each of his sets of lectures and personal reflections, arranged chronologically so as to show the development of his thought. (Relates to Willaschek et al. (eds.) (2015) on reference works.)
- Himmelmann and Serck-Hanssen (eds.) (2021), *The Court of Reason: Proceedings of the 13th International Kant Congress*. Presents the contributions to the 13th International Kant Congress, held at the University of Oslo on 6-9 August 2019 and dedicated to the court of reason, the idea that reason stands before itself as a tribunal. Special emphasis was put on Kant's methodology, his account of conceptual critique and the relevance of his ideas to political philosophy and the philosophy of law, with further sections on a range of topics. (Belongs to the Kant-Kongress series: Waibel et al. (eds.) (2018).)

## 2022

The 2022 entries include metaphysical readings of Kant's idealism (Schafer and Stang (eds.) (2022)), work on the Opus postumum (Basile and Lyssy (2022)), Kant and race and labour (Lu-Adler (2022), Pascoe (2022)), Kant and artificial intelligence, a Critical Guide to the *Metaphysical Foundations*, and a critical edition of the *Träume*.

- Schafer and Stang (eds.) (2022), *The Sensible and Intelligible Worlds: New Essays on Kant's Metaphysics and Epistemology*. Collects essays on the metaphysical presuppositions of Kant's philosophy and on how they square with his critique of dogmatic metaphysics, set against an earlier tendency in analytic Kant scholarship to read him as anti-metaphysical. The chapters draw on Leibnizian and Aristotelian backgrounds and on links to German Idealism, and cover transcendental idealism, modal metaphysics, the continuum, the epistemology of the a priori and the foundations of Kant's metaethical views. (Continues the metaphysical-reading line: Jauernig (2021), Willaschek (2018).)
- Gomes et al. (2022), *On the Necessity of the Categories*. Asks whether Kant leaves open the possibility of discursive cognizers whose categories differ from ours, as he leaves open creatures with different pure forms of sensibility. Argues that textual and systematic considerations do not settle the question, and that Kant's wider views on the limits of our knowledge of mind allow him to stay neutral, within unexpected constraints. The result is an asymmetry between other forms of sensibility and other categories, in which Kantian humility takes a distinctive character.
- Pendlebury (2022), *The Shape of the Kantian Mind*. Argues that conceptualist and nonconceptualist readers alike wrongly assume that concepts are involved in intuition only as particular concepts applied in particular exercises of sensibility. On the author's view no concepts are applied in exercises of sensibility, yet the understanding is teleologically internal to sensibility, whose exercises are directed to providing objects to which the categories apply. The picture rests on a distinction between capacities and acts and is said to be demanded by Kant's account of a priori knowledge in the Transcendental Deduction. (Continues the conceptualism debate: McLear (2016), Conant (2016), Watkins and Willaschek (2017).)
- McNulty (ed.) (2022), *Kant's Metaphysical Foundations of Natural Science: A Critical Guide*. A Critical Guide to the 1786 *Metaphysical Foundations*, which accounts for an acting-at-a-distance gravitational force, demonstrates the infinite divisibility of matter and derives analogues to Newton's laws of motion. Its chapters treat matter, motion, the mathematization of nature, space and natural laws, relate the metaphysics of nature to the critical philosophy, and describe the historical context of the work. The volume presents the text as under-studied. (Relates to Friedman (2013).)
- Wilson (2022), *Kant and the Naturalistic Turn of 18th Century Philosophy*. Argues that the critical philosophy was meant as a bulwark against radical naturalism (determinism, atheism, materialism) associated with the radical Enlightenment of Locke, La Mettrie, Buffon, Hume, Herder and others, while leaving room for empirical work in physics, chemistry and the life sciences. Presents Kant as no more than a proponent of a moderate Enlightenment and holds that, on topics such as science, mind-body relations, animals, women and non-Europeans, the more progressive positions were held by his opponents. The NDPR reviewer, Fugate, describes the book as a sharp attack on Kant scholarship and on Kant himself.
- Timmons (2022), *Kant's Doctrine of Virtue: A Guide*. A reader's guide to the Doctrine of Virtue, Part II of Kant's 1797 *Metaphysics of Morals*, in five parts. It covers the background and the general introduction to the *Metaphysics of Morals*, then the introduction to the Doctrine of Virtue (ends that are also duties, radical evil and the nature of virtue, the science of ethics), duties to oneself, duties of love, vices and friendship, and finally moral education and practice. (Relates to Trampota et al. (eds.) (2013) on the Doctrine of Virtue.)
- Herman (2022), *Kantian Commitments: Essays on Moral Theory and Practice*. Ten essays that rethink fundamentals of Kant's ethics. Part One revisits the formulas of the categorical imperative, the idea of making exceptions, and the contrast between Kant's project and other deontologies, especially recent contractualism, reading Kant as giving a theory of moral reasoning with standards of validity and soundness. Part Two takes up Kant's moralized view of history, social pluralism, moral psychology and normal human development, the argument about duties to animals, and the highest good in a morally good life.
- Kim and Schönecker (eds.) (2022), *Kant and Artificial Intelligence*. Asks how artificial intelligence, and the strong claims of its philosophical advocates, look from a Kantian perspective, and conversely what AI and its functions can teach about the validity claims of Kantian philosophy. The volume treats the self, spirit and self-consciousness, ethics, law and aesthetics.
- Pascoe (2022), *Kant's Theory of Labour*. A Cambridge Element that examines Kant's account of labour in his political philosophy and develops an intersectional analysis of Kant. Argues that his treatments of slavery, citizenship and sex developed in linked ways, so that his account of independence is built on his theory of labour and is continuous with his anthropological accounts of race and gender, giving a systematic justification for the dependency of women and non-whites. Adds that Kant's eventual rejection of slavery may owe more to his changing conception of labour than to his theory of race. (Continues the gender-and-citizenship discussion: Sabourin (2021).)
- Stone and Hasan (2022), *What Is Provisional Right?*. Resolves the apparent contradiction in Kant's claim that property claims are possible but merely provisional in the state of nature and conclusive only in a civil condition. Argues that provisional right is not a special kind of right but marks the imperfection of an action, that of acquiring ordinary rights, where public authorization is lacking. On this reading it works as a methodological device in a sequential exposition of the moral basis of public law, developed from Kant's two-step account of having and acquiring. (Relates to Byrd and Hruschka (2010) on private right.)
- Geiger (2022), *Kant and the Claims of the Empirical World: A Transcendental Reading of the Critique of the Power of Judgment*. Argues that a principal concern of the third Critique is to complete the account of the transcendental conditions of empirical experience and knowledge, covering Kant's analysis of natural beauty and his teleological judgments of organisms and of nature generally, so that the third Critique forms a unified whole. The NDPR review adds that the thread of the reading is the principle of the logical purposiveness of nature, and notes that some of its claims are controversial. (Relates to Ypi (2021) on purposiveness.)
- Lu-Adler (2022), *Kant on Lazy Savagery, Racialized*. Argues that Kant develops a concept of savagery, partly characterized by laziness, for a programme of human progress, and racializes it by treating native Americans as literal savages with a hereditary physiological laziness. Because perfection must be reached through industry, which Kant denies to the supposed savages, the racialization blocks any prospect of their fulfilling their perfections despite their having the same germs as Europeans. The case is said to bear on the debated relation between Kant's moral universalism and his racism. (Continues the race debate: Allais (2016), Sandford (2018).)
- Rosen (2022), *The Shadow of God: Kant, Hegel, and the Passage from Heaven to History*. Argues that many apparently secular modern ideas were shaped by religion, with the German Idealists as the thinkers seeking to reconcile faith and reason. For Kant, pursuing the moral law means working with others in an invisible church toward justice in history; later Idealists, dropping personal immortality, put historical immortality through social projects at the centre. The NDPR review calls the book a display of erudition and finds in it in effect two books.
- Basile and Lyssy (2022), *Perspectives on Kant's Opus postumum*. An edited volume on the theoretical elements of the Opus postumum, which it reads as central to the relation between Kant's transcendental philosophy and his natural philosophy, practical philosophy, philosophy of religion and metaphysics, and as showing Kant correcting and developing the critical philosophy. Chapters address the transition from the *Metaphysical Foundations* to physics, the ether and its transcendental deduction, self-affection and the self-positing of the subject, and the idea of God in the highest standpoint of transcendental philosophy. (Continues the transition-project discussion: Hall (2014), Thorndike (2017), Howard (2019).)
- Kant (2022), *Träume eines Geistersehers, erläutert durch Träume der Metaphysik. Historisch-kritische Edition*. A German-language edition of Kant's 1766 *Dreams of a Spirit-Seer*, with introduction and notes by Kreimendahl and Oberhausen (editors as named in web search results for the Meiner edition). The publisher describes it as presenting the text in historical-critical form, with German translations of the Swedenborg passages Kant refers to and commentary linking the treatise to contemporary philosophy and to Kant's pre-critical and critical works. The treatise is read as turning a critique of Swedenborg into a rejection of traditional metaphysics and as already containing basic features of Kant's moral philosophy.

## 2023

The 2023 entries include a book-length treatment of Kant, race and racism (Lu-Adler (2023)), work on freedom and rational agency (Kohl (2023)), the formulas of the categorical imperative (Fahmy (2023), Kleingeld (2023)), the unity of the third Critique and natural history, the late philosophy of nature, sex, love and friendship, and Heidegger's reading of Kant.

- Schafer (2023), *Kant's Reason: The Unity of Reason and the Limits of Comprehension in Kant*. Develops an interpretation of Kant's conception of reason on which theoretical and practical reason are two manifestations of one capacity for theoretical and practical understanding, which the book calls comprehension, both governed by a version of the Principle of Sufficient Reason and both essentially autonomous. The resulting position, rational constitutivism, treats neither substantive reasons or values nor merely structural rationality as fundamental. It contrasts with reasons fundamentalism and with the agency-first constitutivism that has dominated Kantian metaethics. (Follows Schafer (2019) and Schafer (2021).)
- Stang (2023), *Kant's Schematism of the Categories: An Interpretation and Defence*. Reconstructs the argument of the Schematism chapter of the *Critique of Pure Reason*, whose task is to solve the problem of the inhomogeneity of intuitions and categories. The reconstruction rests on two premises, Subsumption Sufficiency and Real Possibility, which with a modal link entail that objects can be subsumed under categories. The author reads transcendental schemata as time-determinations, understood as rules for reflexively representing the temporal relations among our own representational states, and concludes that on this reading the argument is sound.
- Cooper (2023), *Kant and the Transformation of Natural History*. A study of Kant's account of natural history that argues that Kant contributed to the introduction of time into the frame in which explanations are sought and justified in natural science. It challenges the claim that his theory of science rules out a developmental conception of nature, argues that it supplies a method by which natural historians can dispute historical claims, and uses that method to expose flaws in Kant's own historical reasoning, including the formation and defence of his racist views. The NDPR review says the book rejects the standard reading on which natural history was an early interest later sidelined by the critical philosophy. (Has links to the race debate: Sandford (2018).)
- Kohl (2023), *Kant on Freedom and Rational Agency*. Interprets Kant's doctrine of freedom on the view that absolute transcendental freedom, or rational autonomy, is the necessary presupposition of all norm-governed human agency in its moral, epistemic and aesthetic dimensions. The doctrine emerges as a critique of a naturalistic worldview, culminating in the argument that naturalistic cognizers must presuppose freedom in devising rationally valid theories. The NDPR review reports that the book argues against non-metaphysical readings of the practical standpoint and takes moral freedom to depend on the freedom of cognitive faculties. (Relates to Allison (2020) on freedom.)
- Fahmy (2023), *Never Merely as a Means: Rethinking the Role and Relevance of Consent*. Argues that consent-based criteria for using another merely as a means, built over several decades on the Groundwork false-promising example, yield counter-intuitive and un-Kantian verdicts in relatively simple cases. Holds that the example involves a consent-sensitive duty and so encourages too narrow an understanding, since the prohibition covers both consent-sensitive and consent-insensitive duties. Offers a duty-based account that reflects this. (Continues the humanity-formula discussion: Kleingeld (2020), Pallikkathayil (2010).)
- Kleingeld (2023), *A Defense and Development of the Volitional Self-Contradiction Interpretation of Kant's Formula of Universal Law*. Replies to comments by Timmons, Walschots, Romero and Lo Re on the author's 2017 reading of the Formula of Universal Law as requiring that one can will, without volitional self-contradiction, both the maxim as one's own action principle and its becoming a universal law. The reply expands the interpretation, holding that Kant also constructs the diagnostic contradiction between willing a maxim to become a universal law and willing what finite rational beings necessarily will, namely the means to their actual and possible ends. It contrasts the view with the Practical Contradiction and Logical Contradiction interpretations. (Follows Kleingeld (2017).)
- Davies (2023), *Kant on Civil Self-Sufficiency*. Argues that Kant attributes civil self-sufficiency, the qualification for active citizenship and political participation, to those who are not under the authority of any private individual for their survival. The author holds this reading to be more textually grounded than the dominant reading, which understands self-sufficiency in terms of economic relations alone. Adds that Kant's interest in relations of authority reflects a concern to eliminate certain forms of corruption in public lawgiving. (Relates to Pascoe (2022) on passive citizenship.)
- Sweet (2023), *Kant on Freedom, Nature, and Judgment: The Territory of the Third Critique*. Treats the *Critique of Judgment* as examining an independent sphere of human life rather than as serving practical or theoretical interests, and argues that it answers the question what we may hope for. The answer lies in Kant's account of territory, a region of experience that underlies and mediates between freedom and nature. The NDPR reviewer notes that commentators have tended to treat the third Critique as two projects, aesthetics and philosophy of biology, and that the literature stressing its unity is sparse. (Relates to Geiger (2022) on the unity of the third Critique.)
- Clewis (2023), *The Origins of Kant's Aesthetics*. Examines the sources and development of Kant's aesthetics through his publications, correspondence, handwritten notes and university lectures, in eight chapters on aesthetic judgment and normativity, formal beauty, partly conceptual beauty, genius, the fine arts, the sublime, ugliness and disgust, and humor. Considers how Kant's thought was shaped by authors including Wolff, Baumgarten, Meier, Mendelssohn, Sulzer, Herder, Hutcheson, Hume, Burke, Home, Batteux, Rousseau and Voltaire.
- Chignell (2023), *Demoralization and Hope: A Psychological Reading of Kant's Moral Argument*. Examines three varieties of Kant's moral proof under the doctrine of the primacy of the practical, and argues that the third, a moral-psychological argument based on the need to sustain moral hope and avoid demoralization, has advantages over the others. It starts from a premise that coheres better with Kant's mature account of moral motivation and invokes empirical-psychological theses to support a commitment to the full-blown classical deity. (Relates to Höwing (ed.) (2016) on moral faith.)
- Lu-Adler (2023), *Kant, Race, and Racism: Views from Somewhere*. Challenges the assumptions that Kant's racism is personal prejudice that can be disentangled from his core philosophy and that it contradicts his moral theory. Argues that Kant's raciology, divided into racialism and racism, is integral to his philosophical system, and treats racism as an ideological formation in which Kant took part as a scholar who developed a scientific theory of race and as an educator who transmitted denigrating depictions of racialized others. Urges Kant scholars and teachers to engage in antiracist work in a color-conscious way. (Continues Lu-Adler (2022), as the note states, and the race debate.)
- Rinne and Brecher (eds.) (2023), *Kant on Sex, Love, and Friendship*. Collects essays on how sex, love and friendship function in Kant's philosophy, individually and in conjunction. The essays examine the theory of sexuality, marriage (including same-sex marriage), morality and sexual objectification, love and autonomy, love of human beings, the conceptual structure of love, friendship, misanthropy and the highest good. The volume sets this against what it calls common prejudices about Kant's contribution to these topics. (Relates to Papadaki (2010) and Varden (2020).)
- Howard (2023), *Kant's Late Philosophy of Nature*. An Element, according to its abstract, on the Opus postumum, Kant's final drafts attempting a transition from the metaphysical foundations of natural science to physics. It outlines a framework combining the systematic ambition of early twentieth-century readings with the rigor of recent studies, sets aside the question which gap in the earlier philosophy the project was meant to fill, and directs attention to fascicles X and XI and to the arrival point of the transition, Kant's question what physics is. (Follows Howard (2019) and relates to Basile and Lyssy (2022).)
- Lambeth (2023), *Heidegger's Interpretation of Kant: The Violence and the Charity*. Gives an account of Heidegger's method of interpreting Kant, arguing that it is more promising than usually recognized, a failure of recognition that has limited its uptake in anglophone Kant scholarship. On this account Heidegger locates Kant's insights in moments of tension where Kant struggles to put a fresh point into words, so that the interpreter must disentangle competing strands of argument and decide which to favour. The book traces this method through Heidegger's reading of the *Critique of Pure Reason*, situates it against other post-Kantian readings, and shows how the engagement bears on Heidegger's own views. (Relates to Golob (2013), Engelland (2017).)
- Baiasu and Timmons (2023), *The Kantian Mind*. An edited reference volume (CrossRef lists the two editors as authors) in the Routledge Philosophical Minds series, with forty-five chapters in four parts: Background to the Critical Philosophy, Transcendental Philosophy (Critique and Doctrine), Posthumous Writings and Lectures, and Kant and Contemporary Kantians. Besides the main works it covers Kant's views on logic, mathematics, the natural sciences, anthropology, religion, politics and education, and his influence on contemporary analytic and continental philosophy; each chapter has suggestions for further reading. (Relates to Wuerth (ed.) (2021) on reference works.) (Summary based on a publisher description.) (Dated by its CrossRef print or online date; see YEAR NOTE.)

## 2024

In the tercentenary year of Kant's birth, the 2024 entries include the *Oxford Handbook of Kant* (Gomes and Stephenson (eds.) (2024)), imagination and intuition (Matherne (2024)), dignity and Kant's racism (Ameriks (2024)), race, radical evil and the Haitian Revolution (Papish (2024)), Baumgarten and practical philosophy, two-aspect arguments from Kant's language, and a source-materials volume for the second Critique.

- Smyth (2024), *Intuition in Kant: The Boundlessness of Sense*. Offers an overview of Kant's conception of intuition in all its species (divine, receptive, sensible and human). Kant treats sense perception as the paradigm of intuition yet holds that infinities can be represented in intuition; the book argues that the features Kant ascribes to intuition remedy specific cognitive shortcomings arising from the discursivity of our intellect, so that intuition acts as the intellect's cognitive partner. It reconstructs the role of this conception in Kant's philosophy of mind, epistemology and philosophy of mathematics. (Relates to Allais (2010) on the Transcendental Aesthetic.)
- Rosefeldt (2024), *"In Itself": A New Investigation of Kant's Adverbial Wording of Transcendental Idealism*. Investigates the linguistic forms in which Kant expresses transcendental idealism, arguing that Prauss's case for reading *in itself* as an adverbial expression modifying verbs of philosophical reflection is flawed, so that exegetical evidence for methodological two-aspect interpretations is poor. Kant's adverbial uses of *in itself* largely reduce to a standard use modifying verbs of predicative being such as the copula. The paper also discusses how this fits two-object and ontological two-aspect interpretations. (Continues the two-aspect versus two-object debate: Stang (2014), Marshall (2013).)
- Matherne (2024), *Seeing More: Kant's Theory of Imagination*. Defends a systematic interpretation of Kant's theory of imagination, on which imagination is a flexible cognitive capacity exercised as much in relation to real and present objects as to absent or unreal ones. The book traces its exercise in perception and ordinary experience, in the appreciation of beauty and sublimity, in art, and in the pursuit of happiness and of morality, and holds that one unified theory underlies these uses. The NDPR review describes it as answering what imagination is for Kant, against skeptics who assign it little weight in his system. (Relates to Horstmann (2018) on imagination.)
- Russell (2024), *Kant's Fantasy*. Reconstructs Kant's conception of fantasy (Phantasie), the unintentional, unstructured, obscure and pleasurable imaginative activity that his anthropology describes and criticizes, and asks whether it might play a positive role in experience. Kant distinguishes the lawless freedom of imagination in fantasy from the free lawfulness of imagination in aesthetic judgment. Departing from Kant and drawing on Susan Sontag, the paper argues that certain modernist works invite fantasy, so that fantasy belongs to proper aesthetic appreciation while remaining normatively ambiguous.
- Guyer (2024a), *Kant's Impact on Moral Philosophy*. Traces the response to the moral philosophy of Kant's Groundwork, second Critique, *Religion* and *Metaphysics of Morals* from Fichte, Schelling and Hegel through T. H. Green, Josiah Royce and Nietzsche to Rawls, O'Neill, Korsgaard and Parfit. It argues that the responses that develop Kant's ideal of maximal but equal freedom as the inner worth of the world answer charges of empty formalism and of leaving no room for happiness, for responsibility for evil or for moral feeling.
- Ameriks (2024), *Kantian Dignity and its Difficulties*. A systematic and historical study of Kant's conception of dignity. It defends dignity as rooted in a basic capacity of reason for morality and as an unconditional, inviolable feature of all human beings that deserves universal respect, then examines how the notion was clarified, misunderstood or neglected in five contexts (the Baltics and Herder, Berlin and the rise of Fascism, Philadelphia, London and Richard Price, Washington and Thomas Mann), arguing that Kant held racist and anti-Semitic prejudices and a general old-world elitism in conflict with his own principles. (Relates to Sensen (2011) on dignity.)
- Vilhauer (2024), *Kant on Rational Sympathy*. An Element explaining Kant's distinction between rational sympathy, regulated by practical reason and necessary for adopting as our own those ends of others that are contingent from the standpoint of practical rationality, and natural sympathy, which is passive and can prompt affect and dispose us to act wrongly. On the account given, sympathy is a function of a posteriori productive imagination, and in rational sympathy we freely use the imagination to step into the perspectives of others. (Relates to Ware (2014) and Sorensen and Williamson (eds.) (2018) on moral feeling.)
- Guyer (2024b), *The Moral Foundation of Right*. An Element arguing that Kant's Universal Principle of Right follows straightforwardly from the foundational idea of his moral philosophy as a whole. Right is the coercively enforceable part of morality, contrasted with Ethics as the part that is not coercively enforceable, and the author sets this against those who have questioned whether Right is part of morality.
- Papish (2024), *Kant on race and the radical evil in the human species*. Reads the *Religion within the Boundaries of Mere Reason* alongside Kant's natural history writings on the concept of race and the development of the human races, a pairing that the article says the literature has neglected. Argues that this clarifies the claim of a radical evil in the human species: that evil is not a static property but a dynamic, changing defect in human nature that increasingly threatens aspirations to collective moral perfection. (Relates to Papish (2018), Merritt (2021) on radical evil, and Sandford (2018) on race and natural history.)
- Huseyinzadegan (2024), *Conjectures on Kant and the Haitian Revolution*. Puts forward, as a suspicion only, that Kant never thought Black lives had dignity but only price. Following Michel-Rolph Trouillot's argument that the Haitian Revolution was unthinkable for Enlightenment philosophers, it conjectures what Kant could or should have said about the revolution, drawing on his writings on the American and French Revolutions. If the suspicion is right, Kantianism cannot accommodate an antiracist programme, which the article says should change how racism, slavery and colonialism are discussed in Kant scholarship. (Takes the negative side of the systematic-link question: Sandford (2018), Mills (2014).)
- Fugate and Hymers (eds.) (2024), *Baumgarten and Kant on the Foundations of Practical Philosophy*. Thirteen chapters on the historical background and philosophical content of Baumgarten's 1760 *Elements* and its relation to Kant's work. The volume follows the editors' earlier *Kant and Baumgarten on Metaphysics* (2018) and builds on their English translations of Baumgarten's *Elements* and Kant's *Reflections on Moral Philosophy* (2020). It treats the Baumgarten connection as a neglected aspect of classical German philosophy. (Follows Fugate and Hymers (eds.) (2018).)
- Aigner (2024), *Kant and Technics: From the Critique of Pure Reason to the Opus Postumum*. Answers the claim, associated with Heidegger, Simondon and Stiegler, that Kant's philosophy is incapable of thinking technics. Argues that an explicit concept of technics is at work in Kant's philosophy and that, from the *Critique of Pure Reason* to the posthumously published *Opus Postumum*, transcendental philosophy is at once constituted against, reliant upon and proceeding from technics, so that Kant cannot be treated as a techno-oblivious thinker. (Relates to Kim and Schönecker (eds.) (2022) on technology and Howard (2023) on late work.)
- Kinzel (2024), *Neo-Kantian conceptualism: between scientific experience and everyday perception*. Reconstructs the transformations in the Marburg neo-Kantian account of experience by focusing on the problem of conceptualism, linking four issues in the Marburg philosophers' transcendental projects: the interpretation of Kant, the critique of experiential givenness, objective cognition in science, and the relation of scientific and pre-scientific experience. It identifies two shifts: from Cohen's conceptualist answer to subjectivism to Cassirer's functionalist answer to empiricism, and from Cassirer's conceptualism about scientific experience to his symbolic non-conceptualism about everyday perception. (Relates to Heis (2010), Biagioli (2020) on Marburg neo-Kantianism and Pendlebury (2022) on conceptualism.) (Dated by its CrossRef print or online date; see YEAR NOTE.)
- Walschots (2024), *Kant's Critique of Practical Reason: Background Source Materials*. An edition/translation volume of source materials: English translations of texts by Kant's predecessors and contemporaries that he read and responded to in the second Critique, together with several later reactions to it, in eleven chapters with editorial material on each author and text. The NDPR review lists selections from Wolff's German Ethics and Crusius's guide to living rationally, reviews of the Groundwork by Flatt, Tittel and Pistorius, and Wizenmann's 1787 essay, among others. The texts are translations of eighteenth-century sources, not Kant's own works. (Relates to Kant (2015).)
- Gomes and Stephenson (eds.) (2024), *The Oxford Handbook of Kant*. A handbook of commissioned essays that present where the study of Kant's philosophy stands in the twenty-first century, organized around Kant's four questions (what can I know, what ought I to do, what may I hope, what is the human being). CrossRef chapter records show chapters on transcendental idealism, logic, mathematics, the special sciences, autonomy and freedom, virtue, right, religion, evil, hope, education, race, and sex and gender. The entry's note places its publication in the tercentenary year (CrossRef print date 31 October 2024).

## 2025

The 2025 entries include work on the origins and structure of the Critique of Pure Reason (Anderson (2025), Stang (2025)), the Metaphysics of Morals applied to lying and to marriage (Timmermann (2025)), the race debate (Kleingeld (2025)), Kantian ethics and the environment, the Opus postumum, and an edition of student transcripts of the logic lectures. The recorded citation counts for many 2025 entries are low.

- Anderson (2025), *The Skeptical Roots of Critique: Hume's Attack on Theology and the Origin of Kant's Antinomy*. Argues that the Antinomy of Pure Reason, like the Critique of Pure Reason as a whole, stems from Hume's attack on metaphysics, so that Kant's two awakenings from dogmatic slumber, by Hume and by the Antinomy, are reconciled. Traces the Antinomy to Hume's challenge to the causal principle of rationalism and to the metaphysics founded on that principle. (Relates to Proops (2021) on the Antinomies.)
- Stang (2025), *Is Kant's Critique of Metaphysics Obsolete?*. Takes from Kant the problem of what explains why the terms of our metaphysical theories, such as property or grounding, refer to entities and structures in the world. Distinguishes a deflationary meta-metaphysics, which answers this easily, from a substantive view for which the task is harder, and argues that the substantive metaphysician's replies are inadequate or depend on the idea of joint carving.
- Hogan (2025), *Kant's Nutshell Argument for Idealism*. Takes up a passage in which Kant supports transcendental idealism by observing that the whole world could be contained in a nutshell and all elapsed time in a second without any difference being met with. Against the background of Poincaré's and the logical empiricists' treatment of the universal-doubling hypothesis as empty, it asks what the argument means and notes that Kant's wording may signal an idealist argument distinct from those in the published works. (Follows Hogan (2021) and joins the two-aspect line: Jauernig (2021), Rosefeldt (2024).)
- Stan (2025), *Kant's Natural Philosophy*. A Cambridge Element that analyses Kant's metaphysics and epistemology of the exact science of nature, explaining his theory of true motion and his ontology of matter. It reconstructs the patterns of evidential reasoning behind his foundational doctrines. (Relates to Friedman (2013) and McNulty (ed.) (2022).)
- Cureton (2025), *Sovereign Reason: Autonomy and Our Interests of Reason*. Develops from a wide range of Kant's texts a Sovereignty Conception of Reason, on which an autonomous person governs herself completely by reason: reason legislates, executes and adjudicates for all our mental powers and for all kinds of rational standards, and includes substantive interests and a principle of justifiability. Applies it to rational principles of beneficence, coercion, deception, friendship, respect, happiness and self-development. (Continues the constructivism debate: Schafer (2023), Kleingeld and Willaschek (2019).)
- Kain (2025), *Kant and the Origins of the Good Will*. Investigates the emergence of Kant's moral philosophy and the place in it of the good will and the dignity of humanity, working from early published works, notes and drafts, and student notes of lectures before the Groundwork (1785). Begins with a reconstruction of Kant's conception of the divine will and then interprets the emergence of his moral philosophy before the Groundwork.
- Timmermann (2025), *Kant and the Supposed Right to Lie*. A monograph on Kant's 1797 essay *On a Supposed Right to Lie from Love of Humanity*, examining its background (the provocation by Benjamin Constant and his translator Carl Friedrich Cramer), the history of the murderer-at-the-door example (Augustine, Fichte, Michaelis) and Constant's version of the case. Reconstructs Kant's argument that lying, or a right to lie, would undermine contractual rights, and includes a facing-page translation of the essay.
- Heneghan (2025), *Kantian Ethics and Environmental Philosophy: Pursuing the Good in an Age of Crisis*. Argues that a Kantian perspective on the good illuminates a structural tension in moral thought relevant to the environmental crisis. Holds that Kant's ethics shows we are inclined to believe that pursuing our interests is reconcilable with promoting the general good, which leads us to expect environmental win-wins; the author argues that this expectation has no basis in reality and that the way forward in the Anthropocene runs through non-consolatory hope.
- Williams (2025), *Kant Incorporated*. A Cambridge Element arguing that Kant's practical philosophy offers a systematic basis for understanding corporations such as universities, churches, feudal orders and early overseas business corporations. Corporations bridge the distinctions between ethics and right and between public and private right, and can extend freedom, but the unequal liability of those who exercise corporate powers threatens Kant's principle of innate equality. The Element explores the safeguards needed.
- Sabourin (2025), *Kant on Marriage*. A Cambridge Element that reconstructs Kant's account of marriage from its context and background conversations and from the conception of equality at its core. Argues that this equality does not precede marriage and was inaccessible to much of the German population, sets Kant beside the eighteenth-century feminist writers Theodor von Hippel and Marianne Ehrmann, and concludes that his conception is compatible with certain egalitarian goals. (Follows Sabourin (2021); relates to Varden (2020), Papadaki (2010).)
- Teufel (2025), *Kant and Teleology*. A Cambridge Element arguing that Kant's teleological philosophy in the Critique of the Power of Judgment, and a fortiori his aesthetics and philosophy of biology, cannot be understood without a systematic account of the challenges it answers (a threat of transcendental chaos between sensibility and understanding, and of critical chaos between determination and reflection). It also treats its innovations: a transcendental conception of purposiveness, a principle of nature's purposiveness, a self-governing power of judgment, and a view on which nature does make leaps. (Follows Teufel (2012).)
- Kleingeld (2025), *Anti-Racism and Kant Scholarship: A Critical Notice of Kant, Race, and Racism: Views from Somewhere, by Huaping Lu-Adler*. Contests Lu-Adler's claim that the Groundwork concerns only mere rational beings abstracted from embodied humanity, so that contradictions between Kant's moral principles and his racism cannot be set aside. Defends her earlier thesis that Kant abandoned racial hierarchy by the mid-1790s, with the condemnation of colonialism in Toward Perpetual Peace as moral progress, and argues against Lu-Adler's recommendation that scholars set aside questions about contradiction and change in Kant's views. (A critical notice of Lu-Adler (2023).) (Summary based on the publisher's article page.)
- Thomson (2025), *Metaphysics of Nature and Failure in Kant's Opus postumum*. Argues that the Opus postumum is Kant's final attempt to deliver the promised system of speculative reason in a work called metaphysics of nature, and reads the surviving drafts for how they fail to establish that work rather than for projected success. Maintains that this failure gives reason to study the Opus postumum seriously, not to abandon it. (Continues the Opus postumum discussion: Howard (2023), Basile and Lyssy (2022), Hall (2014).)
- Bruno (2025), *Facticity and the Fate of Reason After Kant*. A history of the concept of facticity from Fichte, who coins it to denote brute conditions that reason presupposes, through Hegel's presuppositionless science and Schelling's objection to it, to Heidegger's hermeneutics of facticity. Argues that the post-Kantian trajectory from German idealism to phenomenology is one in which facticity initially impedes the science of intelligibility and then characterizes the situation in which that science is possible. The NDPR review adds chapters on Lask, Dilthey and Heidegger.
- Kant (2025), *Vorlesungen des Herrn Professor Kant über die Logic nach Meier: Nachgeschrieben von Johann Wilhelm Volckmann*. A German-language edition, edited by Erlewein and Walter, of Volckmann's transcript of Kant's lectures on logic (after Meier), from about 1782; it is volume 6 of the series jena-sophia. The publisher describes the logic lectures as the place where many elements of the critical works were successively developed, and says the volume complements previously published transcripts and includes a previously unknown fragment. (Relates to Lu-Adler (2018) on Kant's logic.) (Summary based on a publisher description.)

## 2026

This section covers 2026 through 30 September 2026. The entries include a historicist, religion-centred reading of the critical project (Hunter (2026)), collected essays on transcendental idealism and method (Guyer (2026)), Kant and Freud (Longuenesse (2026)), law and morality in the Doctrine of Right, moral feeling, and Kant and colonial slavery (Lu-Adler (2026), published online without a print date). Indicators for these entries are early, and no NDPR review dated 2026 was found.

- Wolf (2026), *Kant's Conclusions in the Transcendental Aesthetic*. Argues that Kant's claim in the Transcendental Aesthetic that things in themselves are not in space does not deny anything about mind-independent reality: it says only that space is not a necessary constraint on things as a kind. On this reading Kant's view escapes the neglected alternative objection, which rests on taking the Aesthetic to make negative assertions about things in themselves. (Relates to Allais (2010) and Smyth (2024) on the Transcendental Aesthetic.)
- Kleingeld (2026), *The "Striking Similarity" Between Kant's Prolegomena and the Groundwork*. Takes up Kant's remark, made while writing the Prolegomena, of a striking similarity between the central problem of transcendental philosophy and that of moral philosophy. Argues that the two works are closely parallel in their central problems, method, types of argument and terminology, so that the Groundwork is the moral counterpart of the transcendental philosophy, and claims that this clarifies the beginning of Groundwork II and the aim and argument of Groundwork III.
- Guyer (2026), *Selected Essays on Kant II: Method, Idealism, and Purpose*. Collects ten essays published since *Kant and the Claims of Knowledge* (1987) on Kant's critical metaphysics and epistemology. They address the meaning of transcendental idealism while criticising Kant's fundamental argument for it, the value of the transcendental method, Kant's teleological approach to philosophy, his relation to Locke and Leibniz, and the difference between his method and Peter Strawson's. The publisher abstract states that the author won the 2024 International Kant Prize. (Relates to the two-aspect line: Jauernig (2021).)
- Carson (2026), *Philosophy of Mathematics from Descartes to Kant*. A Cambridge Element on the debate in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries over whether metaphysical truths admit proof as certain as geometrical truths. It examines how Descartes, Locke, Leibniz, Wolff, Lambert, Mendelssohn and Kant understood Euclidean demonstration and the certainty it secures, with emphasis on metaphysical and epistemological questions. (Relates to Posy and Rechter (eds.) (2020) and Sutherland (2021).)
- Longuenesse (2026), *The Organization of the Mind: Lessons from Kant and Freud*. Lays out connections between Kant's and Freud's views of the mind, against the accepted contrasts between rational forms necessary for cognition and moral motivation and an irrational unconscious core. Argues that a more fine-grained reading finds proto-Freudian aspects in Kant and elements of a genealogy of the mind in Freud, and draws lessons for philosophy of mind and moral psychology. (Relates to Longuenesse (2017).)
- Schlicht (2026), *Predictive processing's flirt with transcendental idealism*. Argues that many proponents of predictive processing endorse a form of Kantian transcendental idealism, by characterising experiential content as the brain's hypothesis about the world, and that this endorsement undermines the framework's three ambitions: completeness, Bayesian realism and naturalism. The argument draws on philosophy-of-science discussions of realism, computational models and the foundational role of consciousness for science. (Relates to Sturm and Wunderlich (2010) on Kant and the scientific study of the mind.)
- Noller (2026), *Kant on Respect (Achtung)*. A Cambridge Element reconstructing Kant's statements on the moral feeling of respect by its position across the three Critiques, the Religion and the Metaphysics of Morals: noumenal causation, moral motivation, the dynamic sublime, and imputability and education. Argues from the self-reflective volitional structure of respect for a compatibilist account on which both the intellectualist and the affectivist interpretations are true. (Relates to Ware (2014) and Vilhauer (2024) on moral feeling.)
- Khurana (2026), *The Life of Freedom in Kant and Hegel*. Argues that the dominant conception of autonomy generates the paradox of self-legislation and a dualistic opposition of freedom and nature, and develops from Kant's and Hegel's concepts of life an understanding of freedom as a form of life. On this view we are autonomous by inhabiting our living nature in the right way, which calls for a critical theory of second nature. (Relates to Cureton (2025) and Kleingeld and Willaschek (2019) on autonomy; the note says it argues against the self-legislation model.)
- Brecher and Hirsch (eds.) (2026), *Law and Morality in Kant*. A volume of essays on how law and morality relate in Kant: whether law is an application of general moral principles to legal institutions or has its own normativity irreducible to morality. The essays treat the justification of legal coercion, freedom and autonomy in law and politics, legal punishment and its ethical presuppositions, moral luck and permissive laws. (Relates to Guyer (2024b) and Williams (2025) on law and morality.)
- Tuna (2026), *Kantian Art Criticism*. Argues that Kant's aesthetics can ground a theory of art criticism despite his rejection of aesthetic testimony and of general rules of taste. Locates the solution in Kant's judgments of perfection, which mediate between aesthetic evaluations and descriptions of a work's non-aesthetic features.
- Hunter (2026), *The Kantian Religion*. A historical study that treats Kantian philosophy as a form of philosophical religion. Against the view of Kant as supplying a universal theory of knowledge and morality from timeless principles of the human mind, it argues that Kant's arguments were assembled from historical sources and served as ascetic devices for forming the Kantian philosopher's self, which his contemporaries took to be an extra-ecclesiastical path of spiritual and moral regeneration. (Summary based on the publisher page.)
- Lu-Adler (2026), *Kant, the Political Economy of Colonial Slavery, and Racial Capitalism*. - Schnieder (2026), *The Principle of Sufficient Reason in Kant's Nova Dilucidatio*. ## Conclusion

On transcendental idealism, Marshall (2013) offers a qua-object proposal for the one-world reading, Stang (2014) argues against the identity of appearances and things in themselves, and Jauernig (2021) reads critical idealism as having a mind-independent transcendental level and a mind-dependent empirical level. Allais (2010), Schulting and Verburgt (eds.) (2011), Allison (2012), Allais (2015), Schafer and Stang (eds.) (2022), Rosefeldt (2024), Hogan (2025) and Guyer (2026) belong to the same discussion. On perception, Hanna (2011), McLear (2011) and Tolley (2013) argue nonconceptualist positions, Griffith (2012) and Conant (2016) conceptualist ones; Gomes (2014) rejects nonconceptualism while leaving naive realism open, and Stephenson (2015) denies that Kant was a naive realist. Watkins and Willaschek (2017), van Mazijk (2020), Pendlebury (2022) and Kinzel (2024) return to the issue. Stang (2016), Willaschek (2018) and Proops (2021) are book-length treatments of modal metaphysics and the Dialectic.

In ethics, Stern (2011) argues that Kant was a value realist, Wuerth (2014) develops a moral realist account, and O'Neill (2015) and Westphal (2016) present constructivist readings. Kleingeld and Willaschek (2019) restrict autonomy to substantive moral laws, Schafer (2019) and Schafer (2023) defend constitutivism, Cureton (2025) a sovereignty conception of reason, and Khurana (2026) argues against the self-legislation model. The formula entries, Pallikkathayil (2010), Kleingeld (2017), Kleingeld (2020), Fahmy (2023) and Kleingeld (2023), also offer competing accounts; no settlement is recorded.

On race, Kleingeld (2011), Elden and Mendieta (eds.) (2011), Gray (2012) and Mikkelsen (ed.) (2013) precede Flikschuh and Ypi (eds.) (2014), Mills (2014), Allais (2016), Valdez (2017), Mills (2018), Kleingeld (2019) and Basevich (2020). Sandford (2018) links the theory of race to the systematic unity of nature, and Huseyinzadegan (2024) takes the negative side of that question. Lu-Adler (2022), Lu-Adler (2023) and Lu-Adler (2026) examine savagery, racism and colonial slavery in Kant; Kleingeld (2025) is a critical notice of Lu-Adler (2023). Gender: Papadaki (2010), Hay (2013), Varden (2017), Varden (2020), Sabourin (2021), Rinne and Brecher (eds.) (2023), Sabourin (2025). Animals: McLear (2011), Korsgaard (2018), Callanan and Allais (eds.) (2020).

Further strands: Louden (2011), Kant (2012a), Frierson (2013), Frierson (2014) and Cohen (ed.) (2014) on anthropology; Friedman (2013), Massimi and Breitenbach (eds.) (2017), Watkins (2019) and Posy and Rechter (eds.) (2020) on science and mathematics; Longuenesse (2017), Kraus (2020), Longuenesse (2026) and Schlicht (2026) on mind.
