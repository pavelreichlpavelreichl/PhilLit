## Section 2: From Idealism to neo-Kantianism and Cassirer

### 2.1 Fichte, Hegel and the "Kant-to-Hegel" narrative

The standard story of the post-Kantian reception treats the *Idea* as a first step toward Hegel's philosophy of history. The corpus contains no study of Hegel's *Lectures on the Philosophy of History* or of Marx's reading of the 1784 essay, so this story can only be assessed through comparative work. The Fichte link is documented by a single title-level item: Picardi (2013) traces the "guiding thread" of universal history from Kant's *Idea* into Fichte's account of historical development (a pointer only; the record gives no abstract). The Hegel material divides into two positions.

The first holds that Kant's position is unstable and that Hegel's resolves the instability. Rauscher (2002) finds an ambiguity in Kant's theory of history. Kant wants to separate empirical history from reason's a priori regulative role, yet his view of the sciences and of reason precludes the separation. Rauscher traces the problem to the different roles Kant assigns to understanding and reason in experience. In Hegel's theory both together constitute history, which Rauscher takes to make Hegel's the more consistent idealist theory and to explain in part the movement from Kantian to Hegelian thought. The argument turns the regulative reading of the Idea against itself, a challenge that returns in Part II.

The second position resists the picture of continuity. Pinkard (2009) acknowledges that many themes of German Idealist philosophy of history first appear in Kant's essay, so that it is tempting to see Kant as foreshadowing Hegel. Following Kleingeld, however, he rejects both the claim that Kant offers a historicised conception of reason and the claim that Kant's assertions can be made only at the cost of inconsistency with the critical philosophy. The decisive difference is that Kant treats a free political and social life as a regulative ideal that we approximate but never fully attain, whereas Hegel holds a free society possible "in the here and now". Kleingeld's (2001) own contribution is relevant here. She reads Kant's preference for "providence" over "nature" in the *Idea* as the passage from the theoretical question of order in history to a moral approach grounded in the teleological principle. On this reading Kant's progress narrative has its own moral rationale rather than being an incomplete Hegelianism. The disagreement between Rauscher and Pinkard therefore concerns whether the regulative status of the Idea is a defect or a deliberate limit.

Two further items extend the Kantian themes into the Hegelian and Marxian traditions without addressing the 1784 essay's reception directly. Requejo and Valls (2007) trace the thesis of unsocial sociability into Hegel, arguing that both Kant and Hegel are philosophers of history who offer political ways of overcoming conflict, Kant at the individual level and Hegel at the socio-political one. Ypi (2014) compares Kant and Marx on revolution and on its educative role in showing the possibility of moral progress for the species. Her subject is the enthusiasm passage of the *Conflict of the Faculties*, not Marx's reading of the *Idea*.

### 2.2 Neo-Kantian ethical socialism and Cassirer

The neo-Kantian reception was concerned less with the *Leitfaden* than with Kant's ethics and its social implications. Vorländer's (1900) article in *Kant-Studien* is a primary source for this turn. He observes that critical idealism, having entered other disciplines, was now being taken up by the social movement. Neo-Kantians were developing Kant's doctrine on its socio-scientific and social-ethical side, while a call of "back to Kant" came from within party socialism, so that one could almost speak of "socialist Kantians and Kantian socialists". He asks in what respect socialism may appeal to Kant, and means by socialism an ethical outlook rather than a party. Two earlier items are pointers only: Medicus's (1902) two-part article on Kant's philosophy of history in *Kant-Studien* and Krijnen and de Launay's (2013) title-level survey of the philosophy of history in Kant, neo-Kantianism and present-day Kantianism. The corpus holds no record of Windelband or Rickert on the essay.

Recent scholarship qualifies the picture of a unified Marburg socialism. Widmer (2023) argues that the dispute between Cohen and Stammler is symptomatic of a wider current of left-wing Kant interpretation around 1900. Their Kantian justifications of socialism differ in their conceptions of law, history and political implications. Against scholars who treat the Marburg view as a coherent school, she proposes "left-Kantianism" as an open term for a variety of approaches. In a second study (Widmer 2024), she challenges the "purposive labour reading" of Cohen's critique of capitalism, which rests on the demand that humanity be treated as an end. Reconstructing his ethics of cognition, she argues that Cohen aimed at a framework alternative to historical materialism, one that allows social practices to be interpreted in an ethical light. Sieg (2006) asks whether the late Natorp's political philosophy is utopian detachment or perceptive critique of the present. The abstract on record supports nothing more, so Natorp's relation to Kant's cosmopolitan writings cannot be assessed from the corpus.

Cassirer closes this strand. Ferrari (2023) argues that in the early 1920s Cassirer never abandoned his neo-Kantian approach, even as he enriched it with Hegel's *Phenomenology* in a philosophy of culture. Cassirer insists that Kant's critical idealism be contrasted with Hegel's absolute idealism, so that the process of spirit stays open rather than closing in a definitive logical structure. Aramayo (2019) draws a parallel between Cassirer's political turn under National Socialism and Kant's under the French Revolution. On the abstract's account, Cassirer reclaims Kant and the Enlightenment as a whole to defend a republican constitution and the Weimar Republic, and chooses Leibniz, Rousseau, Kant and Goethe as allies in a history of ideas. Together the two studies place Cassirer between Kant and Hegel: he keeps Kant's openness against Hegelian closure and uses Kant's republicanism against the political present. This is a different use of the Kantian philosophy of history from either Hegel's supersession or the socialist programme.

Three limits qualify this section. Cassirer's own writings on Kant and the Enlightenment are not in the corpus, so his reading is known only through Ferrari and Aramayo. No study addresses how directly the 1784 essay, as opposed to Kant's ethics and political writings, figured in the neo-Kantian debates. And the Hegel and Marx material is comparative rather than a reception history. The post-Kantian response to the *Idea* is thus visible mainly in what it set aside (the regulative limit) and what it kept (unsocial sociability, the moral orientation of progress).
