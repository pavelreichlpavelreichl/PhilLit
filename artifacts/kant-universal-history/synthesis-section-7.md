## Section 6: The cosmopolitan aim: federation, right and international order

The essay's title names its aim, and the Seventh Proposition makes the aim concrete: external relations between states must be regulated before the species can fully develop its predispositions. The literature on this proposition divides over three questions. What institution does the 1784 text call for? Can the cosmopolitan condition be grounded without the plan of nature? And how far does the essay's position survive in Kant's later political writings?

Kleingeld (2009) gives the developmental answer to the first and third questions. She argues that the Seventh Proposition advocates a federation of states with coercive powers to enforce its laws, and that this "cosmopolitan condition" secures the stability that the development of reason requires. She also argues that the later texts, namely *Theory and Practice*, *Perpetual Peace* and the *Metaphysics of Morals*, modify this early view in important respects, although the literature usually overlooks the changes. This framing makes the essay the starting point of a trajectory, not a statement of Kant's settled view. It feeds the older dispute over whether the end-state is a league (*Völkerbund*) or a world republic (*Weltrepublik*). Kleingeld's earlier article (2004) takes up the status of the world-federation ideal and the league of states; the entry is known by title only. Höffe (1995) and Hirsch (2018) are pointers to the German-language debate. Hoesch (2012) asks a narrower question, whether the league is limited to republican members, and argues that this common presupposition is unwarranted. Kleingeld (2016) surveys three debates on Kant's political cosmopolitanism, namely the nature of the federation, cosmopolitan right, and colonialism and "race", and the last of these is the subject of Section 7. She also notes that the vocabulary of world citizenship is strikingly rare in Kant's moral theory. Kleingeld (1999) separates six varieties of cosmopolitanism in late eighteenth-century Germany, which situates the title's *weltbürgerlich* among contemporary usages (see Section 1).

Juridical readings propose a different route to the cosmopolitan condition. Flikschuh (2000) argues that Kant's account of political obligation is cosmopolitan in its inception, giving priority to the global over the domestic context, and that the cosmopolitan scope of his theory of justice follows from the metaphysics of freedom as a shared idea of practical reason. Ripstein (2009) reconstructs Kant's legal and political philosophy as a theory of equal external freedom, and Byrd and Hruschka (2009) comment on the *Doctrine of Right* as grounding lasting international peace in the idea of the *Rechtsstaat*. These works do not rest on the plan of nature, so they are a methodological alternative to the 1784 route. Klein (2022) goes the other way, arguing that the system of right is itself teleologically situated and plays the role of a "realist utopia", which links the juridical reading back to the practical-teleological reading in Section 4.

A further group asks what the peace project amounts to politically. Mertens (1995) finds that statist and cosmopolitan readings of Kant's international writings both stem from an ambiguity in them, and proposes an interpretation on which they fit partly within the just-war tradition. Caranti (2006) addresses the realist critique of the project; the abstract records that Kant's theory of peace helped displace realism from its leading position in international relations theory. Brown (2024) offers a source-historical account. He argues that Sieyès, often assumed to be Kant's follower, charted a distinct course, and that *Perpetual Peace* should be read as a curtailment of Kant's earlier cosmopolitan and pacifist idealism in response to the 1790s. This supports Kleingeld's developmental thesis from a different angle. Molloy (2017) argues that Kant's repeated appeals to God and Providence in his international writings, the *Idea* included, can be understood only through the role of belief in God in the critical philosophy. This recalls the Guarantee debate of Section 5.

Contemporary uses of the federation idea are more guarded than the label "Kantian" suggests. Brown (2013) argues that a link between Kant and the European Union holds at best at the level of the first two Definitive Articles, and that the Union falls short of Kant's more radical idea of cosmopolitan right. Ypi (2011) develops a "statist cosmopolitanism" in which the state and cosmopolitan commitments are defended together. Pogge (1988) and Poletti (2021) are pointers to the global-justice and postcolonial-critique literatures.

The corpus has one notable gap here. Propositions 5 to 8 are rarely studied in their own right outside the Critical Guide chapters, and most of the work above reads the essay through *Perpetual Peace* and the *Doctrine of Right*. The question of what the 1784 federation contributes to the philosophy of history, as opposed to what it anticipates in the legal writings, remains largely unaddressed.
