## Section 7: Race, Eurocentrism and the critique of progress

The Ninth Proposition's course from Greece through Rome to Europe, and the Eighth Proposition's expectation that Europe will eventually legislate for the rest of the world, make the essay the point at which the debate about Kant and race meets the philosophy of history. The Herder quarrel's political axis (Section 1) returns here in a different form. The literature divides over whether the racial hierarchy of Kant's 1780s writings was later abandoned, whether it is built into the teleology, and whether progress can be reconstructed or must be given up.

### 7.1 Change of mind versus continuity

Flikschuh and Ypi (2014) anchor the debate in the text. The introduction to their volume notes that in the 1784 essay Kant states, "without any critical distancing," that "our" part of the world will probably eventually give laws to all others. It reads such passages together with Kant's hierarchical account of the races, on which only the white race contains all incentives and talents. Kleingeld (2007) gives the repair position its standard form. Commentators, she says, usually assume that Kant's position stayed stable through the 1780s and 1790s, whether they find an inconsistent universalist or a consistent inegalitarian. She argues instead that he restricted the role of race in the 1790s and dropped the hierarchy for a more egalitarian, cosmopolitan view. Kleingeld (2014) extends the thesis to colonialism. On her account Kant endorsed and justified European colonialism until the early 1790s, his endorsement was tied to the racial hierarchy, and he gave up both in the mid-1790s. This matters for the essay because it implies that the 1784 text predates Kant's own corrections. Bernasconi (2007), Mills (2005), Allais (2016), Hill and Boxill (2000) and Storey (2015) are the main interlocutors on either side, but only as pointers here, since their records carry no abstracts.

Two intermediate positions accept part of this picture. Eberl (2019) argues that Kant's rejection of the traditional discourse of "barbarism" shows a critical stance towards colonial judgements of native peoples before his legal theory, and that his race theory was meant to defend the unity of mankind, not to ground a hierarchy. Mballo (2024) does not deny Kant's racism between 1764 and 1790 and also holds that his aim in the race writings was the unity in diversity of the species, but he agrees that Kant's views changed from 1795. Govedarica and Smajević Roljić (2024) take a deflationary line. They criticise both Kleingeld and Bernasconi, hold that Kant's views evolved but did not change radically, and reject Bernasconi's claim that the third *Critique* and the writings on history present racist views.

The continuity side rejects the premise that racism can be detached from the system. Lu-Adler (2022) argues that Kant's concept of savagery, partly defined by laziness, serves a programme of human progress and is racialised. Native Americans are credited with the same "germs" as Europeans but denied the industry through which perfection is reached. Lu-Adler (2023) argues in book form that Kant's raciology is integral to his system, and she replaces the individualistic question about his beliefs with one about his position as scholar and educator in the formation of racist ideology. Huseyinzadegan (2025) supports this approach. Mosayebi (2025) argues that it makes unnecessary sacrifices and proposes combining it with Kleingeld's individualistic approach.

### 7.2 Is the teleology itself racialised?

The sharper question is whether the teleology of Section 4 carries racial content. Marwah (2022) argues that the teleological framework of Kant's natural history makes race intelligible only in relation to a teleology that limits non-whites' capacity for moral progress. If so, the regulative reading cannot treat the plan of nature as heuristically innocent. McNulty (2025) places Kant's remarks that Indigenous Americans have "no culture" and Black people only a "culture of slaves" within his teleological account of history, where culture is the development of capacities to set and achieve ends. He concludes that these remarks commit Kant to the view that such persons are permanently unable to set their own ends, which supports Mills's Untermensch reading. Larrimore (2008), citing an abstract only, reads race as serving a practical-reason-informed understanding of human destiny and as a model for the pragmatic anthropology without which Kant thought progress impossible. Basevich (2020) describes a racist pragmatic anthropology and a teleological natural history that assumes some groups failed to develop legislative reason. Papish (2024) reads radical evil together with the natural-history writings on race.

Two further positions do not depend on a racialised teleology. Ramsauer (2023) rejects both the "inconsistent egalitarian" and "consistent inegalitarian" readings as textually problematic and argues that Kant's moral egalitarianism is too thin to counter racism. Hahmann (2024) argues that the universalist framework itself can generate pejorative judgements of non-European cultures.

### 7.3 Reconstruction or abandonment of progress?

The remedies differ in how much of the progress narrative they keep. Huseyinzadegan (2019) reads Kant's politics as including a nonideal part, built on history, anthropology and geography, whose purposive and regulative reasoning she distinguishes from the deductive ideal theory of right. Huseyinzadegan (2022) reads Mills's "Black Radical Kantianism" as requiring that the real Kant and establishment Kantianism be admitted to have been racist, sexist and Eurocentric. Basevich (2022) rejects "colorblind cosmopolitanism" and, following Du Bois, assigns race a pragmatic role in history as an instrument of progress. McCarthy (2009) holds the idea of development to be inescapable and so retains it in a "chastened and decentered" form, while Allen (2016) argues that such backward-looking progress cannot ground normativity and recommends a contextualist alternative (see Section 3.3). Valdez (2019) finds limits in Kant's cosmopolitanism that persist in neo-Kantian scholarship and builds a transnational alternative from Du Bois. Gani (2017) uses the concept of coloniality to argue that the universalism of Kantian hospitality obscures its racist context. Pascoe (2024) traces a juridical racism through Kant's account of women's dependency. Inglis (2025) asks whether Kant-derived cosmopolitan theory survives post-colonial critique and argues for its continued timeliness. Chakrabarty (2000) and Mignolo (2011) are pointers to the postcolonial critique of historicism.

These responses divide over a single question, whether the problem lies in Kant's application of the progress narrative or in the narrative's structure. The repair view favours the first, and the continuity and decolonial views favour the second. The corpus has few studies that trace this dispute through the text of Propositions 5 to 9.
