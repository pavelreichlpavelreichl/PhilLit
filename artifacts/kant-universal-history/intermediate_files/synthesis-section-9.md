## Section 8: The essay among Kant's historical writings

A last dispute concerns where the 1784 essay sits in Kant's work on history. The literature divides between readers who find development, with later texts revising the essay, and readers who find unity, with companion texts completing it.

**Development.** Heisenberg (2018) argues that Kant's philosophy of history changed between the 1784 *Idea* and the 1790 third *Critique*. In 1784 progress comes mainly through social antagonism, and from 1790 Kant adds ethical cooperation among moral agents as a second source. This bears on the mechanism of Section 5.2 and on the objection that the essay leaves no place for moral agency. A second revision is placed in the 1790s. Williams (2024) reads the *Renewed Attempt* as complicating the earlier philosophy of history and conceding that rightful progress toward global republicanism is the most we can be sure of. Fenves (2014) takes his title from the *Conflict of the Faculties* chapter on whether the human race is constantly progressing. In the portion of his abstract available here, he argues that continual progress would require a discontinuous, revolutionary advance of positive law toward a natural right determined by reason alone.

**Unity.** Other readers treat the companion texts as completing the essay. Kant's *Conjectural Beginning* (Kant [1786] 2007) supplies the origin narrative that the 1784 text lacks. Lenczewska (2022) reconstructs from it an account of the emergence of reason and the collective development of practical reason, against the view that the beginning of history is philosophically negligible. Lenczewska (2025) connects the speculative account of the beginning to Kant's theory of progress and to his account of what may be hoped for. Oh (2022) argues, in a Korean-language article read through its abstract, that Kant's anthropology rests on a twofold teleology, in which natural teleology extends into historical teleology and the hope for humanity's moral perfection is in turn built on natural teleology. Reichl (2019) offers a methodological parallel. Kant long held that the historiography of philosophy has little value, and Maimon's response to a Royal Academy prize question led him to see the history of philosophy as philosophically significant. The case differs from the 1784 programme, but it resembles it in preferring a philosophical history to an empirical one.

**Anthropology and Enlightenment.** The essay's claims about the species draw on anthropology. Brandt (1996) traces the philosophy of history to the pragmatic anthropology that Kant developed from the early 1770s, and offers an alternative to reading the essay from the critical system. The sources lie in Kant's lectures (Cohen 2014), and the published *Anthropology* (Kant [1798] 2007) ends with the character of the species. Louden (2011) reads this material as integral to practical philosophy. Deligiorgi (2005), finally, studies Kant's conception of enlightenment and its culture of public reason, which bears on the relation of the *Idea* to *What is Enlightenment?* of the same year.

The two readings make different claims about what the essay is. On the first it is an early stage that Kant later qualified. On the second it is one part of a programme that spans the pre-critical, critical and late periods. The corpus offers no study that sets out the essay's relations to these texts proposition by proposition, and this is one of the gaps noted in the Conclusion.
