## Section 3: Twentieth-century reappropriations

### 3.1 Secularisation and conceptual history

Twentieth-century readers often treated the *Idea* as a case within a larger story about modernity. Löwith's thesis, as Chorell (2021) reports it, is that modern philosophy of history and the idea of progress are secularised Christian eschatology. Löwith's own chapter (1983) is a pointer only, since the record does not show how he treats Kant. Chorell's abstract does not mention Kant either; it concerns Svenungsson's postsecular response to Löwith. Lloyd (2009) supplies the Kantian instance. She argues that the *Idea*'s treatment of Providence marks a transition in which Providence becomes the secular idea of progress, and she links this to the cosmopolitan aim and to the narrative of human origins. Wittwer (1996) relates a "transcendental eschatology" to Kant's critical view of history; the abstract on record does not show whether he takes up the secularisation line. Palti (2018) offers a genealogical rather than normative treatment. He places philosophies of history within Koselleck's *Sattelzeit* and Foucault's "Age of History," and notes that Koselleck cites Herder's *Metakritik* of Kant for the plurality of historical times. This returns to the Herder quarrel of Section 1: on this account the dispute concerned the very concept of "History" in the singular that the *Idea* presupposes. Koselleck's (1987) *Critique and Crisis* is a pointer only.

### 3.2 Judgment, spectatorship and the "sign of history"

A second line of reappropriation moves away from the *Leitfaden* altogether. Arendt's lectures on Kant's political philosophy draw on the third *Critique* and the spectator's judgment. Beiner (1997) restates the project, which he says is to ground human dignity in publicly displayed words and deeds, and argues that the conception responds to Plato and to Hegel's historicism and leads to "a sometimes onesided and misleading reading of Kant." Degryse (2011) defends Arendt against two criticisms, that she holds two theories of judgment and that she empiricalises Kant's transcendental principles. She argues that Arendt reads the third *Critique* as an answer to the question "How do I judge?" and finds in "community sense" a foundation for the idea of humanity. Blumenthal-Barby (2021) argues that Arendt's own distinctions in the lectures often falter. None of these abstracts discusses the 1784 essay.

The French readings centre on the 1798 enthusiasm passage. Calcagno (1995) contrasts Kant's view, on which the disinterested sign of the Revolution confirms a moral tendency toward improvement, with Lyotard's denial that any objective proof of such a tendency is possible. Lyotard's (1986) own book is a pointer only. Clewis (2009) reads the same passage through the sublime, connecting enthusiasm to the freedom of individuals and to the moral character of the species. d'Entrèves (2000) describes Foucault's "qualified defence" of Enlightenment as a "philosophical ethos" of critical attitude to the present, drawn from the companion essay *What is Enlightenment?* (Foucault 1984 is a pointer only). In each case the progress thesis is either reduced to a sign or dropped in favour of an ethos. These readings raise the question of which uses are faithful to Kant and which instrumentalise him, and they largely bypass the 1784 essay.

### 3.3 Critical theory: progress after catastrophe

Critical theory retained the question of progress while doubting its guarantees. Honneth (2007) asks what meaning Kant's hypothesis of progress and the "signs of progress" can have today. He distinguishes system-conforming from system-bursting versions and argues that only the latter, a conflict-ridden learning process "without illusions," can confer contemporary meaning on Kant's philosophy of history. Frelek (2025) rereads Adorno's 1962 essay "Progress" in the light of the climate crisis, tracing Adorno's debt to Benjamin and his account of the theological inheritance of progress philosophies, in order to retain a form of negative hope. Pensky's (2010) Kant-Benjamin comparison is a pointer only. The critique turns from catastrophe to empire in McCarthy (2009), who holds that the idea of development is "dangerous and necessary," since variations of it have underwritten global white supremacy, and who retains Kant's cosmopolitanism in discourse-ethical form. Allen (2016) argues that Habermas and Honneth rely on a backward-looking, Eurocentric idea of progress as accomplished, and proposes a contextualist alternative drawn from Adorno and Foucault. Section 7 returns to McCarthy and Allen.

### 3.4 Cosmopolitan constitutionalism, IR, and systematic readings

The third reappropriation rebuilds the cosmopolitan aim in legal-political form. Fine and Smith (2003) describe Habermas's post-1989 attempt to reconstruct Kant's theory of cosmopolitan right in response to twentieth-century catastrophes and globalisation. Habermas, following Apel, aims to "think with Kant against Kant" by correcting inconsistencies, radicalising Kant's break from the nation-state order and linking peace to social justice; Fine and Smith examine the difficulties of grounding this normative commitment. Habermas's own texts (1992; 2008) and Rawls's (2000) Kant lectures are pointers only. In international relations, Hurrell (1990) examines Kant's place in the discipline and the "Kantian paradigm," while Doyle (2017) traces Kant's liberal legacies for foreign affairs, the source of the democratic-peace literature. Höffe (2007) presents the opposite emphasis, a universal cosmopolitanism that combines intercultural validity with a right to cultural difference and draws on education, philosophy of history, teleology and aesthetics.

German and French systematic readings tie the essay back to Kant's own system. Brandt (1996) traces the philosophy of history to Kant's pragmatic anthropology, from the 1772-73 lectures and the 1773 turn to a "pragmatic" practical philosophy. Muglioni (1996) reads the teleological principle as grounded in practical finality, so that the Copernican revolution is complete only when the whole of creation is thought in terms of freedom. Pollmann (2011) argues that Kant was aware of the disheartening picture of human development, and that philosophy of history serves to console and "heal" the melancholy that the horrors of history produce. Höffe's (2011) edited commentary volume is the entry point to German commentary on the essay. Yovel's 1980 book is attested here only by reviews (Galston 1980; Williams 1981), so its argument is not characterised.

Three directions emerge. Some readers push away from the *Leitfaden* toward judgment and spectatorship. Others hold that it is superseded (Hegel) or secularised theology (Löwith). Others rebuild it in legal-political form. A reception history focused on the essay itself remains to be written.
