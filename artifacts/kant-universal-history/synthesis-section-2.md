## Section 1: Contemporaries: Herder and the first quarrel over universal history

### 1.1 The Herder dispute: three readings

The earliest sustained response to the project of the 1784 essay came from Kant's former student. According to the Cambridge translator's introduction, Herder had been Kant's student in Königsberg between 1762 and 1765 and had become a critic of the Enlightenment in the 1770s. He published the first volume of the *Ideas for the Philosophy of the History of Humanity* in 1784, and Kant reviewed the first two volumes in 1785 (Kant [1785] 2007). The review does not mainly defend the *Leitfaden* of the *Idea*. The translator reports that Kant's chief objections concern Herder's derivation of human characteristics from upright posture, and his use of analogies of nature to argue for the soul's immortality. Kant is described as regarding Herder's ideas as lacking rigour and as letting "poetic imaginings" stand in for clear thinking. The second review turns to Herder's reading of Genesis and to his view of reason as the source of the Fall (Kant [1785] 2007). Kant's 1786 "Conjectural Beginning" is a further reply on the same ground, offering a Genesis-based origin narrative on Kant's own terms (Kant [1786] 2007). Wood (1999) opens his account of Kant's "history of human nature" with the Herder contrast and links Kant's turn to history to the articulation of his moral philosophy. The quarrel is therefore one in which both sides were already working out their positions.

The secondary literature can be organised by the axis on which it locates the disagreement. The standard frame is historicist. Beiser (2011) presents German historicism as the tradition holding that history is the key to all human values, beliefs and actions, and Herder stands near its origin. Read in this frame, Herder objects to Kant's Idea as an external, rationalist imposition on history. The revisionist works below do not deny the disagreement but relocate it, and each can be placed on one of three axes.

*Method.* The first axis is Kant's charge that Herder substitutes imagination and analogy for rigour. Reichl (2021) analyses the review in terms of analogical inference and indirect cognition, as an application of Kant's standards for inference about nature and history (a pointer only; the record gives no abstract). Nisbet (2021) places Goethe, Herder and Kant in a shared debate about natural and human history, which suggests that the disagreement concerns how far natural history can be extended into human history (the record's abstract describes only the volume). McNulty (2022) adds a complication. Kant dismisses the "daring adventures of reason" of Buffon and Herder, yet his own race essays rely on teleological speculation about nature's purposes to unify species. On McNulty's account, Kant's position threads a needle between mechanical accounts and "freewheeling, conjectural narratives". The methodological charge thus has to be reconciled with Kant's own practice, and the question of how legitimate the teleology of the *Idea* is returns in Part II.

*Happiness and ethics.* The second axis is ethical. Sikka (2007) takes up the passage of the *Ideas* on which Kant's second review concentrates, where happiness is "everywhere an individual good", climatic and organic, and a "child of practice, tradition, and custom". She presents Herder's valuation of happiness and its diversity as a considered rival to Kant's subordination of happiness to morality and to the species' vocation (*Bestimmung*), which is the problem posed by the Second and Third Propositions of the *Idea*. In her book on Herder, Sikka (2011) develops this into "a qualified relativist" position that pairs a common human nature with the shaping force of culture, and that includes the thesis that no single best society can be constructed. Her Herder is neither a simple relativist nor an opponent of development, and this undermines the opposition between historicism and universalism. Ameriks's (2012) study of the reviews as "fateful" for Kant and for later German philosophy is a pointer to the same dispute; the record gives only its title.

*Politics.* The third axis is political. Piirimäe (2023) reconstructs Herder's engagement with Rousseau, Montesquieu, Abbt, Ferguson, Möser and Kant on patriotism, commerce and peace, and presents him as a critic of colonialism and imperialism who envisaged Europe as a commercially and culturally connected community of peoples. This contests the image of Herder as a cultural nationalist set against Kantian cosmopolitanism. It also makes the quarrel one over the shape of a cosmopolitan order, which links it to the federation of the Seventh Proposition.

Two further works are pointers only. Sakabe (1989) treats freedom as a regulative principle in the controversy (title-level), and van der Zande's (2004) review is the available trace of Zammito's monograph on Kant, Herder and anthropology. These three axes return in Part II as the debates over teleology, over history and morality, and over Eurocentrism.

### 1.2 Other contemporaries

Herder was not the only early interlocutor. Kleingeld (1999) distinguishes six varieties of cosmopolitanism in late eighteenth-century Germany, which provides a context for what "cosmopolitan" meant in the 1784 title. Her book (Kleingeld 2011) sets Kant against Cloots, Forster, Hegewisch, Wieland and Novalis. She argues that Kant's cosmopolitanism changed radically in the mid-1790s, so that the early statement of 1784 is not the same position as his later one. Forster's 1786 critique of Kant's race theory is the setting of Mensch's (2025) account, which turns to Kant's *Physical Geography* lectures and finds Kant asking questions similar to Forster's. Here the disputes over human unity and diversity, which Part II takes up under race and Eurocentrism, appear in the first years of reception. Schiller's 1789 Jena lecture on universal history is a further contemporary reworking. Martinson (1988) reads it as showing the "complex, open-ended nature" of eighteenth-century conceptions of history, in contrast to Kant's, Herder's and the Göttingen historians' (on the evidence of the abstract, which is incomplete).

The first reception thus already contains, in outline, the objections later raised by historicists, eudaimonists and anti-imperialists. The corpus covers Herder well, but it does not cover the wider Göttingen historiography or Kant's other early reviewers.
