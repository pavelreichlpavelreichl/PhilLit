## Introduction

Few contrasts in the history of philosophy are as arresting as the one that organizes the scholarship surveyed in this review. The same thinkers who gave modern philosophy its most powerful vocabulary of autonomy, dignity, universal moral equality, and cosmopolitan right — Immanuel Kant above all, but also Hegel and their contemporaries — produced systematic theories of racial hierarchy and Eurocentric world history. Kant ranked the four human races in ascending order of rational capacity, placing white Europeans at the summit and Africans at the base; Hegel excluded Africa from "world history" altogether and theorized non-European peoples as standing outside the development of freedom. These are not marginal remarks buried in occasional writings. As Bernasconi (2002) argues, Kant should be recognized as a principal constructor of the modern philosophical concept of race, giving it the systematic grounding it had previously lacked. Eze (1996) demonstrates that race is constitutive of Kant's anthropology and empirical geography, not an embarrassing appendix to it. And James and Knappik (2024) observe that, for Hegel as well, the entanglement between progressive ideals and colonial ideology has been underexplored relative to its depth and systematic reach.

The question that runs through every strand of this literature is whether these two faces of German Idealism — its emancipatory universalism and its racial hierarchism — stand in tension or in continuity. The tension reading holds that Kant's and Hegel's racism represents an inconsistency: philosophers committed to universal rational dignity failed, historically and contingently, to apply their own principles consistently to all human beings. On this view, the philosophical frameworks can be retrieved and corrected. The continuity reading holds that the racial hierarchies are not accidental prejudices but are structurally woven into the very architectures of these philosophies — into Kant's teleological natural history, his philosophy of progress, and his cosmopolitan right; into Hegel's logic of world-historical development and his dialectic of recognition. On this view, retrieval without fundamental reconstruction is not available. Mills (1997) offers one of the sharpest versions of the continuity claim: the social contract tradition, including Kant's contractarian ethics, rests on an unacknowledged "Racial Contract" that defines who counts as a full rational person, rendering the universalism structurally partial. Kleingeld (2007), by contrast, argues that Kant underwent a genuine revision of his racial views in the 1790s, moving toward the moral egalitarianism his own universalism demanded — a "recantation thesis" that has anchored the rehabilitative tradition and generated the field's central ongoing debate.

Why does this question matter beyond the historical record? The answer bears on several concerns simultaneously. First, it determines whether Kantian and Hegelian frameworks can be deployed against colonialism today, or whether doing so requires working against the grain of the very conceptual architectures one is invoking. Second, it raises questions about how philosophy reads its own canon: if racial exclusion was not incidental but constitutive, then the canon itself was formed in ways that demand methodological reckoning. Third, it bears on the relationship between a philosopher's empirical prejudices and their systematic thought — a question with ramifications extending well beyond this historical case. The debate, as Lu-Adler (2023) shows in her recent treatment of Kant, is not merely about what Kant said but about what kind of philosophical work racism can do within a systematic philosophy committed to rational universalism.

This review maps the scholarly terrain across five sections. Section 1 establishes the textual and philological foundation: what Kant's race theory actually says, how it was constructed, and how it relates to his broader critical philosophy. Section 2 examines the central rehabilitative claim — Kant as anti-colonialist — and its critics, tracing the developmental-versus-continuity dispute as it plays out in the domain of cosmopolitanism and legal right. Section 3 carries the entanglement question to Hegel, where it appears with particular sharpness because the exclusions seem logically required by the system rather than merely historically contingent. Section 4 presents the external challenge: postcolonial and decolonial critiques that question whether Enlightenment reason is structurally colonial, alongside the Fanon-Hegel interface that concentrates the choice between immanent appropriation and decolonial break. Section 5 makes the organizing question explicit as a meta-philosophical problem, examining the separability/entanglement debate and its implications for how the discipline understands its own canon and its contemporary responsibilities.

## Section 1: The Textual Foundation: Kant's Race Theory and Its Place in the System

### 1.1 The Structure and Sources of Kant's Race Theory

Any assessment of the relationship between Kant's philosophy and colonialism must begin with what the texts actually say. Across four decades — from the lecture notes on Physical Geography in the 1750s through three dedicated race essays (1775/1777, 1785, 1788) to the Anthropology from a Pragmatic Point of View — Kant produced a systematic, teleologically grounded account of race that was not a set of passing remarks but a sustained theoretical project. Robert Bernasconi argues that Kant should be recognized as a constructor of the modern scientific concept of race: his 1775 essay was the first to give race a rigorous philosophical foundation, pairing biological monogenism — the view that all humans descend from a single common stock — with a normative hierarchism that ranked four races in terms of their capacity for civilization and moral development, with white Europeans at the summit and Africans portrayed as deficient in rational self-governance (Bernasconi 2002). What distinguished Kant's contribution was precisely this combination: monogenism meant races shared a common origin and were not separate species, but the theory of fixed, hereditary germs (*Keime*) meant that racial characteristics, once triggered by climate and environment, became permanent and heritable. Kant thereby gave racial hierarchy a philosophical architecture it previously lacked.

Alix Cohen reconstructs the biological premises underlying this architecture. Kant's commitment to epigenesis — the view that organisms are not preformed but develop through an active formative power — is paired with monogenism to generate his account of race as a fixed modification of a common human stock (Cohen 2006). The germs are latent predispositions within the generative power of the human body, activated once and then reproduced unchanged in all subsequent descendants. This biological framework explains why Kant regarded racial characteristics as permanent rather than merely typical: they are not culturally acquired variations but nature's own marks, stamped in and irreversible. Cinzia Ferrini has extended this analysis to the first Critique, arguing that the same conceptual apparatus — germs, predispositions, and the epigenesis model — appears in the Transcendental Deduction of the Categories, where Kant endorses epigenesis as a model for his system of pure reason (B167), suggesting a structural analogy between the a priori origins of pure concepts and the predisposed origins of racial characters (Ferrini 2023). If this connection holds, the race theory is not a detachable appendage to the critical system but shares its generative vocabulary.

The methodology through which Kant developed his race theory is itself philosophically significant. Huaping Lu-Adler shows that Kant operated as a *Naturforscher* — a philosophical investigator of nature — who used travel reports as a form of testimony, selecting, interpreting, and organizing travelers' accounts by means of philosophical reason, in the manner of a judge compelling witnesses to answer the questions put to them (Lu-Adler 2022). This rationalist theory of testimony explains both Kant's confident empirical generalizations about peoples he never observed and his selective use of travel literature that confirmed rather than challenged his racial schema. The methodology of the race essays is thus continuous with Kant's broader natural-philosophical practice rather than departing from it.

Stella Sandford traces how this natural-philosophical ambition links race to the structure of Kant's critical philosophy. She argues that Kant's distinction between natural description (*Naturbeschreibung*) and natural history (*Naturgeschichte*) proper — the latter requiring a genuinely historical, teleological account of nature's development — drove him toward a theory of race as the first developed model of teleological judgment (Sandford 2018). The problem of explaining unified human diversity (one species, four races) was the site where Kant first worked out the idea of teleological natural history, and the solution he reached fed directly into the first and third Critiques. Mark Larrimore, drawing on a wider sweep of Kant's career, argues that Kant effectively "invented" whiteness alongside race: his four-race schema placed white Europeans simultaneously as a race and as the transcendence of race — the norm against which the others were measured — and this schema gained canonical status in the 1790s precisely because it was entangled with Kant's larger project of pragmatic anthropology (Larrimore 2008).

### 1.2 How Deep Does Race Go? Teleology, the Critiques, and Culture

The existence and structure of Kant's race theory is not seriously disputed; the contested question is whether it is philosophically incidental to or constitutive of the critical system. A significant cluster of recent scholarship argues for constitutive entanglement, developing the claim along several intersecting lines.

Inder Marwah argues that Kant's racial theory is only fully intelligible within his teleological natural history, and that this embedding has a normative consequence that cannot be escaped by pointing to Kant's late cosmopolitanism: the teleological framework structurally constrains non-white peoples' capacity for moral progress (Marwah 2022). On Kant's account, nature's teleological purpose is the development of human rational capacities, but this development is racially differentiated at the level of the natural-historical germs. Races that nature has equipped with weaker predispositions for rational culture face a teleological ceiling on their moral development that is, by definition, not a contingent social deficiency to be overcome but a feature of their place in nature's purposive order. Marwah's argument cuts across the developmental debate: even a reformed, late Kant remains committed to a teleological natural history that assigns differential moral-developmental capacities by race.

Reza Mosayebi has refined this analysis using the contemporary taxonomy of the metaphysics of race. He argues that Kant's position is distinct from race essentialism and race naturalism in a philosophically important way: it is "race teleo-rationalism," grounding racial hierarchy not in brute empirical facts but in a priori teleological principles of reason (Mosayebi 2025). This distinctiveness is what makes Kant's racism particularly significant for current debates. If racial hierarchy can be licensed by rational, a priori principles — if reason itself, operating teleologically, generates the racial rankings — then the universal pretensions of Kantian rationality are implicated rather than preserved. Mosayebi's second study extends this analysis to the concept of variety (*Varietät*), a sub-racial category that Kant restricts to the white race and that provides the internal diversity necessary for cultural progress (Mosayebi 2025b). Kant's opposition to race mixing is not a peripheral biological prejudice but is integral to his teleological account of civilizational development: white sub-racial variety is the engine of culture, and its dilution by racial mixing would undermine the diversity that drives human progress. This reveals a structural connection between Kant's race theory and his philosophy of culture that had not previously been analyzed.

Michael Bennett McNulty develops the entanglement claim through Kant's concept of culture (*Kultur*). In Kant's teleological system, culture refers to the possession of developed capacities to achieve the ends one sets for oneself, and Kant understands the achievement of culture as part of the development of human beings into members of a socialized, moral kingdom. McNulty argues that Kant's remarks on the cultural limitations of Indigenous Americans and Black Africans commit him to the claim that these peoples are incapable of genuine end-setting, and that these limitations are hereditary and permanent (McNulty 2025). Culture, so understood, becomes the hinge connecting Kant's teleology to his racial hierarchy, and the connection is internal to the system rather than a misapplication of universalist principles.

Sandford and Ferrini, approaching the same question from the side of the Critiques, argue that the connection runs in both directions. Sandford's analysis shows that the philosophical problems first articulated in Kant's race theory — how to explain unity in diversity, how to give natural history scientific status, how to use teleological judgment — were subsequently transformed into the problems of systematic unity in the first and third Critiques (Sandford 2018). The race theory is not an application of a pre-existing critical framework; it is one of the generative sites from which the critical framework itself emerged. Ferrini's argument about epigenesis as a shared model for race and the system of pure reason reinforces this: Kant does not merely use race as an illustration of teleological judgment, he builds his transcendental account of the origins of pure concepts using the same structural logic that governs his account of racial germs (Ferrini 2023).

Together, these analyses map a spectrum of entanglement claims — from Marwah's teleological ceiling on moral progress, through Mosayebi's a priori grounding of racial hierarchy in reason's own principles, to McNulty's systematic exclusion via the concept of culture, and Sandford's and Ferrini's arguments for a generative rather than merely applicative relationship between race theory and the critical system. What they share is the conclusion that Kant's racial hierarchy cannot be dismissed as an unfortunate empirical prejudice that sits uneasily alongside an otherwise intact critical philosophy. The textual record is secure and systematic; what remains contested — and what the subsequent sections of this review address directly — is the interpretive consequence: whether the entanglement is so deep as to compromise the critical system as a whole, or whether internal resources remain for correction and reconstruction.

## Section 2: Kant's Universalism on Trial: Cosmopolitanism, Anti-Colonialism, and the Developmental Debate

### 2.1 The Recantation Thesis and Its Rivals

The most contested interpretive question in recent Kant scholarship is whether the mature political writings represent a genuine break with the racial hierarchy of the 1780s. Pauline Kleingeld's developmental thesis, first advanced in a 2007 article and elaborated in her 2011 monograph, holds that the answer is yes. Kleingeld argues that Kant's 1780s texts — the race essays, the Anthropology lectures — defended white superiority as part of a natural-historical framework, but that by the time of *Toward Perpetual Peace* (1795) and the *Metaphysics of Morals* (1797), Kant had restricted the concept of race to its proper natural-scientific domain and abandoned the normative hierarchy of racial capacities. This revision, on Kleingeld's account, was philosophically motivated: the internal demands of Kant's developing cosmopolitan ethics required a genuinely egalitarian account of peoples (Kleingeld 2007; 2011). In a further essay focused specifically on colonialism, she argues that Kant's earlier endorsement of European expansion was underwritten by the racial hierarchy, and that abandoning the hierarchy in the 1790s entailed abandoning that justification — replacing it with an egalitarian cosmopolitan right that condemns colonial appropriation (Kleingeld 2014).

Against this thesis, Robert Bernasconi has reconstructed what he calls Kant's "third thoughts" on race. He identifies the 1788 essay "On the Use of Teleological Principles in Philosophy" — Kant's response to Georg Forster's challenge — not as a softening of racial theory but as its philosophical consolidation: here Kant grounds racial taxonomy more firmly in teleological natural history, elaborating rather than retracting the hierarchical framework. On Bernasconi's reading, this elaboration represents the continuity of Kant's race theory even at its most sophisticated (Bernasconi 2011). The implication is that the developmental thesis misreads the 1790s by ignoring the theoretical deepening that immediately preceded it.

Huaping Lu-Adler has developed the most sustained recent version of the continuity case. Her 2022 article examines Kant's treatment of colonial slavery and argues that his belated criticisms of certain slavery practices are framed in the language of trade rights and contractual violations rather than in the language of racial equality. Kant condemned specific configurations of slavery without dismantling the racial hierarchy that structured them; the shift in his practical conclusions does not license the inference to a shift in his anthropological framework (Lu-Adler 2022). In her 2023 monograph and in her response to Kleingeld's critical notice, Lu-Adler sharpens this into a methodological point: reading Kant's racism as an ideological formation — one that shaped and legitimated racial domination through his role as educator and natural philosopher — shows that the question cannot be resolved by examining late textual revisions alone, since ideology operates at a structural level not fully visible in explicit retraction (Lu-Adler 2023; 2025). Lu-Adler argues that the demands of antiracism require a more thorough critique of Kant's philosophy than Kleingeld's developmental reading permits.

Charles Mills contributes a different angle of attack. In his 2014 revisitation of the debate, Mills maintains that Kant's anthropological writings imply a hierarchical account of personhood under which Africans and others fall below the threshold of full moral agency demanded by the categorical imperative — a "sub-personhood" thesis. Kleingeld's developmental account, Mills argues, underestimates how philosophically deep the contamination runs: revising explicit racial ranking leaves intact the structure of graded personhood that gave that ranking its normative force (Mills 2014).

Inés Valdez offers yet a different dissent, not by defending the continuity of Kant's racism but by challenging the framework through which Kleingeld connects racial revision to anti-colonialism. On Valdez's account, Kant's condemnation of colonial wars in *Toward Perpetual Peace* is grounded in his theory of just war and republican right: wars for commercial expansion or territorial acquisition violate republican principles because they are unauthorized by citizens and undermine the conditions for perpetual peace. Race is not the pivot on which Kant's anti-colonialism turns; accordingly, the anti-colonialism *co-exists* with the racial hierarchy rather than depending on its revision (Valdez 2017). This reading has the important implication that Kant's legal anti-colonialism may be more robust than the developmental thesis implies — but precisely because it was never tied to racial egalitarianism in the first place.

### 2.2 The Legal Architecture vs. the Structural Critique

The legal dimension of Kant's anti-colonialism has received careful treatment in its own right. Arthur Ripstein analyses Kant's condemnation of colonial appropriation through the framework of the *Doctrine of Right*, arguing that colonialism violates the a priori conditions of rightful external relations: it forcibly incorporates peoples into a legal order without their consent, thereby undermining public right at its foundation. The condemnation is not a moral sentiment or an empirical policy judgment but a conclusion derived from the juridical structure of cosmopolitan law (Ripstein 2014). Peter Niesen reaches a convergent conclusion through a close reading of the hospitality right in *Toward Perpetual Peace*: Kant's distinction between a right to visit and a right to settle entails the explicit denial of any settler entitlement to appropriate land already occupied by indigenous peoples, constituting a normative prohibition on colonial dispossession (Niesen 2007). Christopher Meckstroth complicates this picture by reading the hospitality right not as an affirmation of cosmopolitan universalism but as a critique of the pretension of any universal order to impose itself on different political communities; on his account, hospitality sets limits on what one community may demand of another, thereby also foreclosing the civilizing-mission argument as a justification for colonial intervention (Meckstroth 2017). Together, these legal-architectonic readings suggest that Kant's anti-colonialism has genuine juridical teeth, grounded in the theory of right rather than contingent on historical revision. Sankar Muthu situates Kant within a broader Enlightenment anti-imperialist tradition, though noting that Kant's universalism differs from Herder and Diderot's cultural pluralism by grounding opposition to empire in the moral status of rational agents rather than in the value of cultural diversity (Muthu 2003).

The structural critics accept that this juridical framework is real but argue that it does not resolve the problem, because race is internal to the philosophical architecture that generates it. Todd Hedrick argues that Kant's cosmopolitan project of cultivating human talents toward rational self-governance presupposes the anthropological framework in which non-European peoples are positioned as less developed in their capacity for such cultivation — racial difference thus enters not as an incidental remark but as a structural presupposition of the normative architecture itself (Hedrick 2008). Jasmine Gani extends this argument to the hospitality right specifically: the universalism of Kant's hospitality is achieved by abstracting from the racial differences that his anthropological writings posit, producing a formal equality that conceals rather than dismantles the substantive racial hierarchy beneath it (Gani 2017). Inder Marwah focuses on Kant's teleological philosophy of history, arguing that the teleological framework assigns different races different positions in the developmental scheme oriented toward rational self-governance; this teleological racism persists structurally even after explicit racial ranking has been abandoned, because it is inscribed in the account of moral progress itself (Marwah 2022). Valdez's 2022 essay reinforces this from the anthropological side: Kant's anthropological writings construct a racialized European character as the exemplar of cosmopolitan political virtue, so that the political agency required to instantiate cosmopolitanism is structurally reserved for those whose character has been shaped by European civilization — producing what Valdez calls a "narrow cosmopolitanism" whose formal universalism is undercut by its anthropological conditions of application (Valdez 2022).

What emerges from these structural critiques is a challenge that cuts across the developmental debate. Even if Kleingeld is right that Kant abandoned explicit racial ranking in the 1790s, the structural critics argue that the teleological and anthropological frameworks in which cosmopolitan right is embedded carry their own racial hierarchy — one that is not eliminated by revising the explicit statements in the race essays. The legal anti-colonialism is real; whether it is philosophically deep, or whether it sits atop a framework that systematically undercuts it, remains the open question that subsequent sections of this review must address.

## Section 3: Hegel's System and the Logic of Exclusion: World History, Slavery, and Haiti

Where the debate over Kant turns on whether racial commitments can be separated from an otherwise universalist critical philosophy, Hegel presents the problem in starker form: the exclusion of Africa and other non-European peoples from "world history proper" appears not as a peripheral remark but as something the dialectical system seems to require. Recent scholarship has sharpened this impression considerably, pressing the question of whether the racism in Hegel is contingent error or structural necessity. The master-slave dialectic, meanwhile, has become a second and partly independent test case — one that cuts across the entanglement debate by asking whether the most famous moment in the Phenomenology of Spirit secretly engages, or systematically erases, the violence of New World slavery.

### 3.1 Africa, the Orient, and the Structural-Necessity Thesis

The foundational statement of the structural-necessity view remains Bernasconi's (2000) argument that Hegel's Eurocentrism in the Lectures on the Philosophy of World History is not a contingent prejudice of the age but is grounded in a racial hierarchy that does indispensable philosophical work. Bernasconi argues that the relegation of Africa and its peoples to a pre-historical condition is not an empirical error correctable by better anthropological information; rather, it follows from the logical structure of Hegel's historical teleology, which requires a foil of sheer natural immersion against which Spirit's self-consciousness can begin to emerge. More recently, Bernasconi (2024) has deepened this argument through close analysis of the new critical edition of the Lectures in the Gesammelte Werke, showing that Africa's African element — and its role in the dialectical movement through Egypt to Greece — is textually confirmed by the newly established manuscripts, while also revealing that editors Gans and Karl Hegel shaped the received version of the text in ways that affected how Africa and Egypt were understood across the tradition. The scholarly debate is thus not only philosophical but also philological: what Hegel "said" about Africa depends partly on editorial decisions made decades after his death.

Stone (2020) extends the structural-necessity thesis from the Lectures into the Philosophy of Right, arguing that colonial expansion is legitimated in Hegel not as a contingent historical episode but as a normatively required moment in Spirit's self-realization: peoples deemed to lack adequate statehood and property relations can acquire freedom only by having European civilization imposed upon them, and this imposition is, for Hegel, therefore legitimate. Stone (2017) pursues the meta-level question of whether any teleological philosophy of history can avoid Eurocentric implications or whether they are built into the grammar of universal history as such. Together, these two papers establish the most philosophically systematic case that Hegel's Eurocentrism runs not merely through his empirical judgments but through his normative framework for understanding freedom and political development.

James and Knappik (2022) ground the structural-necessity thesis in Hegel's general metaphysics. On their reconstruction, Hegel's hierarchical racial taxonomy is not a brute empirical assertion but follows from his essentialist model of explanation: the Concept unfolds teleologically through stages that are also necessary articulations of the superordinate kind "humanity," and Hegel's racial divisions are presented as an application of this model to human diversity. Two attempts to "save" Hegel's philosophy from its racial commitments are examined and found unsuccessful. Harris and James (2026) situate this metaphysical argument within a broader survey of the debate, concluding that Hegel's framework links natural and cultural traits to normative and political status in ways that make his philosophy an instance of philosophical racism in a technically precise sense.

The structural case has been extended beyond the Lectures into other parts of the system. Stibor (2024) argues that Hegel's concept of freedom is not the racially neutral universal that commentators have assumed: because freedom is conceptualized as appropriation — the positing of will in external things — and because this model of appropriation underwrites the colonial seizure of land from peoples deemed incapable of proper ownership, Hegel's normative ideal of freedom is itself racialized. Even the emancipatory core of his practical philosophy, on Stibor's reading, cannot serve as an impartial critical criterion against his colonial remarks. Goggin (2024) pursues an analogous argument in the philosophy of religion, showing that the concept of "fetishism" through which Hegel categorizes non-European religious practices is racialized from the inception of the project, not a late or external addition. Hoffheimer (2001), drawing on passages across Hegel's mature works, traces the most extreme consequence: Hegel's explicit endorsement of the extermination of indigenous peoples in the Americas follows, on Hoffheimer's reading, from the logic of a philosophy of history that assigns no world-historical claims to peoples characterized as lacking Spirit.

The contingency interpretation — associated with readers such as McCarney and Buchwalter, who hold that Hegel's racial remarks are empirical prejudices external to the logical structure of the idealism — receives its sharpest challenge not only from the structural analyses surveyed above but also from Camara (2005), who approaches Hegel's Africa theses from the standpoint of the Africana scholarly tradition. Camara argues that Hegel's claims about Africa — that it has no history, no state, no religion in the proper sense — are simply false, based on ignorance of African societies, and represent a philosophical rationalization of ideological prejudice rather than an informed philosophical judgment. Where the structural-necessity arguments operate on Hegel's own immanent terms to show that the racism is philosophically necessary, Camara's empirical refutation attacks the premises themselves, arguing that no rehabilitation of the Hegelian framework is possible while retaining the Africa passages.

### 3.2 The Haiti Thesis and the Master-Slave Dialectic

The canonical readings of the lordship-and-bondage section of the Phenomenology of Spirit that dominated Hegel scholarship through the twentieth century treated the passage as a philosophical argument about the social constitution of self-consciousness: for Pinkard (1994), the section illustrates the inadequacy of non-mutual recognition and the need for genuinely reciprocal social structures; for Pippin (2010), the primary stakes are epistemological, concerning the conditions of self-knowledge through desire and the life-and-death struggle, with no essential reference to actual slavery. These non-racialized readings establish the baseline against which Buck-Morss's intervention registered as disruptive.

Buck-Morss (2000; 2009) argues that Hegel composed the lordship-and-bondage section while reading accounts of the Haitian Revolution in the journal Minerva, making actual New World chattel slavery — not a philosophical abstraction — the historical ground of the dialectic. On this historiographical thesis, the dialectic's claim to universality is not undermined but substantiated by its historical grounding: enslaved Haitians enacted the demand for freedom more concretely than any European revolutionary. The 2009 book extends this into a programmatic argument for rethinking "universal history" itself from the perspective of the colonized and enslaved, proposing a genuinely global intellectual history that takes seriously the contributions of non-European events to the formation of modern philosophical concepts.

The critical responses to Buck-Morss are themselves internally divided across three distinct registers that the domain literature recommends keeping analytically separate: the historiographical question of whether Hegel actually knew of Haiti, the textual-philosophical question of whether the Phenomenology's dialectic encodes or erases actual slavery, and the normative-political question of whether the dialectic can be put to emancipatory anti-colonial use. Bowman (2023) provides detailed archival evidence bearing on the historiographical register, drawing on transatlantic print culture, parliamentary records, and the documented exchanges between Hegel and Grégoire to argue that European literate publics were well aware of events in Saint-Domingue, while also acknowledging that recovering Haiti as the dialectic's historical inspiration does not dissolve Hegel's complicity with colonial racial ontologies.

Ciccariello-Maher (2014) operates primarily on the normative register, accepting the historical grounding but identifying what he characterizes as ironic silences in Buck-Morss's account: her centering of Toussaint L'Ouverture at the expense of Jean-Jacques Dessalines misses the anti-identitarian violence that was constitutive of the revolution's universality, and her neglect of Fanon means she cannot think emancipation in genuinely decolonized dialectical terms. Fischer (2010) raises a methodological concern from within Caribbean studies: the Buck-Morss thesis risks re-centering Hegel rather than Haiti, asking whether it serves the goal of centering Caribbean history or instead subordinates that history's significance to what it means for European philosophy. Heller (2020) advances the Marxist objection that the postcolonial framing imports idealist baggage without adequate replacement: the Haitian Revolution's universal significance is better grasped through historical-materialist analysis than through rehabilitation of a Hegel whose Eurocentrism and racism are, on Heller's account, fundamental rather than peripheral.

The most challenging complication for any emancipatory appropriation of the dialectic comes from Jaarte (2024), who argues on the textual-philosophical register that the lord-bondsman section cannot be easily separated from Hegel's pro-colonialism because Hegel himself applied it in a pro-colonial direction, arguing that colonial slavery temporarily educated its victims and was thereby temporarily justifiable. Jaarte further shows that this reading, largely suppressed in contemporary commentary, was recognized and embraced by the St. Louis Hegelians as a qualified defense of pre-Civil War American slavery — a reception history that complicates any claim that emancipatory appropriations of the dialectic are simply "truer" to Hegel's text than pro-colonial ones. James and Knappik (2025), in their systematic Cambridge Element study, corroborate Jaarte's finding while also examining how C.L.R. James and Angela Davis transformed Hegelian concepts for anti-colonial ends, preserving the tension between systemic complicity and appropriative possibility without resolving it.

Taken together, the Hegel debate has moved substantially toward the structural-necessity view on the question of world history and Africa, while the dialectic's emancipatory potential — the question of whether, and in what form, it remains available for anti-colonial thought — has been sharply narrowed by Jaarte's pro-colonial reading and complicated, rather than dissolved, by Buck-Morss's historiographical proposal. Whether the dialectic can ground a genuinely decolonized politics remains live primarily through the Fanon connection and through reconstructive projects that seek to use Hegel against Hegel's own system.

## Section 4: The View from Outside: Postcolonial, Decolonial, and Fanonian Critiques

The debates examined in the preceding sections take place primarily within the terms of Kantian and Hegelian scholarship. Section 4 surveys the challenge posed by traditions that approach German Idealism from outside its inherited frameworks — asking not whether the racism is separable from the system but whether the system's claim to universality is itself the problem.

### 4.1 Coloniality of Reason and the Racial Contract

The foundational document for the contemporary critique of Enlightenment reason is Eze's (1997) anthology, whose editorial argument establishes that race was not incidental to Enlightenment anthropology but integral to it: Kant, Hume, and Hegel together constructed a hierarchy of rational capacity that underwrites, rather than merely accompanies, their universalist claims. The argument developed from Eze's earlier essay-length intervention (Eze 1996) and set the terms for what followed. Mills (1997) extends the structural diagnosis to the social contract tradition as a whole, arguing that an unacknowledged "Racial Contract" distributes full personhood exclusively among whites, making the liberal tradition's universalism not an ideal imperfectly realized but a racially exclusive arrangement that functions ideologically. Mills's subsequent essay on Kant (Mills 2005) tightens the argument: Kant's categorical imperative was designed for a restricted community of persons, and the universalism operates to conceal this restriction. While Allais (2016) contests Mills on this point — locating Kant's racism primarily in his empirical anthropology rather than in the critical philosophy proper — the structural claim remains the live challenge to rehabilitative readings.

The decolonial school associated with Quijano, Mignolo, and Dussel deepens the diagnosis by embedding it in a theory of modernity itself. Quijano (2000) introduces the concept of the "coloniality of power," arguing that European colonialism produced a global racial classification of populations that persists beyond formal decolonization, and that Enlightenment philosophy — Hegel's philosophy of history above all — is the epistemological expression of this colonial power structure. Modernity and coloniality are two sides of the same process, not sequential stages. Mignolo (2011) draws the implication: the Enlightenment's rhetorics of salvation and progress constitute the "darker side" of colonial expansion, and the appropriate response is not internal reform but "epistemic delinking" — what he calls the "decolonial option" — constructing "border thinking" from the colonial difference rather than within the Western epistemic framework (Mignolo 2009). Dussel (2012) occupies a distinct position within this school: rather than calling for delinking, he proposes "transmodernity" as a reformed universalism built through intercultural dialogue with those excluded by Hegel's teleological world history. Liberation philosophy develops, on Dussel's account, from an "exterior" position that Hegelian dialectics cannot accommodate, yet it aims at a genuinely universal culture rather than plural incommensurabilities.

Wynter (2003) contributes a distinct argument that runs through these debates: the Western liberal humanist subject — descended from Kantian autonomous rationality — functions as an "overrepresentation of Man," claiming to represent the universal human while in fact encoding a specific European, masculine, and bourgeois form of life. Decolonization requires, on this view, not merely political liberation or epistemic reform but the construction of a new concept of the human beyond the Western Man figure. Allen (2016) brings this challenge directly into conversation with the Frankfurt School, arguing that critical theory's inheritance of Kantian-Hegelian progressivism is entangled with Eurocentrism and coloniality; what survives decolonization is the emancipatory aim, not the philosophy of historical progress that underwrites it. Bhambra (2014) helpfully maps the broader methodological landscape, distinguishing postcolonial theory's focus on representation and discourse from decolonial theory's focus on the structural matrix of power/knowledge — a distinction that clarifies why the immanent-critique strategy (using Enlightenment resources against Enlightenment failures) remains contested by those who argue the matrix itself must be abandoned.

### 4.2 Fanon and Hegel: Break or Radicalization?

The sharpest site for adjudicating between immanent critique and decolonial break is the encounter between Fanon and Hegel. Fanon appropriates three passages from the *Phenomenology of Spirit* in *Black Skin, White Masks* while arguing that colonial conditions of racialized dehumanization foreclose the recognition dialectic: the colonized subject is not recognized as a self-consciousness at all, so the dialectical movement toward mutual recognition cannot begin.

Recent scholarship divides on whether this constitutes a rejection or a radicalization. Badenhorst (2022) offers a careful textual reading via Kojève that identifies the precise mechanism of Fanon's divergence: the problem is not that Hegel assumes shared humanity between master and slave, but that he assumes reciprocal struggle — that freedom is won through a life-and-death confrontation whose cost and value are experienced by both parties. The colonial subject, Fanon argues, did not get to experience freedom through such a struggle, and the Kojève-Sartre-Beauvoir commitments running through *Black Skin, White Masks* illuminate this more precisely than ontological readings. This is a claim about the conditions under which the dialectic operates, not a wholesale rejection of dialectical thinking.

Bird-Pollan (2012) argues for the radicalization reading: Fanon inherits from Kant and Hegel the conception of agency as something achieved through struggle, not naturally given, and applies it to colonized subjects who are denied the conditions for such achievement. This makes Fanon's project the completion of German Idealism's universalist promise from a position the tradition excluded. Ng (2024) develops the most philosophically detailed version of this argument, distinguishing three senses of "universal humanity" in Fanon — false, abstract, and concrete — and arguing that Fanon's "concrete universal" provides a normative orientation for recognition that avoids both Hegel's abstraction and colonial essentialism. Faustino (2021) offers a third description: "creolization," in which Fanon takes the Hegelian dialectic and transforms it from the standpoint of the colonized, showing that colonialism "interdicts" the normal dialectical operation and that emancipation requires generating genuinely new theoretical forms rather than simply applying existing ones.

The reconstructive tradition extends beyond Fanon directly. Monahan (2006) argues for a "liberatory" reconstruction of recognition that draws on the *Philosophy of Right*'s non-agonistic account of mutual recognition as a resource against racism. Lima (2024) pursues an "immanent critique" strategy, finding in Hegel's own concept of Spirit and in the *Philosophy of Right*'s treatment of colonization a significant discrepancy with the Eurocentric teleology of the *Lectures on the Philosophy of History* — deploying Hegel against Hegel in the manner Adorno modeled. Brown (2024) argues that while the Buck-Morss thesis provides compelling historical grounding for the dialectic, it does not dissolve Fanon's philosophical critique: a concrete analysis of racialized experience must supplement what Hegel's phenomenology systematically omits. Basevich (2022) shifts the comparative frame to Du Bois, arguing that Kant's and Hegel's philosophies of history structured by racial hierarchy find their critical transformation in Du Bois's appropriation of Hegelian themes — the phenomenology of double consciousness as a model for an anti-racist philosophy of history built from the ruins of its racist precursors.

### 4.3 Comparative Enlightenment Cases: Herder, Forster, Fichte, Schiller

The decolonial and Fanonian critiques raise a question that comparative historical analysis presses with particular force: was complicity with colonial hierarchy a structural feature of the period's thought, or a choice among available options? The answer matters because a "spirit of the age" defense — that Kant and Hegel could not have done otherwise — is considerably weakened if contemporaries within the same intellectual milieu did otherwise.

Herder is the most frequently invoked counter-case. Muthu (2003) argues that Diderot, Herder, and Kant together constituted a significant anti-imperial strand of Enlightenment thought, grounding opposition to colonialism in the positive value of cultural plurality and human agency. Noyes (2015) extends this argument by focusing on Herder's anti-rationalist epistemology: by refusing to grant any culture a monopoly on truth, Herder established not merely a moral but an epistemological foundation for anti-imperialism that connects productively to postcolonial theory. Both readings, however, require qualification. Sikka (2011) argues that Herder is best understood as a "qualified relativist" who combined recognition of cultural difference with normative criteria for transcultural judgment; his rejection of racial hierarchy and Eurocentric teleology does not constitute a fully coherent anti-colonial philosophy, and the normative dimensions of his culture concept partially undercut his intentions. Niekerk (2025) presses this internal tension further, arguing that the normative dimensions of Herder's concept of culture risk reintroducing the hierarchies he intends to criticize. Kyslan (2024) provides a useful comparative summary: Herder's cultural relativism opened space that Kant's universalism foreclosed, but neither position escapes the period's ambiguities without residue.

The Forster-Kant controversy is arguably more damaging to any "spirit of the age" defense than Herder, because it shows anti-racist and abolitionist positions being articulated not at the margins of the period but in direct response to Kant's own texts. Gray (2012) reconstructs the 1786–1788 controversy as a metaphysical dispute over the status of skin color: Forster's empiricist challenge to Kant's teleological race taxonomy was not merely a different reading of the evidence but an incompatible framework for understanding nature and human diversity. Mensch (2025) widens the frame by bringing Kant's *Physical Geography* lectures into the picture, showing that the two thinkers shared more common ground as ethnographers than the formal debate suggests while their genuine divergences on racial hierarchy reflect deep commitments to incompatible methodologies. Most pointedly, Van Gorkom (2022) recovers the largely forgotten figure of Johann Gottlieb Stoll, who went further than Forster by explicitly condemning slavery and the slave trade on grounds of human dignity and by defending racial mixing as proof of species unity. Stoll's argument, developed within the same German Enlightenment conversation that produced Kant's racial taxonomy, demonstrates that abolitionist positions were available and articulated — making Kant's silence on these matters a choice with philosophical consequences rather than an uninstructed oversight.

Fichte and Schiller complete the comparative picture by showing how post-Kantian idealism generated its own forms of exclusion without biological determinism. Abizadeh (2005) argues that Fichte's *Addresses to the German Nation* harbors a "crypto-ethnic" nationalism: though not grounded in racial biology, the criterion of "original" versus "dead" languages effectively naturalizes national community and excludes non-German speakers from political belonging. Dwivedi (2022) identifies the underlying logic as "hypophysics" — a consecration of nature as value through which language, community, and race flow from a single spiritual-natural law — arguing that this structure constitutes a form of racism distinct from biologism yet enabling comparable exclusions. Schiller's *Letters on the Aesthetic Education of Man* operates differently: Leontiev (2023) shows that Schiller's diagnosis of "savage" and "barbarian" states as failures of aesthetic balance maps onto contemporary colonial categories, implicitly hierarchizing non-European peoples as aesthetically deficient while claiming universality for the aesthetic ideal. The irony is that Schiller's critique of Kantian ethics issues in a civilizational typology whose political implications are not easily distinguished from the colonial paternalism he might otherwise have rejected.

Taken together, the comparative cases demonstrate that the intellectual field of the period was internally contested. Herder opposed Eurocentric teleology; Forster challenged Kant's racial taxonomy; Stoll condemned slavery; and yet Kant maintained his hierarchy, Fichte's nationalism consolidated exclusion, and Schiller's aesthetics reproduced civilizational ranking. The range of positions available confirms that complicity was a choice — and makes structural-necessity defenses harder to sustain.

## Section 5: What Follows: Separability, Entanglement, and the Canon

### 5.1 The Separability/Entanglement Debate

The question that has run through every preceding section now becomes the explicit object of inquiry: can Kant's racism be methodologically isolated from the philosophy that constitutes his systematic contribution, or is it structurally woven into that philosophy's teleology, anthropology, and cultural historiography? The Kleingeld–Lu-Adler exchange, spanning from 2007 through the 2024–2025 debate in *Mind* and *Critical Philosophy of Race*, provides the live spine of this methodological controversy.

Kleingeld's position, articulated across a sequence of interventions, is that Kant's racial hierarchy is inconsistent with his moral universalism and that there is textual evidence of partial self-correction in the 1790s (Kleingeld 2007). In a critical notice of Lu-Adler's 2023 book, Kleingeld defends this inconsistent-egalitarian framework against the entanglement reading, contesting Lu-Adler's methodology and maintaining that the historical record supports revision rather than systematic integration (Kleingeld 2024). Kleingeld's methodological corollary, developed in an earlier treatment of sexism and racism together, is that acknowledging the inconsistency demands correction from within Kantian resources rather than either minimizing the racism as peripheral or rejecting the entire framework wholesale (Kleingeld 2019).

Lu-Adler's response takes the debate onto different terrain. Her 2023 monograph had argued that racism functioned as an ideological formation in Kant's work — expressed through his dual roles as natural philosopher of race and as educator who transmitted a racialized worldview — rather than as a contingent personal failing in logical tension with otherwise sound principles (Lu-Adler 2023). Her 2025 reply to Kleingeld sharpens this argument: the demands of antiracism require understanding how racism operated structurally, not merely identifying formal inconsistencies; and Kant's belated criticisms of some slavery practices do not amount to a principled rejection of colonial slavery as an institution (Lu-Adler 2025). The disagreement is thus not merely historical but normative: what would genuine engagement with Kant's racism actually require of contemporary scholars?

Several interventions resist reduction to either pole. Allais argues that neither the inconsistent-egalitarian nor the Millsian consistent-inegalitarian reading is fully satisfactory: Kant cannot be made internally consistent on race, but rather than treating this as a mere anomaly to be corrected or as evidence of a hidden coherence to be exposed, the more productive move is to use Kant's racism as philosophically instructive about the nature of racism itself. Kantian moral psychology, she contends, illuminates how racist attitudes can coexist with genuine moral commitments (Allais 2016). Ramsauer advances a more skeptical conclusion: even if Kant's racism is formally inconsistent with his universalism, the universalism is too thin to serve as any practical antidote. The categorical imperative's formal egalitarianism, on this view, provides insufficient resources for opposing racism regardless of whether the logical tension is real (Ramsauer 2023). Both interventions suggest that the separability/entanglement binary may not capture what is philosophically most important about the case.

Larrimore's earlier structural-antinomy reading offers a related but distinct diagnosis. Rather than simple inconsistency or systematic integration, Kant's treatment of race generates genuine internal tensions arising from within his own framework: between the teleological commitment to human unity and the fixity required by his biological race theory; between universal moral personhood and cultural-developmental hierarchy. These antinomies, Larrimore argues, are not external anomalies but symptoms of deeper structural pressures within Kant's teleological philosophy (Larrimore 2008). McNulty's recent argument reinforces the entanglement dimension of this diagnosis: the concept of "culture" — Kant's account of developed capacities for self-directed ends — serves as the systematic hinge connecting his teleology to his racial hierarchy, effectively excluding Indigenous Americans and Black people from the horizons of moral agency within his own framework (McNulty 2025). Govedarica and Smajević Roljić, surveying the terrain from a critical distance, find weaknesses in the shared premises of both Kleingeld and Bernasconi, and attend with particular care to the evidential weight that has been placed on the draft for *Perpetual Peace* — the text central to Kleingeld's developmental argument (Govedarica 2024).

### 5.2 Canon, Complicity, and Reconstruction

The methodological debate over separability does not terminate in abstract interpretive questions; it bears directly on the institutions and curricula through which philosophy reproduces itself. Park's study of the period 1780–1830 argues that the exclusion of African and Asian philosophy from the European philosophical canon was not a neutral scholarly development but was driven by racial Eurocentrism, with Kant and his follower Christoph Meiners instrumental in constructing a "whites only" philosophical canon that identified rational philosophy with European culture (Park 2013). Strickland and Wang extend this analysis across a longer arc, documenting a historical shift from inclusive to exclusively Eurocentric histories of philosophy after 1780 and attributing that shift to racist ideology rather than scholarly judgment (Strickland 2023). Smith situates Lu-Adler's account within this lineage, showing that her work adds a further dimension to Park's narrative by identifying Kant's own contribution to racially structured historiography of philosophy — racism was embedded not only in his followers but in his own scholarly practice — while raising methodological questions about the scope of the educator argument and the role of pre-Kantian historian Jacob Brucker (Smith 2025).

Bernasconi's 2024 essay widens the frame further. Where earlier work had focused on specific figures, Bernasconi argues that the discipline of academic philosophy has been structurally silent about its own complicity with chattel slavery: moral philosophy textbooks from the period of slavery were deeply conservative even by the standards of their time, and post-abolition histories of ethics have largely erased this record. The silence is not incidental but reflects how the discipline defines its subject matter and selects its canonical figures (Bernasconi 2024). Décarie-Daigneault reconstructs from Bernasconi's writings an implicit three-part methodology — archaeological uncovering of material conditions, dialectical interpretation through the critical lens of race, and pedagogical transformation of philosophical practice — arguing that what the entanglement reading ultimately demands is not rejection of the tradition but a fundamental reorientation in how it is taught and practiced (Décarie-Daigneault 2024).

The question of what follows in practice admits of several answers. Graness, approaching the problem from the perspective of African philosophy, argues that remedying canon exclusion requires more than adding African names to syllabi; the methodological assumptions through which the discipline defines philosophy and demarcates it from mythology require a project of "conceptual decolonisation" (Graness 2018). Basevich develops a reconstructive Kantianism that neither simply accepts nor simply rejects Kant's racial commitments, instead extracting Kantian resources — the ideal of civic equality, the model of public reason, a nonideal theory of justice — for theorizing racial justice reform under conditions of partial compliance (Basevich 2020). Huseyinzadegan, contributing to the 2025 symposium on Lu-Adler's book, notes that defining racism in relation to Kantian philosophy and determining the scope of what remains to be uncovered are themselves open methodological questions, and that the project of bringing race from the margins to the center of Kant scholarship — the project shared by Mills and Lu-Adler — has further to run (Huseyinzadegan 2025).

Taken together, the positions in this section array along a spectrum: from corrective separability (Kleingeld), which holds that internal inconsistency can be repaired using the tradition's own resources, through structural-antinomy readings (Larrimore, Ramsauer) that find the repair more difficult than separability theorists acknowledge, to constitutive entanglement (Lu-Adler, McNulty, Bernasconi), which demands not correction but a reckoning with how the discipline and its canon were formed. Between full rejection and uncritical continuation, the reconstructive and canon-reform options — Basevich's nonideal Kantianism, Décarie-Daigneault's pedagogical reorientation, Graness's conceptual decolonization — mark out a practical middle ground that the literature has only recently begun to develop in detail.

## Conclusion

The literature reviewed here converges, across every figure and sub-domain, on a single structural axis: the question of whether the racist and Eurocentric commitments of Kant, Hegel, and their contemporaries are contingent prejudices separable from the systematic philosophy or are woven into its teleology, anthropology, and concept of freedom. This is not a debate that has reached resolution. What has shifted in recent years is the weight of scholarly opinion. Lu-Adler's argument that Kant's racism operated as an ideological formation — present in his role as educator and in the structure of his natural-historical and anthropological projects, not merely as biographical embarrassment — moves the conversation from internal inconsistency to structural embedding (Lu-Adler 2023). James and Knappik's work on the metaphysics and political stakes of German Idealism's racial frameworks, and Marwah's account of how race functions within Kant's teleological conception of white "progress," reinforce that gravitational shift (Marwah 2022). Yet the rehabilitative and reconstructive traditions remain active and analytically serious: Kleingeld's inconsistent-egalitarian reading and its reform-oriented sequel continue to generate direct exchanges (Kleingeld 2024; Lu-Adler 2025), and Allais's use of Kantian moral psychology to understand rather than merely condemn Kant's racism represents a distinct third path that belongs to neither camp (Allais 2016).

Several tensions in the literature remain unresolved and will define the agenda for future research. On Kant, the developmental-versus-continuity dispute has not been closed. Whether Kant's late cosmopolitan texts mark a genuine revision of racial hierarchy — as Kleingeld argues — or whether, as Bernasconi and Lu-Adler contend, the antislavery gestures fall short of racial egalitarianism and coexist with the hierarchical framework, turns on contested interpretations of the same passages. On Hegel, the question of what the Haitian Revolution and Fanonian reconstruction mean for the dialectic is equally open: Buck-Morss's argument that the master-slave dialectic was historically conditioned by the Haitian rebellion suggests resources within Hegelianism for an emancipatory reading, but Fanon's own appropriation restructures the phenomenology from the standpoint of the colonized in ways that may repurpose rather than redeem the dialectic. Whether that distinction matters — whether a sufficiently transformed Hegelianism is still recognizably Hegelian — is itself a contested meta-philosophical question. At the methodological level, the confrontation between immanent critique (using Kantian or Hegelian resources against their own colonial uses) and decolonial delinking (Mignolo's argument that the colonial matrix is constitutive of Enlightenment epistemology and therefore cannot be corrected from within) has not been resolved (Mignolo 2009; Quijano 2000). Additionally, the connections between German Idealism's aesthetics and its racial anthropology, and the links between Schelling's philosophy of nature and the colonial-administrative discourses of the period, remain underexplored relative to the attention Kant and Hegel have received.

This project contributes to the literature in two respects. First, it brings together the analytic critical-philosophy-of-race tradition — represented by Mills, Allais, and Lu-Adler, whose arguments are developed with close attention to the texts and to philosophical argument-structure — and the continental decolonial tradition represented by Mignolo and Dussel, whose structural analysis of modernity/coloniality supplies a broader historical and political frame. These two bodies of scholarship rarely engage each other directly; reading Kant and Hegel through a single separability/entanglement lens makes that convergence explicit. Second, by foregrounding that complicity was contested within the period itself — Georg Forster's exchange with Kant over racial monogenism, Herder's resistance to the racial hierarchy — the project reframes the canon question. The issue is not whether present-day scholars apply anachronistic blame to eighteenth-century thinkers. It is whether the structural role that race played in the formation of the philosophical canon (Park 2013; Bernasconi 2024), in the teleological frameworks that organized Enlightenment philosophy of history, and in the exclusion of non-European peoples from the community of rational agents represents a problem that demands structural analysis rather than individual exculpation. The debates surveyed here make the case that it does.
