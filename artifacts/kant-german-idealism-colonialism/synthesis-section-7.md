## Conclusion

The literature reviewed here converges, across every figure and sub-domain, on a single structural axis: the question of whether the racist and Eurocentric commitments of Kant, Hegel, and their contemporaries are contingent prejudices separable from the systematic philosophy or are woven into its teleology, anthropology, and concept of freedom. This is not a debate that has reached resolution. What has shifted in recent years is the weight of scholarly opinion. Lu-Adler's argument that Kant's racism operated as an ideological formation — present in his role as educator and in the structure of his natural-historical and anthropological projects, not merely as biographical embarrassment — moves the conversation from internal inconsistency to structural embedding (Lu-Adler 2023). James and Knappik's work on the metaphysics and political stakes of German Idealism's racial frameworks, and Marwah's account of how race functions within Kant's teleological conception of white "progress," reinforce that gravitational shift (Marwah 2022). Yet the rehabilitative and reconstructive traditions remain active and analytically serious: Kleingeld's inconsistent-egalitarian reading and its reform-oriented sequel continue to generate direct exchanges (Kleingeld 2024; Lu-Adler 2025), and Allais's use of Kantian moral psychology to understand rather than merely condemn Kant's racism represents a distinct third path that belongs to neither camp (Allais 2016).

Several tensions in the literature remain unresolved and will define the agenda for future research. On Kant, the developmental-versus-continuity dispute has not been closed. Whether Kant's late cosmopolitan texts mark a genuine revision of racial hierarchy — as Kleingeld argues — or whether, as Bernasconi and Lu-Adler contend, the antislavery gestures fall short of racial egalitarianism and coexist with the hierarchical framework, turns on contested interpretations of the same passages. On Hegel, the question of what the Haitian Revolution and Fanonian reconstruction mean for the dialectic is equally open: Buck-Morss's argument that the master-slave dialectic was historically conditioned by the Haitian rebellion suggests resources within Hegelianism for an emancipatory reading, but Fanon's own appropriation restructures the phenomenology from the standpoint of the colonized in ways that may repurpose rather than redeem the dialectic. Whether that distinction matters — whether a sufficiently transformed Hegelianism is still recognizably Hegelian — is itself a contested meta-philosophical question. At the methodological level, the confrontation between immanent critique (using Kantian or Hegelian resources against their own colonial uses) and decolonial delinking (Mignolo's argument that the colonial matrix is constitutive of Enlightenment epistemology and therefore cannot be corrected from within) has not been resolved (Mignolo 2009; Quijano 2000). Additionally, the connections between German Idealism's aesthetics and its racial anthropology, and the links between Schelling's philosophy of nature and the colonial-administrative discourses of the period, remain underexplored relative to the attention Kant and Hegel have received.

This project contributes to the literature in two respects. First, it brings together the analytic critical-philosophy-of-race tradition — represented by Mills, Allais, and Lu-Adler, whose arguments are developed with close attention to the texts and to philosophical argument-structure — and the continental decolonial tradition represented by Mignolo and Dussel, whose structural analysis of modernity/coloniality supplies a broader historical and political frame. These two bodies of scholarship rarely engage each other directly; reading Kant and Hegel through a single separability/entanglement lens makes that convergence explicit. Second, by foregrounding that complicity was contested within the period itself — Georg Forster's exchange with Kant over racial monogenism, Herder's resistance to the racial hierarchy — the project reframes the canon question. The issue is not whether present-day scholars apply anachronistic blame to eighteenth-century thinkers. It is whether the structural role that race played in the formation of the philosophical canon (Park 2013; Bernasconi 2024), in the teleological frameworks that organized Enlightenment philosophy of history, and in the exclusion of non-European peoples from the community of rational agents represents a problem that demands structural analysis rather than individual exculpation. The debates surveyed here make the case that it does.
