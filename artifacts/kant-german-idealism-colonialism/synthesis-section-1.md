## Introduction

Few contrasts in the history of philosophy are as arresting as the one that organizes the scholarship surveyed in this review. The same thinkers who gave modern philosophy its most powerful vocabulary of autonomy, dignity, universal moral equality, and cosmopolitan right — Immanuel Kant above all, but also Hegel and their contemporaries — produced systematic theories of racial hierarchy and Eurocentric world history. Kant ranked the four human races in ascending order of rational capacity, placing white Europeans at the summit and Africans at the base; Hegel excluded Africa from "world history" altogether and theorized non-European peoples as standing outside the development of freedom. These are not marginal remarks buried in occasional writings. As Bernasconi (2002) argues, Kant should be recognized as a principal constructor of the modern philosophical concept of race, giving it the systematic grounding it had previously lacked. Eze (1996) demonstrates that race is constitutive of Kant's anthropology and empirical geography, not an embarrassing appendix to it. And James and Knappik (2024) observe that, for Hegel as well, the entanglement between progressive ideals and colonial ideology has been underexplored relative to its depth and systematic reach.

The question that runs through every strand of this literature is whether these two faces of German Idealism — its emancipatory universalism and its racial hierarchism — stand in tension or in continuity. The tension reading holds that Kant's and Hegel's racism represents an inconsistency: philosophers committed to universal rational dignity failed, historically and contingently, to apply their own principles consistently to all human beings. On this view, the philosophical frameworks can be retrieved and corrected. The continuity reading holds that the racial hierarchies are not accidental prejudices but are structurally woven into the very architectures of these philosophies — into Kant's teleological natural history, his philosophy of progress, and his cosmopolitan right; into Hegel's logic of world-historical development and his dialectic of recognition. On this view, retrieval without fundamental reconstruction is not available. Mills (1997) offers one of the sharpest versions of the continuity claim: the social contract tradition, including Kant's contractarian ethics, rests on an unacknowledged "Racial Contract" that defines who counts as a full rational person, rendering the universalism structurally partial. Kleingeld (2007), by contrast, argues that Kant underwent a genuine revision of his racial views in the 1790s, moving toward the moral egalitarianism his own universalism demanded — a "recantation thesis" that has anchored the rehabilitative tradition and generated the field's central ongoing debate.

Why does this question matter beyond the historical record? The answer bears on several concerns simultaneously. First, it determines whether Kantian and Hegelian frameworks can be deployed against colonialism today, or whether doing so requires working against the grain of the very conceptual architectures one is invoking. Second, it raises questions about how philosophy reads its own canon: if racial exclusion was not incidental but constitutive, then the canon itself was formed in ways that demand methodological reckoning. Third, it bears on the relationship between a philosopher's empirical prejudices and their systematic thought — a question with ramifications extending well beyond this historical case. The debate, as Lu-Adler (2023) shows in her recent treatment of Kant, is not merely about what Kant said but about what kind of philosophical work racism can do within a systematic philosophy committed to rational universalism.

This review maps the scholarly terrain across five sections. Section 1 establishes the textual and philological foundation: what Kant's race theory actually says, how it was constructed, and how it relates to his broader critical philosophy. Section 2 examines the central rehabilitative claim — Kant as anti-colonialist — and its critics, tracing the developmental-versus-continuity dispute as it plays out in the domain of cosmopolitanism and legal right. Section 3 carries the entanglement question to Hegel, where it appears with particular sharpness because the exclusions seem logically required by the system rather than merely historically contingent. Section 4 presents the external challenge: postcolonial and decolonial critiques that question whether Enlightenment reason is structurally colonial, alongside the Fanon-Hegel interface that concentrates the choice between immanent appropriation and decolonial break. Section 5 makes the organizing question explicit as a meta-philosophical problem, examining the separability/entanglement debate and its implications for how the discipline understands its own canon and its contemporary responsibilities.
