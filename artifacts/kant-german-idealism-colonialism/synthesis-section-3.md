## Section 2: Kant's Universalism on Trial — Cosmopolitanism, Anti-Colonialism, and the Developmental Debate

### 2.1 The Recantation Thesis and Its Rivals

The most contested interpretive question in recent Kant scholarship is whether the mature political writings represent a genuine break with the racial hierarchy of the 1780s. Pauline Kleingeld's developmental thesis, first advanced in a 2007 article and elaborated in her 2011 monograph, holds that the answer is yes. Kleingeld argues that Kant's 1780s texts — the race essays, the Anthropology lectures — defended white superiority as part of a natural-historical framework, but that by the time of *Toward Perpetual Peace* (1795) and the *Metaphysics of Morals* (1797), Kant had restricted the concept of race to its proper natural-scientific domain and abandoned the normative hierarchy of racial capacities. This revision, on Kleingeld's account, was philosophically motivated: the internal demands of Kant's developing cosmopolitan ethics required a genuinely egalitarian account of peoples (Kleingeld 2007; 2011). In a further essay focused specifically on colonialism, she argues that Kant's earlier endorsement of European expansion was underwritten by the racial hierarchy, and that abandoning the hierarchy in the 1790s entailed abandoning that justification — replacing it with an egalitarian cosmopolitan right that condemns colonial appropriation (Kleingeld 2014).

Against this thesis, Robert Bernasconi has reconstructed what he calls Kant's "third thoughts" on race. He identifies the 1788 essay "On the Use of Teleological Principles in Philosophy" — Kant's response to Georg Forster's challenge — not as a softening of racial theory but as its philosophical consolidation: here Kant grounds racial taxonomy more firmly in teleological natural history, elaborating rather than retracting the hierarchical framework. On Bernasconi's reading, this elaboration represents the continuity of Kant's race theory even at its most sophisticated (Bernasconi 2011). The implication is that the developmental thesis misreads the 1790s by ignoring the theoretical deepening that immediately preceded it.

Huaping Lu-Adler has developed the most sustained recent version of the continuity case. Her 2022 article examines Kant's treatment of colonial slavery and argues that his belated criticisms of certain slavery practices are framed in the language of trade rights and contractual violations rather than in the language of racial equality. Kant condemned specific configurations of slavery without dismantling the racial hierarchy that structured them; the shift in his practical conclusions does not license the inference to a shift in his anthropological framework (Lu-Adler 2022). In her 2023 monograph and in her response to Kleingeld's critical notice, Lu-Adler sharpens this into a methodological point: reading Kant's racism as an ideological formation — one that shaped and legitimated racial domination through his role as educator and natural philosopher — shows that the question cannot be resolved by examining late textual revisions alone, since ideology operates at a structural level not fully visible in explicit retraction (Lu-Adler 2023; 2025). Lu-Adler argues that the demands of antiracism require a more thorough critique of Kant's philosophy than Kleingeld's developmental reading permits.

Charles Mills contributes a different angle of attack. In his 2014 revisitation of the debate, Mills maintains that Kant's anthropological writings imply a hierarchical account of personhood under which Africans and others fall below the threshold of full moral agency demanded by the categorical imperative — a "sub-personhood" thesis. Kleingeld's developmental account, Mills argues, underestimates how philosophically deep the contamination runs: revising explicit racial ranking leaves intact the structure of graded personhood that gave that ranking its normative force (Mills 2014).

Inés Valdez offers yet a different dissent, not by defending the continuity of Kant's racism but by challenging the framework through which Kleingeld connects racial revision to anti-colonialism. On Valdez's account, Kant's condemnation of colonial wars in *Toward Perpetual Peace* is grounded in his theory of just war and republican right: wars for commercial expansion or territorial acquisition violate republican principles because they are unauthorized by citizens and undermine the conditions for perpetual peace. Race is not the pivot on which Kant's anti-colonialism turns; accordingly, the anti-colonialism *co-exists* with the racial hierarchy rather than depending on its revision (Valdez 2017). This reading has the important implication that Kant's legal anti-colonialism may be more robust than the developmental thesis implies — but precisely because it was never tied to racial egalitarianism in the first place.

### 2.2 The Legal Architecture vs. the Structural Critique

The legal dimension of Kant's anti-colonialism has received careful treatment in its own right. Arthur Ripstein analyses Kant's condemnation of colonial appropriation through the framework of the *Doctrine of Right*, arguing that colonialism violates the a priori conditions of rightful external relations: it forcibly incorporates peoples into a legal order without their consent, thereby undermining public right at its foundation. The condemnation is not a moral sentiment or an empirical policy judgment but a conclusion derived from the juridical structure of cosmopolitan law (Ripstein 2014). Peter Niesen reaches a convergent conclusion through a close reading of the hospitality right in *Toward Perpetual Peace*: Kant's distinction between a right to visit and a right to settle entails the explicit denial of any settler entitlement to appropriate land already occupied by indigenous peoples, constituting a normative prohibition on colonial dispossession (Niesen 2007). Christopher Meckstroth complicates this picture by reading the hospitality right not as an affirmation of cosmopolitan universalism but as a critique of the pretension of any universal order to impose itself on different political communities; on his account, hospitality sets limits on what one community may demand of another, thereby also foreclosing the civilizing-mission argument as a justification for colonial intervention (Meckstroth 2017). Together, these legal-architectonic readings suggest that Kant's anti-colonialism has genuine juridical teeth, grounded in the theory of right rather than contingent on historical revision. Sankar Muthu situates Kant within a broader Enlightenment anti-imperialist tradition, though noting that Kant's universalism differs from Herder and Diderot's cultural pluralism by grounding opposition to empire in the moral status of rational agents rather than in the value of cultural diversity (Muthu 2003).

The structural critics accept that this juridical framework is real but argue that it does not resolve the problem, because race is internal to the philosophical architecture that generates it. Todd Hedrick argues that Kant's cosmopolitan project of cultivating human talents toward rational self-governance presupposes the anthropological framework in which non-European peoples are positioned as less developed in their capacity for such cultivation — racial difference thus enters not as an incidental remark but as a structural presupposition of the normative architecture itself (Hedrick 2008). Jasmine Gani extends this argument to the hospitality right specifically: the universalism of Kant's hospitality is achieved by abstracting from the racial differences that his anthropological writings posit, producing a formal equality that conceals rather than dismantles the substantive racial hierarchy beneath it (Gani 2017). Inder Marwah focuses on Kant's teleological philosophy of history, arguing that the teleological framework assigns different races different positions in the developmental scheme oriented toward rational self-governance; this teleological racism persists structurally even after explicit racial ranking has been abandoned, because it is inscribed in the account of moral progress itself (Marwah 2022). Valdez's 2022 essay reinforces this from the anthropological side: Kant's anthropological writings construct a racialized European character as the exemplar of cosmopolitan political virtue, so that the political agency required to instantiate cosmopolitanism is structurally reserved for those whose character has been shaped by European civilization — producing what Valdez calls a "narrow cosmopolitanism" whose formal universalism is undercut by its anthropological conditions of application (Valdez 2022).

What emerges from these structural critiques is a challenge that cuts across the developmental debate. Even if Kleingeld is right that Kant abandoned explicit racial ranking in the 1790s, the structural critics argue that the teleological and anthropological frameworks in which cosmopolitan right is embedded carry their own racial hierarchy — one that is not eliminated by revising the explicit statements in the race essays. The legal anti-colonialism is real; whether it is philosophically deep, or whether it sits atop a framework that systematically undercuts it, remains the open question that subsequent sections of this review must address.
