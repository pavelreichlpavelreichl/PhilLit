## Section 1: Introduction: The Puppet, the Dwarf, and the Stakes of Benjamin's Philosophy of History

Walter Benjamin opens "On the Concept of History" (1940) with an image whose strangeness has never fully yielded to interpretation: a chess-playing automaton, draped in Turkish costume, that wins every game by concealing a hunchbacked dwarf — a master of chess — in its hollow interior. Historical materialism, Benjamin proposes, can win every engagement if it enlists theology as its hidden operator. The figure sets the terms for everything that follows in the eighteen theses (and their appendices): it announces a relationship between two discourses — Marxist historical analysis and Jewish messianic theology — that is neither straightforward alliance nor mere ornamentation, but something more unsettling. As Simpson (2021), reconstructing Peter Gordon's interpretation, puts the point precisely, Benjamin occupies a position of "ambivalent secularization," poised between Marxism and messianism in a way that resists resolution into either. The question the puppet-and-dwarf image poses — is theology master of materialism, its servant, or so deeply entangled with it that the distinction collapses — is the interpretive question that has organized Benjamin scholarship for the better part of four decades.

The textual core of that scholarship is "On the Concept of History" itself, composed in the months before Benjamin's death at the Spanish border in September 1940, under the shadow of both fascism and the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact. But the Theses cannot be read in isolation from two other bodies of work. Konvolut N of the *Arcades Project* — Benjamin's massive, unfinished investigation of nineteenth-century Parisian modernity — provides the methodological elaboration of the dialectical image and the theory of historical knowledge that the Theses compress into aphorism. And the experience essays, above all "On Some Motifs in Baudelaire" and "The Storyteller," furnish the phenomenological diagnosis of modernity — the decline of transmissible experience (*Erfahrung*) into isolated shock (*Erlebnis*) — that underpins the Theses' account of what has gone wrong with both historiography and political consciousness.

Tiedemann (1983), Benjamin's editor and among the most rigorous philological readers of the Theses, argues that the text is irreducibly torn between historical materialism and political messianism: it does not resolve this tension but performs it, thesis by thesis, through sources — Marx, Blanqui, Brecht, Kraus — that pull in opposing directions. This claim has proved generative. Three interpretive camps have formed around it. Theological or messianic readings (Scholem, Jacobson, Mosès) hold that the theological commitments are primary, constitutive of Benjamin's critique rather than instrumental to it. Materialist or Marxist readings (Tiedemann himself, Löwy, Khatib in one register, List) insist that historical-materialist analysis provides the critical substance, with theology supplying motivational energy rather than doctrinal content. Löwy (2005) characterizes the synthesis as a "revolutionary-romantic" critique of progress ideology that combines Jewish messianism and libertarian Marxism without reducing either to the other. Hybrid or tension readings — represented variously by Wohlfarth (1978), Khatib (2013), and Marramao (2008) — treat the irresolvability of the theology-materialism relation not as a flaw to be corrected but as the productive paradox constitutive of Benjamin's thought: the "messianic without messianism," a structural rather than doctrinal theological index within materialist practice.

Beiner (1984) offers a useful early orientation, reading the Theses as a politics of memory directed against the socialist movement's accommodation to progress ideology — an anti-fascist argument whose messianism is political in function rather than devotional in content. This framing establishes that the stakes of the theology-materialism debate are not merely exegetical: they determine how Benjamin's historiography bears on questions of political action, the redemption of defeated struggles, and the possibility of interrupting catastrophe.

The review that follows tracks the central tension through four domains: the structure of historical time (Jetztzeit, the Angel of History, the critique of progress); historiographic method (the dialectical image and its critics); experience and remembrance (*Eingedenken*, *Rettung*, the decline of *Erfahrung*); and the critical reception that most forcefully contests whether Benjamin's synthesis is coherent or politically defensible. The objections of Adorno, Habermas, Wolin, Brown, and Clark are not peripheral correctives but integral nodes of the debate, and they receive proportional treatment throughout.

## Section 2: The Structure of Historical Time: Jetztzeit, Messianic Time, and the Critique of Progress

### 2.1 Now-Time Against Homogeneous Empty Time

At the center of Benjamin's philosophy of history stands a polemic against a specific conception of time: the "homogeneous, empty time" of historicism, in which events are arranged in a uniform continuum available for narration and accumulation. Against this, Benjamin posits Jetztzeit — now-time — as a qualitative rupture that arrests the flow of chronological sequence and charges the present moment with historical recognition. The structural opposition is stated most directly in Thesis XIII, where social-democratic faith in progress is diagnosed as parasitic on exactly the temporality Benjamin rejects: a time that fills evenly, accumulates its contents, and generates moral confidence in the direction of history.

What Jetztzeit positively *is*, however, is among the most contested questions in the literature. Three genealogies are regularly advanced, and their differences have substantive implications for how the concept functions. Fenves (2010) traces the concept's philosophical formation through phenomenology and Kantian practical philosophy, arguing that what Benjamin calls the "messianic reduction" is structurally analogous to the phenomenological reduction: it strips away the conventional temporal horizon to disclose the structure of the now as such, one that owes its form to the Husserlian analysis of time-consciousness and to Kant's account of practical reason. On this reading, Jetztzeit is not primarily political or theological but philosophical — a finding about the shape of time that cannot be collapsed into either revolutionary programme or theological narrative.

Torbidoni (2021) reinforces the Kantian genealogy from a different angle, reading Jetztzeit as Benjamin's response to the Kantian devaluation of experience (Erfahrung): now-time ruptures homogeneous empty time not only to make the past politically legible but to rescue the ephemeral moment as a metaphysically decisive instance. The concept connects the late temporal theory of the Theses back to Benjamin's early epistemological preoccupations, situating the opposition to "homogeneous empty time" within a critique of Kantian formalism that began decades before the Theses were written.

A third and contrasting lineage foregrounds the directly political-revolutionary register. Inshakov (2023) argues, through an analytical reconstruction of Jetztzeit's conceptual components, that depending on which genealogy one accepts — phenomenological, messianic, or political — the concept yields diametrically opposed politics. Read through kairos and the tradition of critical theology, Benjamin appears as a theorist of pure critique; read through divine violence and the tradition of Marxist revolutionary rupture, he appears as an advocate of direct political action. This divergence is not merely exegetical: it determines whether now-time authorizes transformative intervention or functions as an interruption without political programme. Inshakov situates Benjamin against the "mainstream" Frankfurt School precisely because the political stake of the concept remains alive in a way Adorno's later reception sought to foreclose.

The structural articulation of Jetztzeit in Thesis XVII — where each now-time is described as a monad that "contains within it the compressed history of all humanity" — adds a further philosophical dimension. Desideri (2016) argues that the monadological structure of now-time entails a corresponding spatial logic: the moment is not a point on a line but an intensive totality that "integrates" history into space. The figure of *intermittence* — the differential of time and integral of space — captures the dialectical intertwining of continuity and discontinuity in Benjamin's temporal theory. On this account, the real philosophical foil for the late Benjamin is not Heidegger but Hegel, and the monadological moment is Benjamin's response to Hegelian dialectical totality: not sublation but standstill (Stillstand). Wohlfarth (1978) had already identified the Stillstand — the dialectical standstill — as the structural form of Jetztzeit and the productive paradox around which the Theses are organized: the moment of arrest in which past and present become simultaneously legible. Wohlfarth's reading treats this not as a defect of Benjamin's thought but as its governing logic — a refusal of both triumphalist redemption and mechanical materialism that keeps the tension between them operative.

The tiger's leap (Tigersprung) of Thesis XIV introduces a further dimension: how Jetztzeit authorizes action. Andrew Benjamin (2003), in a close reading of the Romans-as-fashion figure, argues that the revolutionary citation of the past is not imitation but temporal performance — the past is not reproduced but made available through a formal logic of repetition that is inherently political. The Tigersprung thus models how now-time functions practically: not as nostalgia but as the seizure of a past constellation that is "charged with the time of the now." Osborne (1995) situates this temporal politics within the broader crisis of modern temporality, arguing that Benjamin's concepts of now-time and the dialectical image constitute a politics of time that refuses both Enlightenment teleology and the mere contemporaneity of postmodernism, positioning Benjamin's temporal theory as a response to the aporia of modernity's relationship to its own past.

### 2.2 Weak Messianic Power and the Tradition of the Oppressed

The opening theses of "On the Concept of History" introduce a further concept whose ambiguity is constitutive: the "weak messianic power" (schwache messianische Kraft) that each generation is said to have been granted. Thesis II, the source of this formulation, situates the concept between happiness (Glück) and the claim of the past — we possess a weak capacity to redeem the suffering of prior generations, not through eschatological fulfillment but through the "revolution of recognition" that history makes possible in the charged now.

Khatib (2013) argues that this concept should be read neither as theological content nor as secularized messianic residue, but as a structural feature of Benjamin's materialist philosophy of history. The messianic names, in Khatib's analysis, an irreducible non-identity within historical time: a negativity that cannot be appropriated by either secular or religious tradition, and that keeps open the possibility of rupture without positing any transcendent guarantor. The "messianic without messianism" is a form of immanent critique, distinguishing Benjamin's position from both Agamben's Pauline extension and Derrida's "messianicity without a messiah." The force of the concept lies precisely in its structural operation: it is what prevents historical time from closing upon itself.

Deuber-Mankowsky (2008) illuminates the philosophical ancestry of this position by reading it alongside Hermann Cohen's neo-Kantian messianism and Paul's theology of weakness. Against Agamben's claim that Benjamin's hidden dwarf was Paul, Deuber-Mankowsky marshals substantial evidence for Cohen's priority: the image of happiness in Thesis II belongs to a philosophical logic of the "messianic power of weakness" that operates through rational obligation rather than eschatological force. The weak messianic power is weak not because it is diminished but because it functions through the structure of citation, constellation, and the claim of suffering on present action — precisely the logic Cohen had articulated through his synthesis of Maimonides and Kant. Desideri (2015) extends this argument, contrasting it with Taubes's Gnostic reading of Benjamin's theology and insisting that Benjamin's messianism shares with Cohen a fundamentally rational and non-eschatological orientation. The difference between them, Desideri argues, lies in Benjamin's monadological-ontological interpretation of the "infinite task," in contrast to Cohen's ethical formulation.

Khatib (2017) pursues the materialist epistemology underlying the "tradition of the oppressed." Benjamin's injunction to write history from the standpoint of the vanquished (Thesis VIII, and the figure of "brushing against the grain" in Thesis VII) is not, on Khatib's account, a merely ethical commitment, but rests on a specific claim about historical cognition: the past retains an unfulfilled claim on the present, and historical materialism is the practice of responding to that claim by "blasting" specific constellations out of the continuum. The negative corollary — Ranke's "how it really was" (wie es eigentlich gewesen) and "empathy with the victor" — forecloses the constellation by anchoring historical cognition within the perspective of sovereignty. Denysenko (2021) maps how these strands converge: the weak messianic power, Jetztzeit, and Eingedenken (collective memory) form an interlocking complex in which the urgency of the "now" and the obligation to the dead mutually reinforce each other, contrasting throughout with the linear progressive temporality the Theses target.

### 2.3 The Angel of History and the Critique of Progress

Benjamin's most concentrated statement of the critique of progress ideology is Thesis IX, the Angel of History. The angel, facing the past, sees not a chain of events but "one single catastrophe, which keeps piling wreckage upon wreckage"; the storm from paradise — what we call progress — drives it irresistibly toward the future with its back turned. The thesis condenses Benjamin's polemic against two overlapping adversaries: Rankean historicism's claim to reconstruct the past "as it really was," and social-democratic Marxism's faith in the development of productive forces as the guarantor of historical advance.

The philosophical precision of this polemic is brought out by Pensky (2010), who argues that the Angel thesis is best read as a direct response to Kant's philosophy of historical progress rather than solely to Ranke or reformist Marxism. Where Kant grounded the idea of historical progress in regulative ideas and "signs of mankind's moral advance," Benjamin substitutes the allegorical figure of the angel to show that what presents itself as progress from within the continuum is experienced as accumulating catastrophe by those who do not occupy the victor's perspective. The "storm from paradise" is Benjamin's inversion of Kantian teleology: the driving force of history is not the realization of reason but the compounding of disaster. Vaki (2024) synthesizes this argument for a handbook audience, emphasizing how the rejection of both historicist narrative continuity and social-democratic teleological determinism calls for a revolutionary historiography centered on Jetztzeit and the redemption of suppressed historical moments — the standpoint the angel cannot occupy because it is not a historical materialist.

The Angel is also, simultaneously, an allegorical and aesthetic construction drawn from Paul Klee's 1920 monoprint, and this dimension has its own substantial literature. Hope (2019) offers the most detailed rhetorical analysis of Thesis IX as a text, arguing that the proliferation of competing interpretations is not a scholarly failure but is structurally produced by the interplay between grammar and rhetoric in the thesis itself. The sublime metaphor of the angel generates an imperative to "translate" — to interpret and re-interpret — that no single reading can exhaust; the text thereby performs the anti-historicist argument it advances, resisting the totalizing closure that historicist reading demands. Noble-Olson (2020) extends the Adornian dimension: reading the Angelus Novus through Adorno's theory of art's contradictory sociality, he argues that the angel figures the art object's enforced distance from the suffering it cannot alleviate — a bridge between Benjamin's philosophy of history and Frankfurt School aesthetics.

Fiedler (2021) grounds the catastrophe thesis materially in the Arcades Project, arguing that both Thesis IX and the Arcades share a conception of progress as a permanent "state of emergency" rather than an exceptional deviation. Benjamin's reading of Haussmann's modernization of Paris provides the material evidence: catastrophe is the rule, not the exception, and the "Copernican revolution" in historical method that Benjamin proposes locates disaster in the past and present rather than anticipating it for the future. This grounds the allegorical figure in historical materialism rather than in political theology — a positioning that stands in tension with Hong (2026), who develops the concept of "incapacitated transcendence" to situate the Angel against Carl Schmitt's theory of sovereignty. For Hong, Klee's malformed or flightless angels present a divine presence structurally incapable of sovereign intervention, subverting the logic of the secularized miracle that Schmitt's political theology requires. Benjamin's Angel, on this reading, is the culmination of a theological counter-tradition that refuses the decisionism of sovereign power, rendering the site of the angel's impotence the very location of a hope that does not reduplicate power.

Wu (2020) draws out the intellectual-historical genealogy by tracing an unbroken critical line from Nietzsche's "On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life" to the Theses. Both Nietzsche and Benjamin diagnose historicism as the "sickness of time" of modernity, but their remedies diverge sharply: where Nietzsche prescribes vitalist forgetting, Benjamin responds with messianic interruption. This comparison illuminates what is distinctive in Benjamin's anti-historicism — it is not the forgetting of the past but its redemptive recuperation that the historical materialist pursues, and the critique of progress is not nostalgia but the precondition for recognizing the wreckage that progress ideology conceals.

Löwy (2005) connects these strands through the figure of the "emergency brake": Benjamin was, in Löwy's formulation, the first Marxist to break radically with the ideology of progress, and the Angel thesis crystallizes this break by positioning historical catastrophe as the rule rather than the exception. That Frelek (2025) can extend this argument into a reading of Adorno on climate catastrophe demonstrates the ongoing vitality of the catastrophe thesis and the continued relevance of the critical line Benjamin inaugurated.

---

**Section summary.** Benjamin's temporal architecture — Jetztzeit, Stillstand, Tigersprung, the weak messianic power, the "tradition of the oppressed," and the Angel of History — constitutes an interlocking polemic against the homogeneous empty time of historicism and social-democratic progressivism. The literature, however, divides on the character of these concepts: whether their force is phenomenological and Kantian (Fenves, Torbidoni), political-revolutionary (Inshakov, Osborne, Khatib), theological (Deuber-Mankowsky, Desideri, Hong), or materialist-aesthetic (Fiedler, Noble-Olson, Löwy). As Wohlfarth's foundational reading already insisted, and as Khatib's structural analysis reaffirms, this irresolvability is not incidental to the concepts but constitutive of their critical force. The unresolved question — whether Jetztzeit licenses revolutionary action or remains a mode of pure critique — will reappear in the debates over historiographic method and political reception that occupy the sections that follow.

## Section 3: Historiographic Method: The Dialectical Image and Its Discontents

### 3.1 What the Dialectical Image Is

Benjamin's most distinctive methodological contribution to philosophy of history is the concept of the *dialektisches Bild* — the dialectical image — developed most systematically in Konvolut N of the *Arcades Project* and refined through correspondence with Adorno in the 1930s. The concept names a figure in which a past moment and the present "flash together" in a sudden constellation of mutual legibility: what Benjamin calls the *Jetzt der Erkennbarkeit*, the now of recognizability. Rather than narrating the past from the vantage of an advancing present — as in Rankean historicism's ambition to show "how it really was" — the historian constructs the moment at which historical time crystallizes into a readable image. This procedure is explicitly opposed to Hegelian dialectics: where Hegel's *Aufhebung* preserves and elevates contradiction into a higher synthesis, Benjamin's dialectics comes to rest in an image rather than pressing forward. The phrase "dialectics at a standstill" (*Dialektik im Stillstand*) condenses the anti-teleological logic: the image arrests historical movement without resolving it.

Three interpretive lines compete in the secondary literature on what exactly the dialectical image does and what kind of object it is. Buck-Morss (1989) defends it as a genuinely materialist visual epistemology. Her reconstruction of the *Arcades Project* as a coherent philosophical whole argues that commodity culture generates collective dream-images — phantasmagorias — whose historical and social constitution is ordinarily invisible; the dialectical image disrupts this opacity by making visible, in a flash, the social logic encoded in the commodity's surface. On this reading, the "dialectics of seeing" is optical before it is conceptual, and the image's disruptive force is tied directly to the material culture it takes as its object. Friedlander (2008), by contrast, defends the dialectical image as a distinctive mode of philosophical objectivity that cannot be reduced either to Hegelian sublation or to empiricist representation. Drawing on Benjamin's neo-Kantian and Goethean inheritances, Friedlander argues that the image "measures" the contingent by presenting historical material in the form of an archetype — not a representation of reality but a standard for judging its significance. The past emerges in authentic historical remembrance initially in a distorted form analogous to dream, and recognition of the image traverses that space of semblance toward truth. Pensky (2004) offers a third reading, arguing that the dialectical image is primarily a *temporal* rather than a visual or spatial category: it names the collision of different historical temporalities in a moment of recognizability, and its methodological roots lie in Benjamin's account of allegory in the *Trauerspiel* book, where time functions as both the medium and the object of historical cognition. Ross (2020) reinforces this point by showing that the constellational method — holding concepts in tension rather than resolving them — is present from Benjamin's earliest philosophical writings, giving the *Arcades* methodology a deeper continuity than accounts emphasizing the post-1924 Marxist turn typically acknowledge.

What unites these readings, despite their differences, is the insistence that the dialectical image is neither a metaphor for historical understanding nor a subjective impression but a claim about the structure of historical time itself: specifically, the claim that legibility — the moment at which the past becomes readable — is not gradual but explosive, a flash (*Aufblitzen*) that is also a political demand.

### 3.2 The Adorno–Benjamin Dispute

The most consequential objection to the dialectical image came from within the Frankfurt School's own correspondence. Adorno's letters to Benjamin throughout the 1930s — collected in the *Complete Correspondence* (Adorno and Benjamin 1999) — register a sustained methodological anxiety: that Benjamin's images stand "at the crossroads of magic and positivism," treating historical phenomena as brute, self-presenting facts rather than mediating them through immanent conceptual critique. Adorno's central worry, applied initially to the 1935 *Arcades* exposé and renewed with greater force in response to "The Paris of the Second Empire in Baudelaire," is that Benjamin's procedure collapses the distinction between the category of the image and the empirical content it is supposed to illuminate. To present the arcades, the fashion commodity, or the prostitute as dialectical images without first constructing their mediation through the totality of social relations is, for Adorno, to risk a fetishism of the concrete that mirrors the very ideology the method claims to expose.

Feldman (2011) provides the philosophically most careful unpacking of this dispute. She demonstrates that Adorno's accusation of being "not dialectical enough" is not a single charge but a structurally *chiastic* one: when applied to Benjamin's artwork essay, it targets a perceived surrender of aesthetic autonomy to social-ideological narration; when applied to the dialectical image and Benjamin's historical materialism, it targets the opposite failure — Benjamin's refusal of historical narrative, which is precisely where Benjamin locates ideology. Tracking this chiasm reveals that what Adorno calls "undialectical" means something different in each context, and that Benjamin's own procedure contains a disruptive potential that corresponds, on the terrain of historiography, to the disruptive potential that Adorno grants to the autonomous artwork on the terrain of aesthetics. The two thinkers, on Feldman's reading, are not simply in opposition but are mapping the same problem from opposite methodological commitments.

Helmling (2003) approaches the dispute through the contrast between *constellation* and *dialectical image* as formal-critical methods. Both concepts respond to what Benjamin called the "arrested or stalled progress of modernity," but they respond differently. Adorno's constellation — developed partly in response to and in tension with Benjamin's dialectics at a standstill — extends and subjects to immanent critique what Benjamin had formulated as an image; Adorno worried that Benjamin's images *reinforced* stasis rather than critiquing it, that the "Medusa-gaze" Benjamin turned on his objects petrified them rather than exposing the reification already operative within them. Helmling maps this as a productive tension rather than a clean division: each method gains something the other lacks. The constellation's critical mediation preserves the capacity for negative dialectical judgment that the image risks sacrificing; the image's arrest preserves a materiality of historical content that the constellation, in its determination to keep thought moving, risks sublimating.

Jorgensen (2014) adds a third axis to the dispute by tracing how the concept of the dialectical image functions differently across the Frankfurt-Surrealism divide — not only for Benjamin and Adorno but also for Breton, whose deployment of image-shock aimed at the liberation of desire rather than historiographic illumination. This comparative mapping clarifies what is at stake in Adorno's objection: by charging Benjamin with proximity to surrealist immanence, Adorno is pointing toward the risk that the image's political force will be consumed in an aestheticizing shock of recognition rather than converted into the sustained conceptual labor that critical theory demands.

Montanelli (2020) reconstructs how this dispute played out in the specific textual negotiations of the Baudelaire project: Benjamin's revision of "The Paris of the Second Empire" under Adorno's pressure produced an incomplete reconciliation of their methodological differences, not a resolution. Benjamin conceded the demand for greater mediation without fully abandoning the image's claim to self-legibility. The correspondence as a whole thus documents a productive impasse rather than a correction, and the dialectical image that emerges from it is already marked by the pressure of the dispute. The methodological tension Adorno identified — between the image as an epistemological event and the image as a moment of mediated thought — remains active in Benjamin's late work and is not dissolved by any of the revisions he undertook. For any comprehensive account of Benjamin's philosophy of history, this impasse is not a biographical detail but a structural feature: the claim of the image to arrest thought and the claim of critique to keep it moving define the space within which Benjamin's method operates.

### 3.3 Surrealism, Dream, Awakening, and Phantasmagoria

The surrealist genealogy of the dialectical image opens a different set of resources — and risks — for Benjamin's historiographic method. Cohen (1993) establishes that Benjamin's *Arcades Project* is best understood as a critical appropriation and materialist transformation of surrealism: specifically of Breton's concept of "profane illumination," the secular and politically charged ecstasy produced by shock recognition of the social content latent in the commodity image-world. On Cohen's reading, the "dream" in Benjamin is not a metaphor for ideological mystification but a historically specific collective unconscious that functions as historical evidence — the record of a class's wish-images and fears encoded in the material culture of modernity. To awaken from the dream is to read it critically, and the dialectical image is the instrument of that reading. The historiographic import is direct: where Breton sought liberation of desire, Benjamin sought collective awakening as historical knowledge.

Khatib (2014) traces how Benjamin's 1929 Surrealism essay — with its call to "win the energies of intoxication for the revolution" — attempts to transform surrealist *profane illumination* into a revolutionary political force through what Benjamin calls "anthropological materialism." On Khatib's account, this is the site where Benjamin's theological and materialist commitments are most productively and most tensionally combined: the intoxicatory image-shock of surrealism is redirected from individual ecstasy toward collective bodily experience as a political medium. Ibarlúcia (2017) extends this analysis using newly available primary sources, showing that Benjamin's concept of the "organization of pessimism" — drawn partly from Pierre Naville and the Surrealist left — anticipates the Theses' rejection of progressive optimism and their embrace of a catastrophic, interruptive temporality. Surrealism's politics of failure and defeat, on Ibarlúcia's reading, is the proximate source of the philosophical posture Benjamin later calls brushing history against the grain.

Pujol-León (2025) presses the materialist reading furthest by arguing that *phantasmagoria* — not the dialectical image — is the central concept of Benjamin's late *Arcades* work. Grounded in his intensified engagement with Marx's mature writings and Karl Korsch's commentaries in the late 1930s, phantasmagoria is reinterpreted not as a psychological category but as a structure of social-aesthetic mediation intrinsically linked to capitalism's organization of temporality and historical consciousness. On this account, the dialectical image is what *disrupts* phantasmagoric temporality by interrupting "empty time": it is not independent of the critique of political economy but is its dialectical correlate. Pujol-León's argument repositions the entire method as materialist in a strong sense — closer to the Marxian *Darstellung* of commodity logic than to messianic illumination. This directly contests the surrealist-theological readings that treat the image primarily as a mode of shock-awakening, and reinforces the orthodox-Marxist worry, formulated with particular sharpness by Fan (2024), that the dialectical image remains confined to the transformation of mass consciousness and never adequately addresses the economic and production relations that, on standard Marxist analysis, are causally more fundamental. Fan's reading of the Baudelaire poems as the site where Benjamin moves from melancholic allegory to the dialectical image tracks a genuine methodological advance within Benjamin's corpus, but concludes that the advance still falls short of the political-economic analysis that revolution requires.

Together, these contributions show that the materialist pole of Benjamin's method is not unified: it encompasses Buck-Morss's visual epistemology, Cohen's surrealist historiography, Pujol-León's value-theoretical phantasmagoria, and Fan's critical demand for harder Marxist content. The Adorno dispute does not simply divide "objective critique" from "psychologized image" — it runs through the materialist camp itself, as different materialisms contest what it means for historical knowledge to be grounded in the social logic of capitalism rather than in the illuminating flash of an image encounter.

## Section 4: Experience, Remembrance, and Redemption

### 4.1 Erfahrung and Erlebnis

Benjamin's diagnosis of historical modernity as catastrophe is grounded not only in his critique of progressivist temporality but in an account of what happens to experience under modern conditions. The key distinction is between *Erfahrung*—long, cumulative, communicable experience anchored in tradition and narrative—and *Erlebnis*, the isolated, shock-absorbing perceptual event that industrial modernity imposes on its subjects. The canonical formulations are found in "The Storyteller" (1936) and the Baudelaire essays of the late 1930s, but the distinction carries philosophical weight well beyond those specific texts.

Lindroos (2001) traces the experiential dimension to Benjamin's account of the fragmentation of communal life: the decline of *Erfahrung* does not merely impoverish individual consciousness but dissolves the narrative bonds through which communities reproduce themselves across time. The storyteller's *Erfahrung* was never simply personal; it was the form in which shared, transmissible wisdom circulated. Its replacement by the novel—and, more radically, by the information of the daily press—marks the scattering of community into private, non-transmissible *Erlebnis*. Crucially, Lindroos argues that Benjamin's historiographic response to this diagnosis is not nostalgic reconstruction but a critical practice that works through fragments, preserving traces of communal experience without fabricating lost wholes.

The Baudelaire essays present the shock-structure of *Erlebnis* in its most acute form: Baudelaire's *flâneur* absorbs the crowd's jolts as an artist of Chockerlebnis, and his poetry becomes, for Benjamin, both a symptom of and a resource for diagnosing modern experience. Bielsa (2022) draws out an underappreciated dimension of this engagement by examining Benjamin's translations of Baudelaire alongside his theoretical essays. For Bielsa, translation models the same redemptive transfer of experience across temporal and linguistic gaps that Benjamin's historiography attempts: the translator and the materialist historian share a vocation of rescuing meaning from the ruins of its original context. The Baudelaire project thus binds the philosophy of experience to the philosophy of history at a practical level, not merely a theoretical one.

Elsaesser (2009), reading Benjamin through film theory, uses the *Erfahrung*/*Erlebnis* distinction to characterize cinema spectatorship as producing precisely the intense, non-integrated perceptual event that Benjamin associates with modernity: cinema offers *Erlebnis* without *Erfahrung*, a state once associated with trauma and now normalized as the standard form of the media event. While Elsaesser's context is film studies, the reconstruction of the conceptual pair clarifies how it functions across Benjamin's writings on Baudelaire, technology, and media.

The Proustian axis connects experiential diagnosis to historiographic practice. Llorens (2018) reconstructs Benjamin's reading of involuntary memory through a range of philosophical interlocutors—Freud, Bergson, Baudelaire—and argues that involuntary memory, for Benjamin, is the paradigmatic instance of "profane illumination": a moment in which a past image breaks into the present with auratic intensity, offering a template for *Eingedenken*. Proustian involuntary memory is not merely a literary motif for Benjamin but a phenomenological model for the kind of remembrance that can interrupt the flow of homogeneous time and open a "now of recognizability." Llorens reads the Proust essay as the philosophical staging ground for the theory of historiographic remembrance that culminates in the "Theses." Loveluck (2011) situates this move within the broader hermeneutical tradition, arguing that Benjamin's project constitutes a "hermeneutical materialism" that seeks to redeem human experience by charting a path between idealist hermeneutics of authenticity (Heidegger) and positivist historicism—grounding interpretation in the concrete, material manifestations of social life rather than in the nostalgic continuity of tradition.

### 4.2 Eingedenken, Rettung, and the Redemption of the Past

*Eingedenken*—Benjamin's term for a form of remembrance that carries redemptive historical force—is the conceptual hinge between the experiential diagnosis and the historiographic practice. Unlike voluntary historiographical memory, which organizes the past under the categories of a sovereign present, *Eingedenken* draws on the involuntary, interruptive character of Proustian memory and fuses it with a messianic structure: it can "complete" what was left undone, rescue the defeated from oblivion, and open the present to redemptive possibility. Palermo (2011) traces a developmental arc in Benjamin's use of the concept, arguing that *Eingedenken* marks a decisive shift in his late work: messianic time ceases to be an eschatological event awaited from without and becomes a structure that remembrance itself can activate. The "happiness" of the remembered and the possibility of historical redemption are thus internally connected—each generation's capacity for *Eingedenken* is the narrow gate through which the Messiah "might enter" (Thesis XVIII).

Alías (2023) offers a related but more cautious formulation, describing *Eingedenken* as an "eschatological remainder" (*resto escatológico*) within Benjamin's secular-materialist philosophy: even after theology's categories have been materialized, a residual messianic structure persists, preventing historical time from collapsing into progressivist continuity. For Alías, this is a formal, not a substantive, theological commitment—the eschatological structure is preserved as a regulative frame, not as doctrinal content.

This interpretation confronts a significant critical objection. Tiedemann and Horkheimer, from within the materialist tradition, denied the political significance of *Eingedenken*, dismissing it as theological or confining it to the realm of aesthetics. For these critics, Benjamin's messianic vocabulary is not merely metaphorical but constitutively theological, and a materialism that depends on it forfeits its political and explanatory force. Bohn (2019) directly addresses this objection, arguing that the Tiedemann-Horkheimer critique misunderstands the structure of Benjamin's project. For Bohn, the theological dimension of *Eingedenken* is not a residue to be overcome but the very condition of its counter-ideological force: it is precisely the blend of materialist and messianic thinking that gives remembrance its capacity to break with ideology. Stripping the theological from *Eingedenken* in the name of political credibility would, on this reading, disarm the concept rather than clarify it.

Friedlander (2012) situates *Rettung* (rescue) as the broader philosophical category within which *Eingedenken* operates: the task of Benjamin's philosophy is to rescue past appearances by finding the constellation in which they become legible—what Benjamin calls the "now of recognizability." Rescue is not restoration but a specific philosophical act of making the past flash up in its dialectical, politically charged relation to the present. Jessop (2013) extends this framework toward Benjamin's philosophy of childhood, arguing that the child's mimetic, non-instrumental mode of perception models the kind of perceptual openness that *Eingedenken* reactivates: remembrance is not only an intellectual-historiographic operation but depends on a phenomenology of recognition that modernity systematically suppresses.

Losiggio (2018) approaches *Eingedenken* from the angle of historical subjectivity, tracing a line from Benjamin's early epistemology through his engagements with Freud and Lukács to his late concept of remembrance. Losiggio argues that *Eingedenken* is Benjamin's answer to the question of how a subject can be an agent of historical transformation while remaining embedded in unconscious and collective structures: remembrance synthesizes the epistemological and political strands of his thought in a single concept. Whether this synthesis is coherent—whether remembrance can carry both the individual-involuntary phenomenology of Proust and the collective-political force of Marxist historiography—remains a genuine open question in the scholarship.

### 4.3 Melancholy, Mourning, and the Ruin

If *Eingedenken* names the redemptive operation, melancholy names the subjective posture from which that operation proceeds. Pensky (1993) offers a sustained analysis of melancholy in Benjamin, arguing that it is not a psychological condition or a political disability but a dialectical epistemic stance—a mode of knowing that dwells on ruin and fragment precisely in order to redeem them. Drawing on the *Trauerspiel* book, the Baudelaire essays, and the "Theses," Pensky reconstructs melancholy as Benjamin's characteristically modern form of historical consciousness: one that refuses consolation (the false reconciliation of triumphalist historiography) while remaining open to the messianic interruption that *Eingedenken* can seize. Allegory—the figure that makes ruin visible by showing how the fallen object persists in its own decay—is, on this reading, both the formal principle of the *Trauerspiel* and the historiographic mode of the "Theses."

The continuity between the early allegorical theory of the *Trauerspiel* and the late philosophy of history is not merely biographical. Friedlander (2012) argues that the concept of rescue (*Rettung*) bridges the two works: the allegorist rescues the moribund object by exposing the death's head in it, while the materialist historian rescues the defeated by making their unfulfilled claims legible in the present. Mourning, in both registers, is a form of fidelity to what has been lost—a refusal to let it be sublated into a consoling narrative of progress or redemption-as-restoration.

This Benjaminian posture of melancholic mourning has become a contested resource in contemporary political thought. Pensky's reconstruction foregrounds a tension that later debates—particularly over what Wendy Brown (2003) calls "left melancholy"—will sharpen: whether the political posture of dwelling on loss enables critical consciousness or produces a paralysis disguised as radicalism. The stakes of that dispute, which surfaces explicitly in Section 4, are already implicit in the structure Benjamin gives to melancholy: a dialectical epistemic posture that opens onto redemption, but only if the "small gate" of *Eingedenken* is available to pass through. Whether that gate can be opened by a materialist practice—or whether it requires a theological key that secularization has already misplaced—is the question that runs from the *Trauerspiel* book to the final theses and remains unresolved in the scholarship reviewed here.

## Section 5: Intellectual Context and Critical Reception: Adjudicating the Tension

### 5.1 The Intellectual Constellation and the Secular/Theological Dispute

The most consequential institutional fact about Benjamin's reception is that it was shaped from the outset by competing claims to his legacy. Gershom Scholem's memoir of their friendship argues that the "real" Benjamin was a Jewish religious thinker whose deepest impulses were theological and mystical, and that his engagement with Brecht and Marxism constituted a misguided — indeed fatal — detour from his authentic intellectual trajectory (Scholem 1981). Scholem's reading identifies the messianic theology of the Theses as the culmination of the linguistic-mystical commitments Benjamin had formed in early Weimar, and it leaves no doubt that Marxism figures in this account as an outer political garment for an essentially kabbalistic vision. Jacobson (2003) provides the fullest scholarly elaboration of this position, reconstructing the shared framework of Jewish messianism, divine justice, and the critique of law that linked Benjamin and Scholem from the beginning and arguing that Benjamin's theological commitments are primary, not instrumental.

Against this stands the Brecht relationship and everything it represents. Wizisla (2009), working from archival sources, demonstrates that Benjamin's engagement with Brecht was intellectually serious and reciprocal — far from the contaminating influence Scholem described — and that the collaboration shaped Benjamin's materialist methodology in ways that cannot be explained away as political accommodation. The materialist pole is therefore no less authentic than the theological one. Eiland and Jennings (2014) adopt a similar balanced position in their biographical account, treating both the Scholem and Adorno relationships as genuine intellectual engagements and resisting the narrative that either pole distorts a truer underlying Benjamin.

Beyond the Scholem-Adorno binary, the reception is complicated by further axes. Mattner (2023) shows that Hannah Arendt's 1968 New Yorker essay introduced a third interpretive framework into the Anglophone world — Benjamin as melancholic collector and literary critic, a figure of failure and political impotence — that competed with both the theological and dialectical readings and shaped how generations of English-language readers approached the Theses. Raz-Krakotzkin (2013) adds a further dimension by arguing that the Scholem-Benjamin relationship was structured by their divergent responses to Zionism, and that this political difference inflected Scholem's posthumous canonization of Benjamin in ways that have been underexamined. Löwy (2019) situates Benjamin within the broader constellation of Central European Jewish radical intellectuals — Lukács, Bloch, Landauer — and proposes that an "elective affinity" between German Romanticism, Jewish messianism, and revolutionary politics explains the synthesis better than any account that privileges one strand at the expense of the other.

Recent scholarship has largely refused the theological-vs-materialist binary as a false dichotomy. Naishtat (2019) proposes that theology functions in Benjamin's philosophy as an "invisible organon" — a set of conceptual resources deployed in transformed, secularized mode without losing their distinctive philosophical force. Brusotti (2017) illuminates the Blanqui-Nietzsche strand: Benjamin reads Nietzsche's eternal recurrence not as cosmological doctrine but as the ideological form that commodity capitalism takes in the cultural unconscious, a mythic time of perpetual sameness against which the explosive Jetztzeit of messianic interruption is defined. What the reception scholarship increasingly converges on is that Benjamin's originality lies in the indissolubility of the two registers, not in the dominance of one over the other.

### 5.2 The Frankfurt School Critique: Habermas and Wolin

The strongest objections to Benjamin's philosophy of history come not from outside but from within the critical-theoretical tradition. Habermas's essay "Consciousness-Raising or Rescuing Critique," collected in Philosophical-Political Profiles (1983), is the most systematic attempt by a major Frankfurt School figure to assess and contain Benjamin. Habermas acknowledges the diagnostic power of Benjamin's arrest of historical continua — the capacity to seize a moment of revolutionary possibility — but argues that the messianic categories are untranslatable into communicative rationality. The strategy Habermas names "rescuing critique" proposes to retain the critical content of Benjamin's thought while stripping away its theological-mystical residue: what can be salvaged is Benjamin's sensitivity to the suffering sedimented in historical wreckage; what must be discarded is the claim that redemption is a structural category of historical knowledge rather than a metaphor requiring rational reconstruction. In The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity (1987), Habermas extends this critique to the broader trajectory of first-generation Frankfurt School thought: the total critique of instrumental reason — in which Benjamin's negative-theological correctives play a role — reaches an aporia in which critique can no longer ground itself rationally, since reason itself has become the target. Benjamin's messianism, on this account, is a symptom of the "counter-Enlightenment" pathology that substitutes illumination for argument.

The force of this objection should not be underestimated. Habermas is identifying a structural vulnerability in Benjamin's position: if the Jetztzeit is recognizable only through a form of perception that escapes discursive justification — what Benjamin sometimes calls "profane illumination," the flash of recognition available to the historical materialist who brushes history against the grain — then it is unclear on what basis competing claims about which moments are genuinely redemptive can be adjudicated. The messianic structure appears to underwrite a decisionism about historical significance that cannot be brought before the tribunal of intersubjective reason. This is not a merely academic worry; it bears on the political defensibility of Benjamin's entire historiographic program.

Wolin (1994) presses a parallel objection in a sustained English-language critical study. Where Habermas focuses on the untranslatability of Benjamin's categories into communicative reason, Wolin argues that the "aesthetic of redemption" produces a politically ambiguous framework that cannot ground normative foundations for critical theory. The messianic dimension of Benjamin's philosophy — particularly the theory of history — reflects an irrationalist horizon in which the catastrophic structure of redemption may as readily support political quietism or even a certain aestheticized fascination with rupture as it supports progressive political action. Wolin's critique targets precisely the feature that Benjamin's defenders prize: the non-teleological, non-progressive openness of Jetztzeit, which refuses to specify in advance what a moment of revolutionary possibility looks like or how it will be actualized. That openness, Wolin contends, is a normative vacuum rather than a virtue.

Frelek (2025), reading Adorno's 1962 essay "Progress" in the context of the climate crisis, traces how this legacy was worked through differently within critical theory: Adorno's "negative hope" — a form of critical vigilance that refuses both resigned catastrophism and naive progressivism — is shown to depend on Benjamin's earlier reformulations while subjecting them to a dialectical discipline that Habermas finds lacking. Frelek's argument suggests that the Frankfurt School critique of Benjamin was not monolithic: Adorno retained a Benjaminian impulse while refusing the premature image-thinking that Habermas also diagnosed, a position that lies between Benjamin's messianism and Habermas's communicative rationalism. Neither the Habermasian dismissal nor the wholesale affirmation of the messianic framework captures what the reception actually produced.

### 5.3 The Marxist Critique from Within

If Habermas and Wolin attack from the rationalist flank, Clark (2003) attacks from the Marxist flank, producing a critique that is structurally symmetrical but dialectically opposite. Clark argues that Benjamin's philosophy of history is critically impoverished by his inadequate engagement with Marx's specific analyses of capitalism — particularly the theory of value and the logic of commodity fetishism. The Marxism operative in the Arcades Project and the Theses tends to remain at the level of cultural superstructure, at the sphere of appearances and phantasmagorias, without descending to the more determinative level of economic base that would explain how those appearances are generated and reproduced. The result is a philosophy of history that is culturally acute but structurally underspecified: Benjamin can describe the experience of capitalist modernity with extraordinary precision, but he cannot account for the material mechanisms that produce and reproduce it. Clark uses this argument not to dismiss Benjamin but to explain both what is most powerful and what is most mystifying in his account of historical experience.

Fan (2024) articulates a parallel concern from a different angle, arguing that Benjamin's dialectical images remain limited by their focus on the transformation of consciousness rather than material revolution. The allegorical and dialectical imagery in the Baudelaire studies, Fan contends, displaces the question of economic base and productive relations in favor of a theory of cultural awakening that an orthodox Marxist framework would regard as insufficient. From this perspective, Benjamin's messianic-aesthetic categories invert the hierarchy of base and superstructure and substitute cultural diagnosis for political economy. Pujol-León (2025), by contrast, challenges this reading directly: his argument that phantasmagoria — grounded in Benjamin's late encounter with Marx's mature writings and Korsch's commentaries — is the central methodological category of the late Arcades work repositions the project as far more materialist than critics like Fan suggest. On Pujol-León's account, Benjamin came to see revolution specifically as an interruption of capitalist phantasmagorias of empty time, a conception that depends precisely on the Marxian critique of political economy rather than displacing it.

List (2020) reclaims Benjamin's materialism from a different direction, arguing against Agamben's depoliticizing reception. She contends that Agamben's political defeatism stems from a "Schmittian reduction of Benjamin" that replaces the historical-materialist critique with messianic nihilism, and that recovering Benjamin's own materialism against this assimilation demonstrates that a genuinely radical political theology must remain anchored in historical analysis rather than dissolving into juridical abstraction. For List, Benjamin's materialism is not a residue or an outer layer but the critical substance that prevents the theological dimension from becoming politically inert.

### 5.4 Contemporary Extensions and the Left-Melancholy Debate

The contemporary reception of Benjamin's philosophy of history proceeds along two primary axes: the extension of his temporal concepts into postcolonial critique and political theology, and the debate over whether his characteristic mode of historical mourning is politically enabling or paralyzing.

Agamben's The Time That Remains (2005) constructs a political ontology by reading Paul's Epistles through the lens of Benjamin's Jetztzeit, arguing that the "time of the now" — the messianic remnant of time that is neither present nor past — displaces both apocalyptic finalism and progressive teleology. As Cimino (2016) analyzes, this extension draws decisively on Benjamin's temporal concepts but transforms them through Pauline exegesis in ways that detach messianism from historical-materialist analysis. Brittain (2010) compares Adorno's and Agamben's distinct engagements with Benjamin's messianism, showing that while both mobilize a form of interruption that refuses to name its positive content, Adorno's negative-dialectical approach retains a critical distance from the political-theological register that Agamben explicitly embraces. List (2020) presses this point further, arguing that Agamben's reading ultimately colonizes the radical openness of Benjamin's messianism with a defeatist logic of the state of exception, and that Hay (2024) similarly contests the Pauline-antinomian reading by recovering Benjamin's debts to Hermann Cohen's prophetic-normative tradition, which is incompatible with the anarcholibertarian politics Agamben projects onto Benjamin.

Chakrabarty (2000) deploys Benjamin's critique of homogeneous empty time for postcolonial historiography in a manner that bypasses the Agambenian move. His distinction between "History 1" (capital's own self-reproductive logic) and "History 2" (the diverse pasts that capital encounters but cannot fully subsume) maps onto Benjamin's distinction between the progressive continuum and the messianic interruption, making the Theses' anti-progressivism a resource for subaltern historiography. This is one of the most consequential extensions of Benjamin's temporal concepts, demonstrating that the critique of Eurocentrism and the critique of progressivism are not merely analogous but structurally related through the shared target of homogeneous empty time.

The most contested debate within recent reception concerns melancholy. Brown (2003) coins the concept "left melancholy" to describe a political condition in which the Left remains attached to its lost objects — socialist projects, class identities, revolutionary possibilities — in a way that forecloses adaptation to new political conditions. Drawing on Benjamin's own critical essay "Left Melancholy," which targeted the melancholic bohemian intellectual who mistakes attachment to loss for political engagement, Brown extends the critique to a broader syndrome in which mourning for what has been lost substitutes for engagement with what is. The charge against the Benjaminian inheritance is specific: a philosophy of history organized around Eingedenken and the redemption of past suffering risks cultivating a political affect — attachment to defeat, to the unrealized — that is structurally incapable of orienting toward new struggles. Traverso (2016) offers the most sustained rebuttal, arguing that left-wing melancholia is a legitimate and necessary mode of relating to the defeats of the socialist tradition. Against Brown's framing, Traverso argues that melancholy is an epistemological and ethical attitude rather than a pathological fixation: it holds open the memory of past defeats and the potential of foreclosed futures, preventing the premature closure that amnesia or compulsive mourning would impose. Benjamin's mournful historiography, on this reading, is exemplary rather than paralyzing. Brzezaniak (2024) provides the most recent systematic overview, surveying the left-melancholy literature from Benjamin through Brown and Traverso to Flatley and Fisher, and arguing for a positive model of melancholy as historiographic attitude — one that refuses both the psychoanalytic framing (melancholy as symptom to be overcome) and the sentimental one (melancholy as a relationship to defeat), orienting instead toward the future possibilities still latent in past losses.

The disagreement between Brown and Traverso is not merely terminological. It concerns whether the affective orientation that Benjamin's philosophy of history invites — mourning the tradition of the oppressed, holding the unrealized open — is the condition of possibility for genuinely radical politics, or whether it is the very form of its obstruction. The literature does not resolve this question, and it remains one of the live political stakes of taking Benjamin's philosophy of history seriously today.

## Section 6: Conclusion: Mapping the Tension, Not Resolving It

The puppet-and-dwarf image that opens the "Theses on the Philosophy of History" is not merely a striking figure of speech. It announces the constitutive problem around which the entire body of secondary literature organizes itself: the relation between historical materialism and theology in Benjamin's late philosophy of history is neither a resolved synthesis nor a simple contradiction, but an irreducible tension that generates interpretive disagreement at every level of the text. Every concept examined in this review—Jetztzeit, the dialectical image, the Angel of History, Eingedenken, Rettung—refracts this tension in a different register, and the debates over method, temporality, experience, and reception all return to the same underlying question: does theology function as a productive, operative force in Benjamin's materialism, or does it introduce a residue that ultimately undoes it?

Three interpretive camps have emerged with sufficient clarity to serve as orientation across the literature. The theological or messianic camp—associated with figures such as Scholem and Jacobson—holds that the Theses are best understood as expressing a fundamentally Jewish messianic vision, with historical materialism serving as an outer idiom for religious commitments that remain primary. The Marxist or materialist camp—represented by Löwy (2005), List, and Khatib in his more political mode—reads Benjamin as a heterodox Marxist who deploys theological motifs instrumentally, to revitalize a critique of progress that orthodox Marxism had exhausted. Khatib's formulation of "the messianic without messianism" (2013) points toward a third position: the materialism/theology binary is itself a false dichotomy, and Benjamin's innovation lies precisely in producing a thought that cannot be classified under either rubric alone. Tiedemann (1983), tracing the sources behind each thesis with philological rigor, and Wohlfarth (1978), analyzing the "messianic structure" of the Theses as a productive paradox, both arrive at a version of this third posture: the tension is constitutive of the text, not a flaw to be corrected by choosing one side.

Against these affirmative readings, the critical objections press with genuine force. Habermas (1983; 1987) acknowledges Benjamin's diagnostic power—the capacity to interrupt historical continua and seize a moment of possibility—but argues that the messianic categories are untranslatable into communicative rationality and represent an irrationalist residue that critical theory must shed rather than inherit. Wolin (1994) presses a related but distinct charge: the "aesthetic of redemption" that structures Benjamin's messianic historiography cannot supply stable normative foundations for political critique and leaves the political implications of his thought productively ambiguous. From the opposite direction, Clark (2003) argues that Benjamin's failure to read Marx carefully—his inattention to value, base/superstructure, and class as analytical categories—means that his analysis remains at the level of cultural superstructure and lacks the explanatory grip that a genuinely Marxist philosophy of history requires. Brown (1999) identifies a further political liability: the Benjaminian inheritance of mourning and melancholy risks producing a left that remains attached to lost objects rather than orienting itself to new struggles.

Several questions remain genuinely open. Whether theology in the Theses is operationally constitutive or a residual idiom that Benjamin never fully secularized is contested and, given the fragmentary character of the text, probably undecidable by philological means alone. Whether the dialectical image is an objective historical category or a psychologized dream-image—the charge Adorno pressed in their correspondence—remains the methodological crux, with the Adorno-Benjamin dispute still serving as the reference point for contemporary discussions of how history and thought relate. Whether Benjamin's anti-progressive historiography is politically enabling or paralyzing continues to divide the left-melancholy debate. And the relation between the aesthetic Benjamin of the Trauerspiel book and the historical Benjamin of the Theses remains under-theorized: the allegorical imagination and the historiographic method are clearly connected, but how exactly they hang together has received less systematic attention than either pole alone.

What a recurrent scholarly posture—shared by Tiedemann, Wohlfarth, and Khatib—suggests is that these open questions may be the right product of engagement with the Theses rather than failures of interpretation. A text that refuses to resolve the tension it dramatizes invites reading that maps rather than adjudicates. The present review has followed that orientation: tracing the puppet-dwarf relation as it appears in temporal structure, historiographic method, the theory of experience, and the critical reception, without declaring the doll victorious over the dwarf or vice versa.
