## Section 3: Intellectual Relations and Sources

**Section Purpose**: Situate Foucault's historical thought among its formative debts and polemics, clarifying what is inherited versus novel. Foreground each relation as a debate about *fidelity and departure*.

Foucault's historiographical vocabulary did not emerge from nothing, and in every major case the secondary literature divides over whether he faithfully extends a source or strategically departs from it. This section stages four such disputes — Nietzsche, Hegel, French historical epistemology, and the Annales/structuralist/Marxist cluster — as live disagreements rather than settled pedigrees.

### Subsection 3.1: Nietzsche — how Nietzschean is Foucauldian genealogy? (debate)

No relation is more contested than Foucault's debt to Nietzsche, and the recent literature divides into four positions. A strong-continuity reading comes from Garruzzo (2025), who argues that genuine genealogy — as distinct from the looser normatively-loaded uses of the term in contemporary social philosophy — inherits Nietzsche's "will to power" wholesale: it investigates the contingent integration of an institution within a regime of power, and its critical payoff is not normative insight but "tactical knowledge" of how power has been exercised and resisted. Testa (2025) offers a more archival continuity thesis, recovering Foucault's 1969–1970 Vincennes lectures on Nietzsche to show that, alongside the familiar diagnostic register of genealogy, Foucault developed a medical-therapeutic one in which the "historical sense" functions as both poison and cure — material predating and refining the canonical 1971 essay "Nietzsche, Genealogy, History."

Against these stand two critical readings arguing Foucault departs from, or misreads, his source. Rockhill (2020) challenges the essay's foundational textual move: the Herkunft/Ursprung distinction Foucault presents as "properly Nietzschean" does not, he argues, actually exist in Nietzsche's text, making Foucault's appropriation a strategic invention rather than an exposition — leading Rockhill to propose an alternative "counter-history" beyond genealogy. Manley (2025) mounts a parallel but independent challenge via Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation: where Foucault presents an anti-teleological Nietzsche suspicious of origins, Nietzsche's own essay insists on the necessity of ahistorical, quasi-teleological figures against corrosive historicism, making Foucault's Nietzsche one-sided and Nietzsche himself "an alternative — rather than a precursor — to the Foucauldian paradigm." Finally, Karlsen and Villadsen (2018) complicate the picture from another angle, arguing that Foucault's involvement in the Maoist-inspired Groupe d'information sur les prisons left identifiable marks on the genealogical method and the power-knowledge vocabulary of the 1976 lectures, making Nietzsche not genealogy's sole dialogue partner. What remains unresolved is thus layered: whether genealogy extends Nietzsche's will-to-power framework, selectively (mis)appropriates his vocabulary, or answers to formative pressures partly external to Nietzsche altogether.

### Subsection 3.2: Hegel, the dialectic, and teleology

The standard characterization of Foucault as anti-Hegelian and anti-teleological is developed from two directions. Carr (2016) contrasts Husserl's and Foucault's shared use of "the historical a priori" to show they mean fundamentally different things: Husserl's presupposes an intrinsic teleological structure to history, Foucault's is explicitly anti-teleological and discontinuist (this entry carries an INCOMPLETE flag but a substantive note field, and is cited cautiously on that basis). Olssen (2004) reaches a structurally similar conclusion via Marxism rather than phenomenology: despite parallels between Foucault's method and Marxism as accounts of history and social structure, Foucault's decisive break is his rejection of Hegel's conception of history as a "unified developing totality," together with essences, teleology, and any utopian trajectory tied to laws of economic development.

Giuliani (2021) complicates this consensus at its source with a continuity thesis. Reading History of Madness (1961) against Hegel's Phenomenology of Spirit, she argues Foucault's configuration of madness across the classical and modern age traces a path toward self-awareness for Western rationality, and that the encounter between poverty and unreason in the hôpital, and in Pinel's asile, can be read as figures of dialectical movement — locating a speculative-dialectical structure inside Foucault's earliest major work. The tension is left open: the anti-Hegelian self-understanding archaeology and genealogy later claim (Carr, Olssen) sits uneasily with Giuliani's textual case that Foucault's first historical study retains a Hegelian speculative dynamic.

### Subsection 3.3: French historical epistemology — Bachelard and Canguilhem

Here the dispute concerns not whether Foucault inherits from Bachelard and Canguilhem but precisely what. Peña-Guzmán (2020) frames the tradition — Cavaillès, Bachelard, Canguilhem, Foucault — as unified by two commitments: scientific discourse, not theory or practice, is epistemology's proper object, and concepts are the fundamental units of that discourse, positioning archaeology as continuous with a discourse-and-concept-centered program. Chimisso (2003) narrows to Canguilhem's "normative history" of concepts and its relation to the normal/pathological distinction; though critical of the external philosophical norms this smuggles into history-writing, she concludes Canguilhem's practice of analyzing concepts "from below" offers a generalizable model against which Foucault's own explicitly anti-normative, discontinuist archaeology can be measured. Castelli Gattinara (2018) supplies the connective genealogy directly: Canguilhem radicalizes Bachelard by recognizing the historicity of every aspect of scientific knowledge, including its errors, and explicitly identifies this radicalized, pluralist historicization as opening "the way to the researches of Foucault and [Michel] Serres."

The stakes are sharpened by D'Cruz (2024), who argues Foucault retains a substantive naturalistic commitment traceable to this tradition: his normative epistemological judgments, especially about the human sciences, depend on a comparative respect for the natural sciences, and the descriptive rigor of genealogy derives from its being modeled on empirical inquiry. This reading pushes back against interpretations that take Foucault to reduce knowledge to power and collapse into unconstrained relativism, positioning the Canguilhem inheritance as a resource against the relativism charge taken up later in the review (5.1).

### Subsection 3.4: Annales, structuralism, and Marxism

Three overlapping affiliation disputes converge here. On Annales, Gürkan (2017) treats Braudel and Foucault as engaged in direct textual dialogue rather than loose resemblance: their treatments of structure and event are best grasped through each thinker's own apparatus — Braudel's tripartite schema of economic civilization for the history of capitalism, Foucault's analytics of power and government for (neo)liberalism — and Gürkan works toward integrating rather than opposing the two.

On structuralism, whether Foucault's discontinuist archaeology is itself a structuralist historical method is answered affirmatively by Bryanik (2025), who argues against the assumption that structuralism is anti-historicist: it combines synchronic analysis of internal relations with diachronic analysis organized around discontinuity and "mutation," with Foucault's archaeologist — like Propp's folklorist or a geologist — excavating strata separated by ruptures. Ryder (2013) approaches the same question through Foucault's relation to Althusser specifically, arguing that Foucault's rejection of Marxism in The Order of Things targeted a humanist, historicist Marx while conceding a more compelling non-humanist Marxism for which Althusser's structuralist innovations were crucial — an epistemological divergence rather than a simple rejection.

Marxism proper is treated as disaggregated rather than uniformly hostile. Olssen (2004) describes the relation as "rewriting rather than rejection": Foucault shares Marxism's treatment of knowledge as linked to power and its assertion of historical relativity while rejecting Hegelian totality, essences, teleology, and revolutionary utopianism in favor of localized resistance. Choat (2010) extends this non-rejectionist reading comparatively, situating Foucault's "history of the present" alongside Lyotard's, Derrida's, and Deleuze's own reworkings of Marx as one instance of a broader post-structuralist pattern of engagement rather than dismissal. Sartori (2020) tests the limits of that engagement directly: reading Foucault's genealogical critique of nineteenth-century historicism — which had already, in The Order of Things, consigned Marx to an episteme organized around "historicity" and "the human essence" — against Moishe Postone's postfoundationalist Marx, Sartori argues genealogy's critical purchase does not actually extend to Marx's own categorial analysis of social form, which remains available as a resource for critique immanent to social form rather than reliant on historicist assumptions. Riep (2025), drawing on unpublished 1972 Buffalo lecture recordings, supplies archival confirmation that Foucault's critical distance from Althusserian "scientific Marxism" was explicit and articulated at the transitional moment between archaeology and genealogy, not merely retrospective. Together these accounts leave open whether Foucault's historiography is best classed as a variant of structuralism, a selective heir to non-humanist Marxism, or a method whose critique of historicism, forceful against nineteenth-century teleology, fails to fully reach Marx's own theoretical core.

**Section Summary**: Foucault's sources are matters of active dispute; in every case (Nietzsche, Hegel, Canguilhem, Marx, structuralism) the literature splits between continuity and departure readings.
