## Conclusion

This review has traced a single reorientation of the philosophy of history across six domains of scholarship: Foucault's replacement of continuity, teleology, progress, and the constituting subject-as-origin with discontinuity and rupture, contingency and effective history, power/knowledge, and a historically constituted subject. Archaeology described discursive formations and epistemes without appeal to developmental causation; genealogy, inheriting a Nietzschean vocabulary of *Herkunft* and *Entstehung*, replaced ideology-critique with an account of power and knowledge as mutually constitutive; and the late "history of the present" recast the whole enterprise as a critical ontology of ourselves. Across the secondary literature surveyed, this reorientation is treated less as a settled doctrine than as a working method whose coherence, sources, and legitimacy remain objects of active dispute.

A cross-cutting tension recurs throughout this literature: the relationship between Foucault's professed methodological posture — description of conditions of possibility, refusal of causal-developmental narrative, disavowal of any transcendental standpoint — and his evident normative-political commitments. Garland's exposition of *Discipline and Punish* finds a genealogical narrative that is simultaneously historical description and implicit indictment of disciplinary power, without a stated account of what licenses the move from one to the other (Garland 1986). Fraser presses this into an explicit charge of normative confusion: Foucault relies on evaluative terms like domination and subjugation while insisting power is everywhere and value-neutral (Fraser 1985). Taylor generalizes the objection into a self-refutation charge — if truth is always an effect of power, Foucault's own critical histories cannot ground the emancipatory force he wants them to have (Taylor 1984). Flynn's axial reading of Foucault's oeuvre encounters the same tension from a sympathetic vantage point, leaving unresolved whether the ethics-subjectivation axis is one option among three for charting Foucault's work or the culminating concern that retrospectively unifies it (Flynn 2005). And the genealogy-as-critique debate closing Section 6 shows this tension persisting into Foucault's legacy, as later commentators dispute whether tracing contingent emergence is itself critique or requires normative resources genealogy disclaims. Description and judgment are not obviously separable in Foucault's practice, and the literature has not converged on how, or whether, they can be.

Several questions therefore remain genuinely open rather than resolved by this review: whether the corpus is unified or ruptured, given competing expansion, supplementation, and continuity readings of the archaeology-genealogy transition (Koopman 2008); whether discontinuity is a coherent unit of historical explanation and what ontological status the episteme possesses; whether power/knowledge entails a self-undermining relativism or instead refuses a question-begging demand for extra-historical foundations; whether a decentered subject can still ground critique and agency; and whether genealogy is properly critical or merely descriptive, and whether it survives intact when it travels beyond its original site of articulation.

The literature surveyed also displays identifiable gaps. A systematic realist/anti-realist treatment of Foucauldian discontinuity, comparable to debates over scientific paradigms, remains thin. Direct comparison between Foucault and Ankersmit's narrativism is underdeveloped relative to pairings with White and Arendt. Engagement with non-Anglophone, especially French, secondary scholarship is limited within the discoverable literature this review draws upon. And the ontology of the episteme — what kind of entity it is, and what work "historical a priori" can do — remains an open question even among the most sympathetic reconstructions.

This review has aimed to supply a synthetic, debate-centered map of this terrain: one that reconstructs Foucault's own historiographical practice before staging the controversies it has generated, neither assimilating his work wholesale to analytic or narrativist philosophy of history nor treating his historiography as exempt from the critical assessment historians and philosophers alike have brought to it.
