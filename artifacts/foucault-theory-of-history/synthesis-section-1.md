## Introduction

The traditional philosophy of history that Michel Foucault sets out to dismantle rests on four commitments: continuity, whereby historical periods are linked in an unbroken developmental chain; teleology, whereby that chain moves toward a determinate end; progress, whereby later stages improve upon earlier ones; and the constituting subject, whereby a transcendental or humanist subject stands as the origin and guarantor of historical meaning. Foucault's own methodological writings target each of these commitments directly. In *The Order of Things*, he argues that Western knowledge does not develop continuously but passes through discrete "epistemes"—stable, largely unspoken configurations of thought separated by abrupt ruptures, one at the threshold of the Classical age and one at the threshold of modernity—and he explicitly declines to explain *why* these ruptures occur, restricting the analysis to a description of what changed (Foucault 2005). In "Nietzsche, Genealogy, History," he adapts the Nietzschean vocabulary of Ursprung, Herkunft, and Entstehung to argue against the search for pure origins, proposing instead a "wirkliche Historie" (effective history) attentive to disparity, chance, and the body as the surface on which history is inscribed (Foucault 2019). And in his late essay "What is Enlightenment?," written as a response to Kant, Foucault recasts his entire project retrospectively as a "history of the present"—an inquiry into "what we are" pursued not as a search for universal structures but as critical analysis of the historically specific limits imposed on us, undertaken as a "critical ontology of ourselves" (Foucault 1984).

Against continuity, then, Foucault poses discontinuity and rupture; against teleology, contingency and effective history; against progress, a refusal to rank epistemic configurations against one another; and against the subject as origin, historically constituted subjects who are themselves objects of archaeological and genealogical description. In place of causal-developmental narrative, Foucault offers description of "conditions of possibility"—the historical a priori that governs what can be thought, said, or known within a given configuration, a category whose precise philosophical status (and its debt to Husserlian phenomenology) itself becomes a matter of sustained dispute, as Carr's (2016) contrast between Husserl's teleological and Foucault's anti-teleological deployments of the phrase shows.

This review has a double object: it reconstructs (a) Foucault's own evolving thought, across archaeology, genealogy, and the retrospective self-description as a "history of the present," and (b) the secondary literature that interprets, contests, and extends that thought. A question recurs across both halves of the literature and is worth flagging at the outset: is Foucault best understood as a philosopher or as a historian, and is that dichotomy itself well-formed? Allen (2017) argues that the question is not a neutral request for disciplinary classification but is itself an artifact of the modern historical a priori that Foucault's archaeology sets out to historicize—Foucault is neither simply a philosopher, since he treats philosophy in modernity as a historical enterprise, nor simply a historian, since his historical approach aims to transform the modern historical a priori from within. This framing question resurfaces throughout the review, from the internal reconstruction of Foucault's corpus (Section 1) to the historians' empirical reception (Section 4) and the assessment of his placement within the wider field of philosophy of history (Section 6).

The review proceeds in six sections. Section 1 reconstructs Foucault's theory of history across the corpus, work by work. Section 2 treats archaeology and genealogy as accounts of historical knowledge and method. Section 3 situates Foucault's thought among its intellectual sources and debts. Section 4 turns to historians' empirical reception and critique. Section 5 concentrates the core philosophical debates over relativism, normativity, discontinuity, and the subject. Section 6 traces Foucault's legacy and placement within the philosophy of history. A concluding section synthesizes the recurring tensions and identifies the field's open questions.
