## Introduction

The traditional philosophy of history that Michel Foucault sets out to dismantle rests on four commitments: continuity, whereby historical periods are linked in an unbroken developmental chain; teleology, whereby that chain moves toward a determinate end; progress, whereby later stages improve upon earlier ones; and the constituting subject, whereby a transcendental or humanist subject stands as the origin and guarantor of historical meaning. Foucault's own methodological writings target each of these commitments directly. In *The Order of Things*, he argues that Western knowledge does not develop continuously but passes through discrete "epistemes"—stable, largely unspoken configurations of thought separated by abrupt ruptures, one at the threshold of the Classical age and one at the threshold of modernity—and he explicitly declines to explain *why* these ruptures occur, restricting the analysis to a description of what changed (Foucault 2005). In "Nietzsche, Genealogy, History," he adapts the Nietzschean vocabulary of Ursprung, Herkunft, and Entstehung to argue against the search for pure origins, proposing instead a "wirkliche Historie" (effective history) attentive to disparity, chance, and the body as the surface on which history is inscribed (Foucault 2019). And in his late essay "What is Enlightenment?," written as a response to Kant, Foucault recasts his entire project retrospectively as a "history of the present"—an inquiry into "what we are" pursued not as a search for universal structures but as critical analysis of the historically specific limits imposed on us, undertaken as a "critical ontology of ourselves" (Foucault 1984).

Against continuity, then, Foucault poses discontinuity and rupture; against teleology, contingency and effective history; against progress, a refusal to rank epistemic configurations against one another; and against the subject as origin, historically constituted subjects who are themselves objects of archaeological and genealogical description. In place of causal-developmental narrative, Foucault offers description of "conditions of possibility"—the historical a priori that governs what can be thought, said, or known within a given configuration, a category whose precise philosophical status (and its debt to Husserlian phenomenology) itself becomes a matter of sustained dispute, as Carr's (2016) contrast between Husserl's teleological and Foucault's anti-teleological deployments of the phrase shows.

This review has a double object: it reconstructs (a) Foucault's own evolving thought, across archaeology, genealogy, and the retrospective self-description as a "history of the present," and (b) the secondary literature that interprets, contests, and extends that thought. A question recurs across both halves of the literature and is worth flagging at the outset: is Foucault best understood as a philosopher or as a historian, and is that dichotomy itself well-formed? Allen (2017) argues that the question is not a neutral request for disciplinary classification but is itself an artifact of the modern historical a priori that Foucault's archaeology sets out to historicize—Foucault is neither simply a philosopher, since he treats philosophy in modernity as a historical enterprise, nor simply a historian, since his historical approach aims to transform the modern historical a priori from within. This framing question resurfaces throughout the review, from the internal reconstruction of Foucault's corpus (Section 1) to the historians' empirical reception (Section 4) and the assessment of his placement within the wider field of philosophy of history (Section 6).

The review proceeds in six sections. Section 1 reconstructs Foucault's theory of history across the corpus, work by work. Section 2 treats archaeology and genealogy as accounts of historical knowledge and method. Section 3 situates Foucault's thought among its intellectual sources and debts. Section 4 turns to historians' empirical reception and critique. Section 5 concentrates the core philosophical debates over relativism, normativity, discontinuity, and the subject. Section 6 traces Foucault's legacy and placement within the philosophy of history. A concluding section synthesizes the recurring tensions and identifies the field's open questions.

## Section 1: Foucault's Theory of History Across the Corpus

Foucault never presents his theory of history as a standing doctrine, defended once and then applied. It is instead enacted serially, across a sequence of studies that each redescribe, often retrospectively, what the preceding work had been doing. The result is a corpus that can be read either as a single project accreting vocabulary — archaeology, genealogy, history of the present — or as a set of discontinuous enterprises linked only by family resemblance. This section reconstructs that arc work by work, establishing the textual baseline against which later sections stage the debate over whether the shifts amount to rupture or continuous development.

### 1.1 The archaeological works and their historiographical claims

*History of Madness* (1961/2013) is the point of origin. Foucault there traces the exclusion and subsequent pathologization of "madness" in Western Europe from the Renaissance to the birth of psychiatry, arguing that the exclusion of unreason is constitutive of, rather than incidental to, Enlightenment reason (Foucault 2013). The book's method already works by attention to discontinuities and epistemic breaks, drawing on Bachelard's notion of epistemological rupture and Canguilhem's normal/pathological distinction rather than a continuous narrative of psychiatric progress (Foucault 2013). It is also the first text in which the word "archaeology" appears, in the original preface, though at this stage the term names a style rather than a fully worked-out method (Foucault 2013).

*The Birth of the Clinic* (1963/1973) tightens the program: Foucault proposes to analyze medical discourse "on its own terms," treating signifiers without reference to any fundamental reality they are taken to represent, and centers the analysis on the emergence of the clinical "gaze" as a historically specific, non-prejudicial mode of medical observation tied to new social and political demands (Foucault 1973). This is the first work to carry "archaeology" in its subtitle, marking the term's transition from an incidental label to a named method — although its stated discourse-only program is not yet fully matched by its actual historical analysis (Foucault 1973).

*The Order of Things* (1966/2005) is where archaeology becomes a fully deliberate methodology. Foucault argues that Western knowledge has passed through discrete "epistemes" — stable, largely unspoken ensembles of rules governing what can be known in a given period — separated by two major ruptures, one at the start of the Classical age and one at the threshold of modernity (Foucault 2005). Crucially, Foucault declines to explain why these ruptures occur, restricting archaeology to a description of what changed rather than a causal account of why it changed; the book's closing pages extend this discontinuist logic to anticipate the historically contingent "end of man" as an object of knowledge (Foucault 2005).

*The Archaeology of Knowledge* (1969/1972) then consolidates the method theoretically. It is Foucault's only major work that is not itself a historical study, analyzing discourse in terms of discrete "statements" and their relations independently of any subject who transcends or produces them, and introducing the "archive" as the system governing which statements can appear — archaeology is redefined here as the excavation of that archive (Foucault 1972). The text asserts the "relative autonomy" of discourse against both transcendental subjectivity and reductive social or economic determination, and retroactively reframes the method used in the three prior monographs (Foucault 1972). Together, these four works establish the vocabulary — episteme, discursive formation, statement, archive, historical a priori — and the refusal of causal-developmental explanation that the archaeology-as-method debate (Section 2) and the discontinuity debate (Section 5.3) take as their object.

### 1.2 The genealogical turn and power/knowledge

"Nietzsche, Genealogy, History" (1971/2019) supplies the manifesto for what follows. Adapting Nietzsche's vocabulary of *Ursprung* (origin), *Herkunft* (descent), and *Entstehung* (emergence), Foucault argues against the search for pure origins in history, proposing instead that genealogy trace the contingent, often violent "play of dominations" through which practices and values emerge, and calling for a *wirkliche Historie* — effective history — that abandons suprahistorical, continuous narratives in favor of disparity, chance, and the body as the surface on which history is inscribed (Foucault 2019). The essay functions as the primary methodological statement of Foucault's genealogical period and is the text later secondary literature on the Nietzsche relation (Section 3.1) and the archaeology–genealogy transition (Section 2.3) takes as its central object.

*Discipline and Punish* (1975/1977) puts this program to work on the emergence of the modern prison. Foucault argues that the prison functions structurally to produce criminality and recidivism, situating it within the broader historical emergence of "discipline" — a form of power first developed in seventeenth-century professional soldiering and subsequently generalized across the factory, the school, and the army to produce controllable "docile bodies" (Foucault 1977). Its historiographical innovation is the introduction of "power-knowledge" as a replacement for the Marxist category of ideology: knowledge is not distorted by power from outside but constitutively bound up with it (Foucault 1977). This is Foucault's first genealogical monograph and the primary text through which the archaeology/genealogy relationship becomes concrete rather than programmatic.

*The History of Sexuality, Volume 1* (1976/1978) extends this analysis into a fully elaborated theory of power. Against the "repressive hypothesis" that modernity has silenced discourse on sex, Foucault shows a proliferation of discourses compelling us to speak of it, and rejects a "juridical," monarchical model of power as prohibition in favor of a relational, productive, and strategic conception of power without a single locus or origin, introducing the concepts of biopower and biopolitics (Foucault 1978). *"Society Must Be Defended"* (1975–76/2003), the Collège de France lectures delivered in the same year as the writing of *Discipline and Punish*, offers a genealogy of the discourse of race war as an alternative to the sovereign/juridical model of politics, and contains what has been described as the clearest exposition of Foucault's thinking on biopower (Foucault 2003). As a lecture course contemporaneous with *Discipline and Punish* and preceding *The History of Sexuality*, it functions as a bridge document for tracing continuity or discontinuity in Foucault's historiographical self-understanding across the mid-1970s (Foucault 2003). Together this cluster feeds the transition debate (Section 2.3), the Nietzsche debate (Section 3.1), and the power/knowledge debate (Section 5.2).

### 1.3 "History of the present" as retrospective self-description

Foucault's latest self-characterization recasts the entire project in Kantian terms. In "What is Enlightenment?" (1984), written as a response to Kant's 1784 essay of the same title, Foucault describes his historiographical enterprise retrospectively as a "history of the present": an inquiry into "what we are" pursued not by searching for universal structures but through critical, historical analysis of the limits imposed on us, undertaken as a possible crossing-over of those limits, and proposes a "critical ontology of ourselves" as an ethos rather than a doctrine (Foucault 1984).

Two early secondary readings treat this self-description as evidence for continuity across the corpus rather than as a late addendum. Roth argues that in *The Birth of the Clinic*, *The Order of Things*, and *Discipline and Punish*, Foucault writes a "history of the present" in which archaeological description of past discursive formations is never separable from a critical, present-oriented political stance — a self-conscious field of power relations rather than a neutral reconstruction of the past (Roth 1981). Baert extends this claim further, arguing that Foucault's histories should be understood as a form of self-referential knowledge acquisition — interventions that are themselves constitutive of the present they analyze — and that this concern is common to both the archaeological and genealogical periods, giving the two "more in common than conventionally assumed" (Baert 1998). Roth and Baert thus function as early continuity theses, proposing that the history-of-the-present logic unifies archaeology and genealogy beneath their surface vocabulary.

Read across the whole corpus, this arc — from the incidental archaeology of *History of Madness* to the named method of *The Order of Things* and its theoretical self-clarification in *The Archaeology of Knowledge*, through the Nietzschean manifesto and its institutional applications in *Discipline and Punish*, *The History of Sexuality*, and the Collège de France lectures, to the late Kantian recasting as "history of the present" — establishes what Foucault said and did. It does not settle whether that arc is unified or ruptured. Foucault's own retrospective self-descriptions both raise this question and complicate it, since each new methodological label (archaeology, genealogy, history of the present) can be read either as naming a genuine break or as making explicit what was already latent in the earlier work. This continuity/rupture axis is the through-line that Sections 2 and 6 take up directly.

## Section 2: Archaeology and Genealogy as Philosophy of History and Method

Where Section 1 traced what Foucault said and did, this section asks what kind of historical knowledge archaeology and genealogy claim to produce, and how the two methods relate to one another. The second question — the relation between archaeology and genealogy — constitutes the pivotal methodological controversy of the review's first half.

### 2.1 Archaeology's coherence and the historical a priori

The interpretive baseline for archaeology as a mode of historical explanation is Gutting's (1989) reconstruction, which situates Foucault's archaeological studies of madness, clinical medicine, and the human sciences within the Bachelard–Canguilhem tradition of French historical epistemology rather than treating archaeology as a species of structuralism or a precursor to genealogy. This framing supplies the interpretive backdrop against which subsequent analytic and phenomenological readings react.

The earliest sustained Anglo-American analytic engagement with archaeology is Hacking's (1986) assessment of Foucault's account of discursive "positivities" and historical rule-structures, an essay whose influence is attested by its status as the direct target of Wartenberg's (2017) response. Wartenberg reconstructs Hacking's project as an attempt to render Foucault "palatable" to a reluctant analytic audience by locating his work on the existing analytic landscape, and assesses whether that assimilative strategy, and Rorty's related one, succeeds. This exchange sets the terms for a recurring question: whether archaeology can be captured by analytic categories at all, or whether — as Krarup (2021) argues — it must be understood within the specifically French "history of systems of thought" that Foucault inherited and reworked (via Dumézil's comparative-archaeological methodology) rather than as a freestanding position answerable to Anglo-American criteria.

A second line of attack targets archaeology's coherence directly. Dreyfus and Rabinow charge that archaeology rests on an incoherent conception of the rules governing discursive practices. Tiisala (2015) defends Foucault, arguing the criticism arises from misreading archaeology as a transcendental inquiry into conditions of possibility; archaeology is instead a "diagnostic" project that need not, and should not, render its governing rules explicit — a defense that recasts archaeology as description of historically contingent, implicit normative structures rather than a search for necessary conditions.

A third cluster addresses the "historical a priori," Foucault's term for the conditions of unity of a given field of science. Peters (2021) argues that "episteme" and "historical a priori" are frequently and wrongly conflated: episteme names the overall configuration of thought in a period, while the historical a priori names conditions internal to a specific science — a distinction that, on Peters's reading, brings Foucault closer to Husserl than usually appreciated. Basso (2012) develops this Husserlian lineage further, tracing the concept to the young Foucault's engagement with existential psychiatry and his 1954 introduction to Binswanger, arguing for methodological continuity between this early phenomenological phase and archaeology-as-historical-epistemology, and linking archaeology to Canguilhem's project of tying historicity to transcendentality. Hyder (2009) supplies a more specific version of the same lineage via Cavaillès's phenomenological archaeology of the sciences: Foucault and Cavaillès retain Husserl's technique of reactivating sedimented intentional acts while rejecting its appeal to consciousness. Flynn (2016) likewise reads the historical a priori as retaining a phenomenological debt to Husserl even as Foucault historicizes what Husserl treated as more nearly transcendental. Against this convergent Husserlian reading, Webb (2012) grounds the concept instead in Foucault's engagement with the "mathematical a priori" and Kant, tracing "temporal dispersion" to Foucault's critique of Kant via Cavaillès and Serres read as philosophers of mathematics rather than phenomenologists. Carr (2016) sharpens the comparative stakes by arguing that Husserl and Foucault use the identical phrase "historical a priori" to mean opposite things: for Husserl history has an intrinsic teleological structure, whereas for Foucault it must be understood in anti-teleological, discontinuist terms. This cluster shows the historical a priori functioning as a contested site: its Husserlian ancestry is widely acknowledged, but whether that ancestry commits Foucault to a teleological residue he elsewhere disavows remains open.

### 2.2 Genealogy as a mode of historical explanation

If archaeology's coherence is defended against an incoherence charge, genealogy is more often characterized positively as a distinctive mode of historical explanation. Bevir (2008) offers a general metahistorical account of genealogy as an expression of radical historicism: genealogies reject appeals to transcendental truths and to principles of unity or progress, embracing nominalism, contingency, and contestability, and operate as denaturalizing critiques of practices that conceal their own contingency behind ahistorical or developmental self-presentations.

Koopman (2013) develops a fuller account of genealogy as critique, arguing that Foucault's method involves not only diagnosing modernity's problems but transforming them practically, via the concept of "problematization": genealogy shows how a practice, concept, or norm was actually composed through complex historical vectors, equipping present actors with resources for reconstructing it differently. Koopman distinguishes this "problematizing" genealogy from Nietzsche's subversive genealogy and Bernard Williams's vindicatory genealogy — Foucault's version, unlike theirs, does not aim to establish or undermine specific normative conclusions, which also blunts the standard "genetic fallacy" objection.

Lichtenstein (2022) complicates the picture of genealogy as a purely philosophical enterprise. Most philosophical genealogists eschew historiographic methodology in favor of a purely philosophical approach; Foucault's early-1970s genealogy is unusual in borrowing from contemporary historiographic models of explanation, modifying two central Nietzschean commitments to expand the range of objects proper to genealogical accounts of change. On this reading, historical method carries normative weight for genealogy, since different historiographic choices yield different normative conclusions — integrating historiographic explanation and evaluative critique rather than treating genealogy as purely one or the other.

Garland (1986) tests these characterizations against genealogy as practiced in Discipline and Punish. He provides a systematic exposition of the book's account of the shift from sovereign to disciplinary power and its introduction of "power-knowledge," but subjects this account to critique, questioning both its evidentiary basis and the coherence of its claims about the functions of the prison — a sympathetic but critical assessment that raises, ahead of Section 4's discussion of historians' reception, whether genealogy's explanatory ambitions are matched by its historical rigor.

### 2.3 The archaeology–genealogy transition (structured debate)

The relationship between archaeology and genealogy is the pivotal methodological controversy of the review's first half. An older reading treats the shift from The Archaeology of Knowledge to "Nietzsche, Genealogy, History" and Discipline and Punish as a clean break: archaeology abandoned, genealogy substituted in its place. The literature surveyed here disputes this "abandonment" reading from at least four directions, without converging on a single alternative.

The **expansion** position, developed by Koopman (2008), holds that the shift is better conceived as a historiographical expansion than a replacement: Foucault moves from analyzing singular, isolable domains of practice (knowledge alone) to analyzing interactions among multiple domains (power/knowledge), and from an analytic relying on a single temporal category of rupture to one invoking multiple temporalities, including continuity alongside discontinuity. Genealogy, on this reading, retains and builds upon archaeology rather than superseding it.

The **continuity/self-reference** position is advanced by Baert (1998), who argues that Foucault's "history of the present" is a form of self-referential knowledge acquisition — histories that are not neutral reconstructions of the past but interventions constitutive of the present they analyze. Baert traces this concern across Foucault's career and argues that archaeology and genealogy "have more in common than conventionally assumed" because both are directed toward this same self-referential form of knowledge, a claim in productive tension with Koopman's expansion thesis insofar as it locates continuity at the level of epistemic function rather than analytic scope.

The **supplementation** position belongs to Thompson (2016), who argues, against readings that treat genealogy as archaeology's replacement, that genealogy is best construed as a supplementary addition to archaeological investigation. Thompson traces the movement from the "historical a priori" to the later concept of the "dispositif" through the problem of transcendental genesis inherited from the phenomenological tradition, arguing that the dispositif absorbs and extends the historical a priori rather than discarding it — resolving a problem that the historical a priori alone could not.

The **intertwinement** position is represented by two studies of transitional vocabulary and practice. Elden (2025) traces Foucault's use of the term "dynastics" (from dunamis/dunasteia) in lectures and one interview from the early 1970s — after his explicit engagement with Nietzsche in "Nietzsche, Genealogy, History" but before he settled on "genealogy" — as a complementary approach to archaeology relating to both power and knowledge; even after Foucault drops the term, Elden argues, dynastics continues to inform how he subsequently writes about power relations and lineage, refining the standard two-stage narrative with an intermediate, Nietzsche-adjacent vocabulary. T'Jampens and Versieren (2020) offer a complementary case study at the level of practice: in Society Must Be Defended, Foucault deploys the method of "critical archaeology" upon genealogy itself, producing a descriptive discursive history of his own genealogical project in order to historicize and criticize Nietzsche's equation of the social with struggle — a discovery that led Foucault to distance his concept of struggle from Nietzsche's and to rethink the social within the framework of gouvernementalité. On this reading, archaeology and genealogy are methodologically intertwined — used jointly on the same material — rather than sequential or discrete stages.

These four positions are not straightforwardly reconcilable. Expansion (Koopman) and supplementation (Thompson) both reject the abandonment reading but locate the relationship differently — added domains and temporalities versus a resolved transcendental problem. Continuity/self-reference (Baert) locates the unity beneath both terms, in shared epistemic function, rather than in a directional relation between them. Intertwinement (Elden; T'Jampens and Versieren) resists periodization altogether, treating the vocabulary and technique of the two methods as overlapping in Foucault's actual practice during the very years the "shift" is supposed to have occurred. What unites all four is only their shared rejection of a simple abandonment narrative; how, precisely, to characterize the positive relationship between archaeology and genealogy remains unsettled in the literature, and this unsettled status recurs as the throughline connecting Section 1's corpus-wide continuity/rupture question to the discontinuity debate taken up in Section 5.3.

Archaeology's coherence is defended — against the Dreyfus/Rabinow incoherence charge (Tiisala) and situated within both analytic (Hacking, Wartenberg) and French disciplinary (Krarup) contexts — even as its central category, the historical a priori, remains contested between Husserlian and Kantian-mathematical genealogies. Genealogy is characterized across converging accounts (Bevir, Koopman, Lichtenstein) as a distinctive, transformative, historiographically engaged mode of critique, though its application in Discipline and Punish draws Garland's pointed methodological criticism. And the relationship between the two methods — expansion, continuity, supplementation, or intertwinement — remains an open question that later sections of this review return to repeatedly.

## Section 3: Intellectual Relations and Sources

**Section Purpose**: Situate Foucault's historical thought among its formative debts and polemics, clarifying what is inherited versus novel. Foreground each relation as a debate about *fidelity and departure*.

Foucault's historiographical vocabulary did not emerge from nothing, and in every major case the secondary literature divides over whether he faithfully extends a source or strategically departs from it. This section stages four such disputes — Nietzsche, Hegel, French historical epistemology, and the Annales/structuralist/Marxist cluster — as live disagreements rather than settled pedigrees.

### 3.1 Nietzsche: how Nietzschean is Foucauldian genealogy? (debate)

No relation is more contested than Foucault's debt to Nietzsche, and the recent literature divides into four positions. A strong-continuity reading comes from Garruzzo (2025), who argues that genuine genealogy — as distinct from the looser normatively-loaded uses of the term in contemporary social philosophy — inherits Nietzsche's "will to power" wholesale: it investigates the contingent integration of an institution within a regime of power, and its critical payoff is not normative insight but "tactical knowledge" of how power has been exercised and resisted. Testa (2025) offers a more archival continuity thesis, recovering Foucault's 1969–1970 Vincennes lectures on Nietzsche to show that, alongside the familiar diagnostic register of genealogy, Foucault developed a medical-therapeutic one in which the "historical sense" functions as both poison and cure — material predating and refining the canonical 1971 essay "Nietzsche, Genealogy, History."

Against these stand two critical readings arguing Foucault departs from, or misreads, his source. Rockhill (2020) challenges the essay's foundational textual move: the Herkunft/Ursprung distinction Foucault presents as "properly Nietzschean" does not, he argues, actually exist in Nietzsche's text, making Foucault's appropriation a strategic invention rather than an exposition — leading Rockhill to propose an alternative "counter-history" beyond genealogy. Manley (2025) mounts a parallel but independent challenge via Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation: where Foucault presents an anti-teleological Nietzsche suspicious of origins, Nietzsche's own essay insists on the necessity of ahistorical, quasi-teleological figures against corrosive historicism, making Foucault's Nietzsche one-sided and Nietzsche himself "an alternative — rather than a precursor — to the Foucauldian paradigm." Finally, Karlsen and Villadsen (2018) complicate the picture from another angle, arguing that Foucault's involvement in the Maoist-inspired Groupe d'information sur les prisons left identifiable marks on the genealogical method and the power-knowledge vocabulary of the 1976 lectures, making Nietzsche not genealogy's sole dialogue partner. What remains unresolved is thus layered: whether genealogy extends Nietzsche's will-to-power framework, selectively (mis)appropriates his vocabulary, or answers to formative pressures partly external to Nietzsche altogether.

### 3.2 Hegel, the dialectic, and teleology

The standard characterization of Foucault as anti-Hegelian and anti-teleological is developed from two directions. Carr (2016) contrasts Husserl's and Foucault's shared use of "the historical a priori" to show they mean fundamentally different things: Husserl's presupposes an intrinsic teleological structure to history, Foucault's is explicitly anti-teleological and discontinuist (this entry carries an INCOMPLETE flag but a substantive note field, and is cited cautiously on that basis). Olssen (2004) reaches a structurally similar conclusion via Marxism rather than phenomenology: despite parallels between Foucault's method and Marxism as accounts of history and social structure, Foucault's decisive break is his rejection of Hegel's conception of history as a "unified developing totality," together with essences, teleology, and any utopian trajectory tied to laws of economic development.

Giuliani (2021) complicates this consensus at its source with a continuity thesis. Reading History of Madness (1961) against Hegel's Phenomenology of Spirit, she argues Foucault's configuration of madness across the classical and modern age traces a path toward self-awareness for Western rationality, and that the encounter between poverty and unreason in the hôpital, and in Pinel's asile, can be read as figures of dialectical movement — locating a speculative-dialectical structure inside Foucault's earliest major work. The tension is left open: the anti-Hegelian self-understanding archaeology and genealogy later claim (Carr, Olssen) sits uneasily with Giuliani's textual case that Foucault's first historical study retains a Hegelian speculative dynamic.

### 3.3 French historical epistemology: Bachelard and Canguilhem

Here the dispute concerns not whether Foucault inherits from Bachelard and Canguilhem but precisely what. Peña-Guzmán (2020) frames the tradition — Cavaillès, Bachelard, Canguilhem, Foucault — as unified by two commitments: scientific discourse, not theory or practice, is epistemology's proper object, and concepts are the fundamental units of that discourse, positioning archaeology as continuous with a discourse-and-concept-centered program. Chimisso (2003) narrows to Canguilhem's "normative history" of concepts and its relation to the normal/pathological distinction; though critical of the external philosophical norms this smuggles into history-writing, she concludes Canguilhem's practice of analyzing concepts "from below" offers a generalizable model against which Foucault's own explicitly anti-normative, discontinuist archaeology can be measured. Castelli Gattinara (2018) supplies the connective genealogy directly: Canguilhem radicalizes Bachelard by recognizing the historicity of every aspect of scientific knowledge, including its errors, and explicitly identifies this radicalized, pluralist historicization as opening "the way to the researches of Foucault and [Michel] Serres."

The stakes are sharpened by D'Cruz (2024), who argues Foucault retains a substantive naturalistic commitment traceable to this tradition: his normative epistemological judgments, especially about the human sciences, depend on a comparative respect for the natural sciences, and the descriptive rigor of genealogy derives from its being modeled on empirical inquiry. This reading pushes back against interpretations that take Foucault to reduce knowledge to power and collapse into unconstrained relativism, positioning the Canguilhem inheritance as a resource against the relativism charge taken up later in the review (5.1).

### 3.4 Annales, structuralism, and Marxism

Three overlapping affiliation disputes converge here. On Annales, Gürkan (2017) treats Braudel and Foucault as engaged in direct textual dialogue rather than loose resemblance: their treatments of structure and event are best grasped through each thinker's own apparatus — Braudel's tripartite schema of economic civilization for the history of capitalism, Foucault's analytics of power and government for (neo)liberalism — and Gürkan works toward integrating rather than opposing the two.

On structuralism, whether Foucault's discontinuist archaeology is itself a structuralist historical method is answered affirmatively by Bryanik (2025), who argues against the assumption that structuralism is anti-historicist: it combines synchronic analysis of internal relations with diachronic analysis organized around discontinuity and "mutation," with Foucault's archaeologist — like Propp's folklorist or a geologist — excavating strata separated by ruptures. Ryder (2013) approaches the same question through Foucault's relation to Althusser specifically, arguing that Foucault's rejection of Marxism in The Order of Things targeted a humanist, historicist Marx while conceding a more compelling non-humanist Marxism for which Althusser's structuralist innovations were crucial — an epistemological divergence rather than a simple rejection.

Marxism proper is treated as disaggregated rather than uniformly hostile. Olssen (2004) describes the relation as "rewriting rather than rejection": Foucault shares Marxism's treatment of knowledge as linked to power and its assertion of historical relativity while rejecting Hegelian totality, essences, teleology, and revolutionary utopianism in favor of localized resistance. Choat (2010) extends this non-rejectionist reading comparatively, situating Foucault's "history of the present" alongside Lyotard's, Derrida's, and Deleuze's own reworkings of Marx as one instance of a broader post-structuralist pattern of engagement rather than dismissal. Sartori (2020) tests the limits of that engagement directly: reading Foucault's genealogical critique of nineteenth-century historicism — which had already, in The Order of Things, consigned Marx to an episteme organized around "historicity" and "the human essence" — against Moishe Postone's postfoundationalist Marx, Sartori argues genealogy's critical purchase does not actually extend to Marx's own categorial analysis of social form, which remains available as a resource for critique immanent to social form rather than reliant on historicist assumptions. Riep (2025), drawing on unpublished 1972 Buffalo lecture recordings, supplies archival confirmation that Foucault's critical distance from Althusserian "scientific Marxism" was explicit and articulated at the transitional moment between archaeology and genealogy, not merely retrospective. Together these accounts leave open whether Foucault's historiography is best classed as a variant of structuralism, a selective heir to non-humanist Marxism, or a method whose critique of historicism, forceful against nineteenth-century teleology, fails to fully reach Marx's own theoretical core.

**Section Summary**: Foucault's sources are matters of active dispute; in every case (Nietzsche, Hegel, Canguilhem, Marx, structuralism) the literature splits between continuity and departure readings.

## Section 4: Historians' Reception and Empirical Critique

Where Sections 1–3 reconstructed Foucault's historiographical self-descriptions and intellectual debts largely on their own terms, professional historians confronting the same texts have not always recognized the enterprise as history at all. This section turns to that disconfirming, discipline-internal perspective: what happens when specialists in madness, medicine, and the human sciences check Foucault's claims against the documentary record, and how the resulting disputes have been prosecuted, reframed as a matter of genre, or absorbed through selective revision. The pattern is not a simple verdict but a conversation split along the fault line Sections 1–3 leave open: is archaeology/genealogy usable historical method, or philosophy borrowing history's evidentiary trappings without submitting to its norms?

### 4.1 The empirical prosecution

Windschuttle (1998) mounts the harshest case, arguing that across *Madness and Civilization*, *Discipline and Punish*, and *The History of Sexuality* Foucault repeatedly misrepresented, selectively quoted, or invented evidence to fit a predetermined thesis about power and exclusion, and that historians who adopted Foucauldian method imported these errors uncritically. His objections are itemized — chronology of confinement, treatment of sources, selective citation — and explicitly deny Foucault standing as a historian.

Scull (1990), a historian of psychiatry, argues that Foucault's account of a unitary "Great Confinement" and his opposition of madness to reason do not survive confrontation with archival and comparative evidence historians of the asylum have since assembled, though he credits the book's rhetorical force with helping create the field of the history of psychiatry as it now exists — a mixed verdict rather than wholesale dismissal.

A parallel prosecution targets the discontinuity thesis itself. Maclean (1998), a historian of Renaissance thought, argues that Foucault's characterization of Renaissance knowledge as governed by resemblance and analogy neglects the substantial Aristotelian logical apparatus actually deployed by Renaissance thinkers, making Foucault's claim of a sharp discontinuity between the Renaissance and Classical epistemes historically inaccurate — testing, from within the archival record, whether one of Foucault's paradigmatic ruptures survives scrutiny (a question resumed formally in Section 5.3).

Wilson (2007) reconstructs the historian Roy Porter's empirical and methodological objections to *The Birth of the Clinic*, contrasting Porter's patient-centered social history of medicine with Foucault's structuralist account of the "clinical gaze," while asking which aspects of the disagreement are genuinely empirical and which reflect differing conceptions of historical explanation — a distinction anticipating the genre-based reframing below.

### 4.2 Genre-reclassification and defenses

A second cluster treats the accuracy dispute partly as a category error. Megill (1990) supplies the framework most cited in this reception debate: Foucault's histories are constitutively ambiguous between empirical historiography and a rhetorically driven, philosophically motivated genre, and this ambiguity is a structural feature of the texts rather than a defect. On his account, historians and philosophers disputing Foucault's "accuracy" are implicitly applying different genre expectations to the same texts.

White (1973), in *History and Theory*, reads *The Order of Things* as "anti-history" that deliberately eschews the connective, developmental emplotment of conventional historiography, arguing that Foucault's archaeological method should be analyzed through literary and rhetorical categories rather than judged by evidentiary standards proper to empirical history (a reading resumed in Section 6.1's placement of Foucault relative to narrativist philosophy of history).

Han-Pile (2005) asks directly "is early Foucault a historian?," arguing that early attacks by historians of ideas charging inaccuracy misconstrue a project never primarily aimed at empirical reconstruction but at a transcendental-historical diagnosis of the conditions of possibility for the human sciences — the "analytic of finitude." She notes that historians of ideas "immediately attacked" Foucault for alleged inaccuracy upon *The Order of Things*' publication, making her essay a hinge between the philosophical and historiographical literatures.

Alford (2000) poses the stakes most pointedly for *Discipline and Punish*, asking what would remain of its apparatus — panopticism, disciplinary power, the carceral continuum — if its narrative about the birth of the prison were wrong. He concludes that much of the book's influence rests on its diagnostic power over the present rather than the accuracy of its genealogy, granting empirical vulnerability while defending residual theoretical value.

LaCapra (1990), an intellectual historian, similarly defends the history of madness against purely empiricist objections, arguing the text operates simultaneously as documentary history and as a self-consciously "excessive" or transgressive form of writing that deliberately unsettles historiographical protocols, so judging it solely by archival-accuracy criteria misses this dual character. (The bibliographic record truncates the author's given name to "Dominick L."; a corroborating institutional record is consistent with the intellectual historian Dominick LaCapra, but the attribution is noted here rather than silently resolved.) The essay appeared in the same 1990 special issue as Scull's prosecution, illustrating a split among historians themselves over whether the history of madness should be judged as history proper or as a hybrid historical-philosophical genre.

### 4.3 Selective appropriation and disciplinary uptake

A third response neither prosecutes nor reclassifies but revises. Weymans (2009) reconstructs how the French historians Marcel Gauchet and Gladys Swain revised Foucault's exclusion thesis from within the same archival territory — the birth of the asylum — arguing that modern psychiatric practice represented a new form of recognition of the mad rather than confinement, and that they saw resistance where Foucault saw only the panoptic asylum's success. Their critique of contemporary societies, Weymans argues, nonetheless remains indebted to Foucault's own problematization of madness and modernity — historians working inside Foucault's field who revise rather than reject his narrative.

Disciplinary self-reflection supplies a further register. Surkis (2012), in *The American Historical Review*, historicizes the profession's own narrative about a "linguistic turn" in which Foucauldian method was credited with (or blamed for) transforming Anglo-American historiography in the 1980s–90s, arguing this standard genealogy oversimplifies a more heterogeneous process of disciplinary uptake. Forrester (1980), writing for a history-of-science audience, assesses *The Order of Things*' treatment of psychoanalysis as a "counter-science," probing whether Foucault's reconstruction of the episteme underlying the human sciences is defensible as history of science or a philosophically motivated schema imposed on the material, arriving at a cautious appraisal that neither wholly rejects nor uncritically adopts Foucault's account.

The debate has also been staged in collective forums. Goldstein (1994) edits a volume assessing, essay by essay, how Foucault's histories of madness, sexuality, and the human sciences hold up against historiographical norms — spanning empirical rejection, rhetorical reclassification, and selective appropriation, and including Megill's essay. Still and Velody (1992) assemble a narrower forum on *Histoire de la folie* specifically, revisiting confinement and the classical age in light of subsequent archival research and situating the English-language controversy within its French context. Earle (2007), writing after the 2006 unabridged translation of *History of Madness*, synthesizes the accumulated critical "reaction-shots" — overstated unity of the classical age, selective sources, romanticized unreason — while asking what remains philosophically valuable once these objections are granted, largely endorsing the genre-reclassification view while taking the empirical objections seriously.

This body of reception resists a binary appropriation/rejection framing: it spans empirical rejection (Windschuttle, Scull, Maclean, Porter via Wilson), genre-reclassification (Megill, White, Han-Pile, Alford, LaCapra), and selective revision (Weymans, Surkis, Forrester). The deepest diagnosis of why these positions so rarely engage one another directly remains Megill's: the dispute over Foucault's "accuracy" is not simply a dispute over facts but a disagreement about which genre — empirical historiography or rhetorically driven philosophical history — the disputants take themselves to be evaluating. Historians applying archival standards and philosophers reading Foucault's texts as diagnostic or transcendental are, on this account, applying incompatible genre expectations to the same texts.

## Section 5: The Core Philosophical Debates

This section concentrates the free-standing philosophical objections and defenses that bear on Foucault's historiography as a general epistemological and political project, independent of any particular archival claim. Four controversies recur: whether power/knowledge collapses into self-undermining relativism; whether Foucault's genealogies smuggle in normative commitments his own premises forbid, staged most fully against Habermas; whether discontinuity and the episteme are coherent units of historical explanation; and whether a subject dissolved into anonymous discursive formations can still ground resistance or judgment. These debates are not independent: the relativism and normativity disputes share the self-refutation structure, and the subject debate supplies a resource — subjectivation, critique as self-practice — that several respondents use to answer both.

### 5.1 Relativism, anti-realism, and self-refutation

Taylor's founding statement of the self-refutation charge argues that because Foucault denies any standpoint of truth or freedom external to power/knowledge formations, his genealogies of madness, sexuality, and confession cannot explain why liberation from one discursive regime should count as progress rather than mere substitution of one power-truth configuration for another, leaving Foucault's implicit valorization of resistance without a defensible basis (Taylor 1984). Nola mounts a parallel, more technical prosecution from analytic philosophy of science: Foucault's "Primacy Thesis" — the causal priority of power over knowledge — equivocates between senses of power and fails either as a general explanatory theory of scientific belief or as a coherent alternative to realist accounts of truth, so that insofar as Foucault wants to criticize beliefs circulating as effects of power, he needs the traditional account of truth he officially disavows (Nola 1994).

Two responses contest the terms of this charge rather than concede it. Han-Pile distinguishes "how possible" questions about the enabling conditions of knowledge from "whether permissible" questions about legitimacy, and argues Foucault's most defensible reply to Taylor and Habermas is not to supply the demanded normative foundations but to refuse the demand as question-begging, replacing justification with critique as a "performative practice of the self" (Han-Pile 2016). Tremain instead adopts Foucauldian historicist relativism affirmatively: her feminist philosophy of disability uses genealogy precisely because it avoids the ahistorical, teleological, and transcultural assumptions she finds elsewhere in disability theory, treating the refusal of ahistorical essences as an advantage rather than a liability (Tremain 2015). D'Cruz supplies a third rejoinder, internal to the historiographical tradition Foucault inherits from Canguilhem: rereading genealogy as modeled on empirical inquiry and grounded in Foucault's continued respect for the natural sciences, D'Cruz argues the reduction of knowledge to power rests on a misreading of Foucault's project as a normative epistemology rather than a historico-critical one (D'Cruz 2024).

What remains unresolved is whether these responses answer Taylor and Nola or merely relocate the disagreement. Han-Pile and Tremain both accept that Foucault offers no independent normative foundation and argue this is not a defect, while D'Cruz denies the practice requires one; none produces the external validation Taylor and Nola take to be minimally required for critique to retain force.

### 5.2 Power/knowledge, normativity, and the Foucault–Habermas debate

Fraser's normative-deficit objection applies the self-refutation worry directly to power/knowledge: Foucault's genealogies characterize power with evaluative terms — domination, subjugation, struggle — while insisting power is everywhere, value-neutral, and without an outside, an equivocation that lets Foucault imply political critique of disciplinary institutions without supplying, or being able to supply, the normative criteria that would justify calling disciplinary power domination rather than simply power (Fraser 1985). No citable stand-alone article by Habermas directly engaging Foucault could be resolved for this review; his position is represented here only through the secondary literature. Freundlieb reconstructs Habermas's charge that Foucault belongs to a lineage of "totalizing" critiques of reason — alongside Nietzsche and Heidegger — trapped in performative self-contradiction, since Foucault's genealogies presuppose the rational, truth-oriented standards that power/knowledge relativizes; Freundlieb notes that Habermas's own defense of rationalism, via knowledge-guiding interests, commits him to a comparable anti-realism (Freundlieb 1988).

A mediating cluster resists treating the exchange as flat opposition. Ingram compares the two thinkers' styles of critical theory, arguing Foucault's virtue-ethical aesthetic self-realization is a genuine counterpoint to Habermas's juridical discourse ethics rather than an inferior alternative, with parrhesia as a non-discursive mode of truth-telling resisting assimilation to communicative rationality (Ingram 2005). King argues responses to Habermas have suffered from imprecision about what "normative foundations" means, concluding that while Foucault's political judgments genuinely lack normative foundations in Habermas's strict sense, Foucault nonetheless adheres to the procedural principles of discourse ethics without conflict with his genealogical method (King 2009). Biebricher pushes further, arguing Habermas misconstrues genealogy by assuming it aims at scientific, truth-claiming reconstruction, when genealogy is better read as a rhetorically hybrid mode between science and literature — tracing the misreading to Habermas's reconstruction of Nietzsche and concluding that rhetorically understood genealogy counts as valid even on Habermas's own terms (Biebricher 2005). Allen relocates the debate onto subjectivation — how individuals become subjects — arguing the two frameworks are not too disparate to integrate, and beginning to use Foucauldian genealogy to supplement Habermasian communicative-action theory's account of discursive subject-formation (Allen 2009).

What remains unresolved is whether the reconciling readings dissolve the dispute or redescribe it as genre mismatch. King and Biebricher converge on the diagnosis that much of the apparent conflict stems from imprecision about normative foundations and genre, but neither shows that Fraser's underlying worry — that Foucault's evaluative vocabulary needs grounding it structurally cannot supply — is answered rather than reclassified.

### 5.3 Discontinuity and the status of the episteme

The discontinuity thesis faces an external historical challenge and an internal formal one. Maclean's reassessment of Foucault's Renaissance episteme argues that Foucault's characterization of Renaissance knowledge as governed by resemblance and analogy neglects the Aristotelian logical apparatus actually deployed by Renaissance thinkers, making his periodization and his claimed sharp break between the Renaissance and Classical epistemes historically inaccurate (Maclean 1998). Cutrofello complements this with an internal critique, examining the quasi-structuralist "table" organizing the classical episteme — the grid relating general grammar, natural history, and the analysis of wealth — and asking whether it is complete in its own terms or harbors gaps that complicate Foucault's claim to a single, internally coherent configuration of classical knowledge (Cutrofello 2003).

Two comparative papers clarify rather than directly refute the historical a priori underwriting discontinuity. Carr contrasts Husserl's and Foucault's shared use of the phrase "the historical a priori," arguing the difference tracks a deeper disagreement over whether history has an intrinsic teleological structure — Husserl affirming it, Foucault developing an explicitly anti-teleological, discontinuist alternative (Carr 2016). Vaccarino Bremner situates Foucault's historical a priori within the lineage of "relativized a priori" theorizing running from logical empiricism through Cassirer's symbolic form, arguing Foucault's version is more thoroughly relativized than Cassirer's but that it admits of internal tensions accounting for transitions to a new a priori, with social power playing a role more fully developed than analogous resources in contemporary philosophy of science (Vaccarino Bremner 2025). This reframes discontinuity as one instance of a family of strategies reconciling historically variable frameworks with the a priori's claim to necessity, rather than a sui generis relativism.

What remains unresolved, and is a flagged gap in the literature, is a systematic realist/anti-realist treatment of Foucauldian discontinuity comparable to Kuhn-paradigm debates; existing engagement splits between Maclean's and Cutrofello's targeted critiques of specific epistemes and the largely comparative, expository work of Carr and Vaccarino Bremner, leaving the general ontological status of an episteme unsettled.

### 5.4 The decentering of the subject

Canguilhem — writing as Foucault's own teacher and examiner — supplies the authoritative gloss on the "death of man" announced in The Order of Things: not the biological extinction of humanity, but the historically recent and contingent emergence of "man" as object and subject of knowledge within the modern episteme, and hence the possible dissolution of that configuration. He situates this within Foucault's structuralist displacement of the transcendental, constituting Cartesian cogito by anonymous discursive formations (Canguilhem 2005). The implication is that historical explanation must proceed without a subject standing as the origin of history — one of the four commitments of traditional philosophy of history that Foucault sets out to dismantle.

This raises a problem shared with the normativity debate: can a decentered subject still be an agent of resistance or judgment? Han-Pile's answer, introduced in 5.1, is that critique survives not as foundational justification but as a "performative practice of the self" (Han-Pile 2016). Allen's account of subjectivation, introduced in 5.2, gives a structurally similar answer from the Habermasian side: subjects are formed through discursive and power-laden processes that genealogy and communicative-action theory can jointly illuminate, making subjectivation the pivot at which the decentered subject and the demand for critical agency might be reconciled rather than treated as flatly incompatible (Allen 2009).

What remains unresolved is whether "critique as a practice of the self" and "subjectivation" answer the agency question or relabel it: both trade a constituting subject for a subject constituted through practices that are themselves candidates for the genealogical unmasking Foucault applies to earlier configurations of "man," leaving open whether the resulting account of resistance is stable or defers the problem Canguilhem identifies to a further historical regress.

These four controversies are tightly intertwined: the relativism and Habermas debates share the self-refutation structure, with Han-Pile's and Biebricher's diagnoses that the demand for foundations is itself part of the dispute recurring across both; the discontinuity debate supplies the historical-a-priori vocabulary that Han-Pile and D'Cruz's naturalist reading draw on to characterize Foucauldian normativity; and the subject debate closes the loop by asking who is left to resist once truth and the subject have both been historicized. Critics and defenders remain squarely opposed throughout, though several disputes — most explicitly Foucault–Habermas — are diagnosed in the literature itself as resting on mutual misreading or imprecise framing rather than a substantive incompatibility a single further argument could resolve.

## Section 6: Legacy and Placement in the Philosophy of History

Foucault's theory of history did not remain a self-contained episode in French intellectual life; it became a durable reference point across historiographical theory, critical theory, and political philosophy. Yet the exact nature of that legacy is itself contested. This section traces three afterlives — Foucault's placement relative to the canonical philosophy-of-history tradition, the "history of the present" as an ongoing research program, and genealogy's diffusion as a still-disputed mode of critique — and shows that in each case the question of what kind of enterprise Foucault bequeathed remains structurally open.

### 6.1 Placement relative to narrativist and analytic philosophy of history

The most sustained attempt to locate Foucault's practice of history within the wider landscape of twentieth-century theories of historical reason is Thomas Flynn's two-volume *Sartre, Foucault, and Historical Reason*. The first volume reconstructs Sartre's *Critique of Dialectical Reason* as an existentialist theory of history grounded in individual and group praxis (Flynn 1997), establishing the baseline against which the second volume reads Foucault's archaeology and genealogy as a "poststructuralist mapping of history" — a nominalist, spatialized, cartographic enterprise that displaces Sartre's subject-centered dialectic while still addressing the same underlying question of what makes history intelligible (Flynn 2005). On this reading, Foucault's histories should not be assimilated to Hayden White's narrativist model of emplotment (a genre-question already introduced through White's own "anti-history" reading of *The Order of Things*; White 1973); rather, Foucault substitutes mapping for narrative, seeking to "render history intelligible without appeal to traditional concepts of evolution, development, or even influence." Flynn's "axial" interpretation — charting Foucault's career along three axes of knowledge-truth, power-governmentality, and subjectification-ethics rather than as a linear development — is offered as a complement to, rather than a replacement for, developmental readings, and it leaves unresolved whether the ethical-subjectivation axis is merely one option among three or the culminating concern that retrospectively unifies Foucault's oeuvre. This tension over the status of the ethical subject within an ostensibly anti-humanist history recurs as an unresolved seam in Flynn's reconstruction rather than a settled result.

A second placement exercise approaches Foucault from the analytic and political-theory side of philosophy of history rather than from narrativist theory. The 2024 *Journal of the Philosophy of History* special issue pairs Foucault with Hannah Arendt, using her as a comparison case internal to twentieth-century political philosophy to ask what "doing genealogy" as history actually requires. Owen (2024) argues that Arendt and Foucault can both be read as practicing genealogy, tracing their shared background in Heidegger and Nietzsche and a common dissatisfaction with the lack of historical groundedness in Heidegger's own philosophy, and stages this convergence as a basis for comparing their diagnoses of political modernity — treating the two thinkers as convergent rather than methodologically opposed practitioners of historically grounded critique. Neill's introduction to the special issue frames Arendt and Foucault as jointly posing a challenge to mainstream philosophy of history, given their shared resistance to standard categories of historical explanation (Neill 2024). The issue's placement of Foucault within a mainstream philosophy-of-history venue, alongside a canonical political philosopher, marks one of the clearer institutional signals of his reception within that field specifically, as opposed to critical theory or history-of-science venues.

What is comparatively thin in this literature is direct, sustained comparison with Frank Ankersmit's narrativist theory of history; most comparative work pairs Foucault with White or with Arendt rather than with Ankersmit, leaving the Foucault–Ankersmit relation an underexplored pairing in the discoverable literature.

### 6.2 "History of the present" as a historiographical program

Foucault's own retrospective self-description of his project as a "history of the present" — introduced in Section 1.3 via Roth's early continuity thesis that archaeology and genealogy share a single logic connecting historical description to present critique (Roth 1981) — has since been reactivated as an active, extendable research method rather than treated as a settled slogan. The edited collection *Foucault and the History of Our Present* tests Foucault's claim that the history of the present should be the starting point for historical enquiry against contemporary domains including freedom, security, war, migration, incarceration, and digital media, asking what it means to write histories of "multiple presents" rather than a single continuous one (Fuggle, Lanci, and Tazzioli 2015). The volume's contributors extend rather than philosophically defend the method, demonstrating its continued viability through uptake across disciplines roughly forty years after Foucault articulated it.

Irrera's more recent archival study deepens this continuity claim by tracing the concept of *actualité* — the "ontology of the present" of Foucault's late lectures — back to his newly published *Le Discours philosophique* and his reflections on the status of philosophical discourse from the mid-1960s onward (Irrera 2024). On this reconstruction, *actualité* was already shaping an archaeological framework for treating philosophy itself as an object of historicization well before the explicitly genealogical "history of the present" texts, supporting a continuity reading against interpretations that treat the history of the present as a late, discontinuous turn — and showing that the history-of-the-present legacy continues to generate new interpretive work as previously unpublished material becomes available.

Hartog's *Regimes of Historicity*, though not a study of Foucault, supplies the wider historiographical-theoretical vocabulary against which Foucault's presentism is now routinely read: a "presentist" regime in which past and future are absorbed into an unending present, following an earlier future-oriented "modern" regime that dominated from the French Revolution to roughly 1989 (Hartog 2015). Read alongside Fuggle et al. and Irrera, Hartog's regimes-of-historicity vocabulary clarifies what is distinctive, and what is not, about Foucault's own history-of-the-present method, situating it within a broader epochal diagnosis of presentism developed independently of Foucauldian scholarship.

### 6.3 Genealogy as critique: the live legacy debate

The third and most contested afterlife concerns whether genealogy, as a mode of tracing contingent historical emergence, constitutes critique at all. Geuss poses the foundational problem: does tracing a concept's, practice's, or institution's contingent historical emergence automatically undermine its authority, or does genealogy require additional normative argument to carry debunking force (Geuss 2002)? Geuss's skeptical, clarificatory position — that genealogy alone may not suffice as critique — functions as the foil against which subsequent, more affirmative reconstructions of genealogy's critical power are framed.

Koopman and Matza diagnose how genealogy has actually traveled across the humanities and social sciences since Foucault, distinguishing between importing his substantive concepts (power, discipline, biopolitics) and genuinely practicing his analytics (the methodological orientations of archaeology, genealogy, and problematization); they argue scholars have far more often adopted the former, producing diminishing methodological returns, and urge a return to analytic practice over conceptual borrowing (Koopman and Matza 2013). This concept-versus-analytic diagnosis of superficial uptake echoes, at the level of reception, the genre-ambiguity problem Megill identifies in Section 4.2 regarding Foucault's own texts: just as historians and philosophers can apply incompatible genre expectations to the same primary texts, later appropriators can adopt Foucauldian vocabulary while bypassing the method that vocabulary was meant to enact.

The normativity pressure this raises is pressed directly against Koopman's own affirmative reconstruction of genealogy as a transformative, problematizing method of critique (Koopman 2013; introduced in Section 2.2). Allen's commentary asks whether that account can secure genuinely normative purchase without covertly relying on the transcendental or quasi-foundational commitments genealogy is supposed to avoid (Allen 2014) — an internal critical dialogue that presses the normativity objection against an affirmative reconstruction rather than resolving it. Harcourt's more recent intervention responds to the resulting proliferation of "genealogy" across critical theory by proposing that the term "critical genealogy" be reserved for genealogical practices that positively contribute to transforming ourselves, others, and society, evaluated by that transformative criterion rather than by formal method alone (Harcourt 2024) — a reformist move that implicitly concedes not every genealogical study advances the goals of critical philosophy.

Genealogy's geographic and disciplinary travel is tested directly by Nigh and Erlenbusch-Anderson, who examine Colombian philosopher Santiago Castro-Gómez's systematic use of Foucauldian inquiry for decolonial study and find that applying the method beyond its European site of articulation requires significant revision of Foucauldian concepts, categories, and methods rather than straightforward transplantation (Nigh and Erlenbusch-Anderson 2020) — affirming genealogy's productive extension into decolonial theory while insisting the transfer necessarily transforms the method. Finally, Lorenzini's *The Force of Truth* offers the most recent synthetic attempt to consolidate these terms, reconstructing critique, genealogy, and parrhēsia (truth-telling) as a unified late-Foucauldian project in which historical critique draws its force from an ethics and politics of truth-telling rather than functioning as a purely descriptive historiographical technique (Lorenzini 2023). This consolidation itself testifies to the absence of settlement: a synthesis is offered precisely because the underlying questions — whether genealogy is properly critical or merely descriptive, and whether it can travel intact — remain live.

Taken together, this cluster demonstrates that genealogy's legitimacy and scope remain structurally contested at the level of legacy just as Megill diagnosed structural ambiguity at the level of Foucault's own practice (Section 4.2): the same text or method can be read as rigorous critique, as diminished conceptual borrowing, as normatively question-begging, or as a portable analytic requiring transformation — and the literature has not converged on which reading is correct, only on naming and refining the terms of the disagreement.

Across all three subsections, Foucault emerges as a reference point whose historiographical status is repeatedly re-litigated rather than fixed: placed alongside Sartre, White, and Arendt as a rival theorist of historical reason; reactivated as an open method for writing histories of the present; and contested as either an authentic or a diluted practice of critique wherever it travels.

## Conclusion

This review has traced a single reorientation of the philosophy of history across six domains of scholarship: Foucault's replacement of continuity, teleology, progress, and the constituting subject-as-origin with discontinuity and rupture, contingency and effective history, power/knowledge, and a historically constituted subject. Archaeology described discursive formations and epistemes without appeal to developmental causation; genealogy, inheriting a Nietzschean vocabulary of *Herkunft* and *Entstehung*, replaced ideology-critique with an account of power and knowledge as mutually constitutive; and the late "history of the present" recast the whole enterprise as a critical ontology of ourselves. Across the secondary literature surveyed, this reorientation is treated less as a settled doctrine than as a working method whose coherence, sources, and legitimacy remain objects of active dispute.

A cross-cutting tension recurs throughout this literature: the relationship between Foucault's professed methodological posture — description of conditions of possibility, refusal of causal-developmental narrative, disavowal of any transcendental standpoint — and his evident normative-political commitments. Garland's exposition of *Discipline and Punish* finds a genealogical narrative that is simultaneously historical description and implicit indictment of disciplinary power, without a stated account of what licenses the move from one to the other (Garland 1986). Fraser presses this into an explicit charge of normative confusion: Foucault relies on evaluative terms like domination and subjugation while insisting power is everywhere and value-neutral (Fraser 1985). Taylor generalizes the objection into a self-refutation charge — if truth is always an effect of power, Foucault's own critical histories cannot ground the emancipatory force he wants them to have (Taylor 1984). Flynn's axial reading of Foucault's oeuvre encounters the same tension from a sympathetic vantage point, leaving unresolved whether the ethics-subjectivation axis is one option among three for charting Foucault's work or the culminating concern that retrospectively unifies it (Flynn 2005). And the genealogy-as-critique debate closing Section 6 shows this tension persisting into Foucault's legacy, as later commentators dispute whether tracing contingent emergence is itself critique or requires normative resources genealogy disclaims. Description and judgment are not obviously separable in Foucault's practice, and the literature has not converged on how, or whether, they can be.

Several questions therefore remain genuinely open rather than resolved by this review: whether the corpus is unified or ruptured, given competing expansion, supplementation, and continuity readings of the archaeology-genealogy transition (Koopman 2008); whether discontinuity is a coherent unit of historical explanation and what ontological status the episteme possesses; whether power/knowledge entails a self-undermining relativism or instead refuses a question-begging demand for extra-historical foundations; whether a decentered subject can still ground critique and agency; and whether genealogy is properly critical or merely descriptive, and whether it survives intact when it travels beyond its original site of articulation.

The literature surveyed also displays identifiable gaps. A systematic realist/anti-realist treatment of Foucauldian discontinuity, comparable to debates over scientific paradigms, remains thin. Direct comparison between Foucault and Ankersmit's narrativism is underdeveloped relative to pairings with White and Arendt. Engagement with non-Anglophone, especially French, secondary scholarship is limited within the discoverable literature this review draws upon. And the ontology of the episteme — what kind of entity it is, and what work "historical a priori" can do — remains an open question even among the most sympathetic reconstructions.

This review has aimed to supply a synthetic, debate-centered map of this terrain: one that reconstructs Foucault's own historiographical practice before staging the controversies it has generated, neither assimilating his work wholesale to analytic or narrativist philosophy of history nor treating his historiography as exempt from the critical assessment historians and philosophers alike have brought to it.
