## Section 2: Archaeology and Genealogy as Philosophy of History and Method

Where Section 1 traced what Foucault said and did, this section asks what kind of historical knowledge archaeology and genealogy claim to produce, and how the two methods relate to one another. The second question — the relation between archaeology and genealogy — constitutes the pivotal methodological controversy of the review's first half.

### Subsection 2.1: Archaeology's coherence and the historical a priori

The interpretive baseline for archaeology as a mode of historical explanation is Gutting's (1989) reconstruction, which situates Foucault's archaeological studies of madness, clinical medicine, and the human sciences within the Bachelard–Canguilhem tradition of French historical epistemology rather than treating archaeology as a species of structuralism or a precursor to genealogy. This framing supplies the interpretive backdrop against which subsequent analytic and phenomenological readings react.

The earliest sustained Anglo-American analytic engagement with archaeology is Hacking's (1986) assessment of Foucault's account of discursive "positivities" and historical rule-structures, an essay whose influence is attested by its status as the direct target of Wartenberg's (2017) response. Wartenberg reconstructs Hacking's project as an attempt to render Foucault "palatable" to a reluctant analytic audience by locating his work on the existing analytic landscape, and assesses whether that assimilative strategy, and Rorty's related one, succeeds. This exchange sets the terms for a recurring question: whether archaeology can be captured by analytic categories at all, or whether — as Krarup (2021) argues — it must be understood within the specifically French "history of systems of thought" that Foucault inherited and reworked (via Dumézil's comparative-archaeological methodology) rather than as a freestanding position answerable to Anglo-American criteria.

A second line of attack targets archaeology's coherence directly. Dreyfus and Rabinow charge that archaeology rests on an incoherent conception of the rules governing discursive practices. Tiisala (2015) defends Foucault, arguing the criticism arises from misreading archaeology as a transcendental inquiry into conditions of possibility; archaeology is instead a "diagnostic" project that need not, and should not, render its governing rules explicit — a defense that recasts archaeology as description of historically contingent, implicit normative structures rather than a search for necessary conditions.

A third cluster addresses the "historical a priori," Foucault's term for the conditions of unity of a given field of science. Peters (2021) argues that "episteme" and "historical a priori" are frequently and wrongly conflated: episteme names the overall configuration of thought in a period, while the historical a priori names conditions internal to a specific science — a distinction that, on Peters's reading, brings Foucault closer to Husserl than usually appreciated. Basso (2012) develops this Husserlian lineage further, tracing the concept to the young Foucault's engagement with existential psychiatry and his 1954 introduction to Binswanger, arguing for methodological continuity between this early phenomenological phase and archaeology-as-historical-epistemology, and linking archaeology to Canguilhem's project of tying historicity to transcendentality. Hyder (2009) supplies a more specific version of the same lineage via Cavaillès's phenomenological archaeology of the sciences: Foucault and Cavaillès retain Husserl's technique of reactivating sedimented intentional acts while rejecting its appeal to consciousness. Flynn (2016) likewise reads the historical a priori as retaining a phenomenological debt to Husserl even as Foucault historicizes what Husserl treated as more nearly transcendental. Against this convergent Husserlian reading, Webb (2012) grounds the concept instead in Foucault's engagement with the "mathematical a priori" and Kant, tracing "temporal dispersion" to Foucault's critique of Kant via Cavaillès and Serres read as philosophers of mathematics rather than phenomenologists. Carr (2016) sharpens the comparative stakes by arguing that Husserl and Foucault use the identical phrase "historical a priori" to mean opposite things: for Husserl history has an intrinsic teleological structure, whereas for Foucault it must be understood in anti-teleological, discontinuist terms. This cluster shows the historical a priori functioning as a contested site: its Husserlian ancestry is widely acknowledged, but whether that ancestry commits Foucault to a teleological residue he elsewhere disavows remains open.

### Subsection 2.2: Genealogy as a mode of historical explanation

If archaeology's coherence is defended against an incoherence charge, genealogy is more often characterized positively as a distinctive mode of historical explanation. Bevir (2008) offers a general metahistorical account of genealogy as an expression of radical historicism: genealogies reject appeals to transcendental truths and to principles of unity or progress, embracing nominalism, contingency, and contestability, and operate as denaturalizing critiques of practices that conceal their own contingency behind ahistorical or developmental self-presentations.

Koopman (2013) develops a fuller account of genealogy as critique, arguing that Foucault's method involves not only diagnosing modernity's problems but transforming them practically, via the concept of "problematization": genealogy shows how a practice, concept, or norm was actually composed through complex historical vectors, equipping present actors with resources for reconstructing it differently. Koopman distinguishes this "problematizing" genealogy from Nietzsche's subversive genealogy and Bernard Williams's vindicatory genealogy — Foucault's version, unlike theirs, does not aim to establish or undermine specific normative conclusions, which also blunts the standard "genetic fallacy" objection.

Lichtenstein (2022) complicates the picture of genealogy as a purely philosophical enterprise. Most philosophical genealogists eschew historiographic methodology in favor of a purely philosophical approach; Foucault's early-1970s genealogy is unusual in borrowing from contemporary historiographic models of explanation, modifying two central Nietzschean commitments to expand the range of objects proper to genealogical accounts of change. On this reading, historical method carries normative weight for genealogy, since different historiographic choices yield different normative conclusions — integrating historiographic explanation and evaluative critique rather than treating genealogy as purely one or the other.

Garland (1986) tests these characterizations against genealogy as practiced in Discipline and Punish. He provides a systematic exposition of the book's account of the shift from sovereign to disciplinary power and its introduction of "power-knowledge," but subjects this account to critique, questioning both its evidentiary basis and the coherence of its claims about the functions of the prison — a sympathetic but critical assessment that raises, ahead of Section 4's discussion of historians' reception, whether genealogy's explanatory ambitions are matched by its historical rigor.

### Subsection 2.3: The archaeology–genealogy transition (structured debate)

The relationship between archaeology and genealogy is the pivotal methodological controversy of the review's first half. An older reading treats the shift from The Archaeology of Knowledge to "Nietzsche, Genealogy, History" and Discipline and Punish as a clean break: archaeology abandoned, genealogy substituted in its place. The literature surveyed here disputes this "abandonment" reading from at least four directions, without converging on a single alternative.

The **expansion** position, developed by Koopman (2008), holds that the shift is better conceived as a historiographical expansion than a replacement: Foucault moves from analyzing singular, isolable domains of practice (knowledge alone) to analyzing interactions among multiple domains (power/knowledge), and from an analytic relying on a single temporal category of rupture to one invoking multiple temporalities, including continuity alongside discontinuity. Genealogy, on this reading, retains and builds upon archaeology rather than superseding it.

The **continuity/self-reference** position is advanced by Baert (1998), who argues that Foucault's "history of the present" is a form of self-referential knowledge acquisition — histories that are not neutral reconstructions of the past but interventions constitutive of the present they analyze. Baert traces this concern across Foucault's career and argues that archaeology and genealogy "have more in common than conventionally assumed" because both are directed toward this same self-referential form of knowledge, a claim in productive tension with Koopman's expansion thesis insofar as it locates continuity at the level of epistemic function rather than analytic scope.

The **supplementation** position belongs to Thompson (2016), who argues, against readings that treat genealogy as archaeology's replacement, that genealogy is best construed as a supplementary addition to archaeological investigation. Thompson traces the movement from the "historical a priori" to the later concept of the "dispositif" through the problem of transcendental genesis inherited from the phenomenological tradition, arguing that the dispositif absorbs and extends the historical a priori rather than discarding it — resolving a problem that the historical a priori alone could not.

The **intertwinement** position is represented by two studies of transitional vocabulary and practice. Elden (2025) traces Foucault's use of the term "dynastics" (from dunamis/dunasteia) in lectures and one interview from the early 1970s — after his explicit engagement with Nietzsche in "Nietzsche, Genealogy, History" but before he settled on "genealogy" — as a complementary approach to archaeology relating to both power and knowledge; even after Foucault drops the term, Elden argues, dynastics continues to inform how he subsequently writes about power relations and lineage, refining the standard two-stage narrative with an intermediate, Nietzsche-adjacent vocabulary. T'Jampens and Versieren (2020) offer a complementary case study at the level of practice: in Society Must Be Defended, Foucault deploys the method of "critical archaeology" upon genealogy itself, producing a descriptive discursive history of his own genealogical project in order to historicize and criticize Nietzsche's equation of the social with struggle — a discovery that led Foucault to distance his concept of struggle from Nietzsche's and to rethink the social within the framework of gouvernementalité. On this reading, archaeology and genealogy are methodologically intertwined — used jointly on the same material — rather than sequential or discrete stages.

These four positions are not straightforwardly reconcilable. Expansion (Koopman) and supplementation (Thompson) both reject the abandonment reading but locate the relationship differently — added domains and temporalities versus a resolved transcendental problem. Continuity/self-reference (Baert) locates the unity beneath both terms, in shared epistemic function, rather than in a directional relation between them. Intertwinement (Elden; T'Jampens and Versieren) resists periodization altogether, treating the vocabulary and technique of the two methods as overlapping in Foucault's actual practice during the very years the "shift" is supposed to have occurred. What unites all four is only their shared rejection of a simple abandonment narrative; how, precisely, to characterize the positive relationship between archaeology and genealogy remains unsettled in the literature, and this unsettled status recurs as the throughline connecting Section 1's corpus-wide continuity/rupture question to the discontinuity debate taken up in Section 5.3.

Archaeology's coherence is defended — against the Dreyfus/Rabinow incoherence charge (Tiisala) and situated within both analytic (Hacking, Wartenberg) and French disciplinary (Krarup) contexts — even as its central category, the historical a priori, remains contested between Husserlian and Kantian-mathematical genealogies. Genealogy is characterized across converging accounts (Bevir, Koopman, Lichtenstein) as a distinctive, transformative, historiographically engaged mode of critique, though its application in Discipline and Punish draws Garland's pointed methodological criticism. And the relationship between the two methods — expansion, continuity, supplementation, or intertwinement — remains an open question that later sections of this review return to repeatedly.
