## Section 4: Historians' Reception and Empirical Critique

Where Sections 1–3 reconstructed Foucault's historiographical self-descriptions and intellectual debts largely on their own terms, professional historians confronting the same texts have not always recognized the enterprise as history at all. This section turns to that disconfirming, discipline-internal perspective: what happens when specialists in madness, medicine, and the human sciences check Foucault's claims against the documentary record, and how the resulting disputes have been prosecuted, reframed as a matter of genre, or absorbed through selective revision. The pattern is not a simple verdict but a conversation split along the fault line Sections 1–3 leave open: is archaeology/genealogy usable historical method, or philosophy borrowing history's evidentiary trappings without submitting to its norms?

### 4.1 The empirical prosecution

Windschuttle (1998) mounts the harshest case, arguing that across *Madness and Civilization*, *Discipline and Punish*, and *The History of Sexuality* Foucault repeatedly misrepresented, selectively quoted, or invented evidence to fit a predetermined thesis about power and exclusion, and that historians who adopted Foucauldian method imported these errors uncritically. His objections are itemized — chronology of confinement, treatment of sources, selective citation — and explicitly deny Foucault standing as a historian.

Scull (1990), a historian of psychiatry, argues that Foucault's account of a unitary "Great Confinement" and his opposition of madness to reason do not survive confrontation with archival and comparative evidence historians of the asylum have since assembled, though he credits the book's rhetorical force with helping create the field of the history of psychiatry as it now exists — a mixed verdict rather than wholesale dismissal.

A parallel prosecution targets the discontinuity thesis itself. Maclean (1998), a historian of Renaissance thought, argues that Foucault's characterization of Renaissance knowledge as governed by resemblance and analogy neglects the substantial Aristotelian logical apparatus actually deployed by Renaissance thinkers, making Foucault's claim of a sharp discontinuity between the Renaissance and Classical epistemes historically inaccurate — testing, from within the archival record, whether one of Foucault's paradigmatic ruptures survives scrutiny (a question resumed formally in Section 5.3).

Wilson (2007) reconstructs the historian Roy Porter's empirical and methodological objections to *The Birth of the Clinic*, contrasting Porter's patient-centered social history of medicine with Foucault's structuralist account of the "clinical gaze," while asking which aspects of the disagreement are genuinely empirical and which reflect differing conceptions of historical explanation — a distinction anticipating the genre-based reframing below.

### 4.2 Genre-reclassification and defenses

A second cluster treats the accuracy dispute partly as a category error. Megill (1990) supplies the framework most cited in this reception debate: Foucault's histories are constitutively ambiguous between empirical historiography and a rhetorically driven, philosophically motivated genre, and this ambiguity is a structural feature of the texts rather than a defect. On his account, historians and philosophers disputing Foucault's "accuracy" are implicitly applying different genre expectations to the same texts.

White (1973), in *History and Theory*, reads *The Order of Things* as "anti-history" that deliberately eschews the connective, developmental emplotment of conventional historiography, arguing that Foucault's archaeological method should be analyzed through literary and rhetorical categories rather than judged by evidentiary standards proper to empirical history (a reading resumed in Section 6.1's placement of Foucault relative to narrativist philosophy of history).

Han-Pile (2005) asks directly "is early Foucault a historian?," arguing that early attacks by historians of ideas charging inaccuracy misconstrue a project never primarily aimed at empirical reconstruction but at a transcendental-historical diagnosis of the conditions of possibility for the human sciences — the "analytic of finitude." She notes that historians of ideas "immediately attacked" Foucault for alleged inaccuracy upon *The Order of Things*' publication, making her essay a hinge between the philosophical and historiographical literatures.

Alford (2000) poses the stakes most pointedly for *Discipline and Punish*, asking what would remain of its apparatus — panopticism, disciplinary power, the carceral continuum — if its narrative about the birth of the prison were wrong. He concludes that much of the book's influence rests on its diagnostic power over the present rather than the accuracy of its genealogy, granting empirical vulnerability while defending residual theoretical value.

LaCapra (1990), an intellectual historian, similarly defends the history of madness against purely empiricist objections, arguing the text operates simultaneously as documentary history and as a self-consciously "excessive" or transgressive form of writing that deliberately unsettles historiographical protocols, so judging it solely by archival-accuracy criteria misses this dual character. (The bibliographic record truncates the author's given name to "Dominick L."; a corroborating institutional record is consistent with the intellectual historian Dominick LaCapra, but the attribution is noted here rather than silently resolved.) The essay appeared in the same 1990 special issue as Scull's prosecution, illustrating a split among historians themselves over whether the history of madness should be judged as history proper or as a hybrid historical-philosophical genre.

### 4.3 Selective appropriation and disciplinary uptake

A third response neither prosecutes nor reclassifies but revises. Weymans (2009) reconstructs how the French historians Marcel Gauchet and Gladys Swain revised Foucault's exclusion thesis from within the same archival territory — the birth of the asylum — arguing that modern psychiatric practice represented a new form of recognition of the mad rather than confinement, and that they saw resistance where Foucault saw only the panoptic asylum's success. Their critique of contemporary societies, Weymans argues, nonetheless remains indebted to Foucault's own problematization of madness and modernity — historians working inside Foucault's field who revise rather than reject his narrative.

Disciplinary self-reflection supplies a further register. Surkis (2012), in *The American Historical Review*, historicizes the profession's own narrative about a "linguistic turn" in which Foucauldian method was credited with (or blamed for) transforming Anglo-American historiography in the 1980s–90s, arguing this standard genealogy oversimplifies a more heterogeneous process of disciplinary uptake. Forrester (1980), writing for a history-of-science audience, assesses *The Order of Things*' treatment of psychoanalysis as a "counter-science," probing whether Foucault's reconstruction of the episteme underlying the human sciences is defensible as history of science or a philosophically motivated schema imposed on the material, arriving at a cautious appraisal that neither wholly rejects nor uncritically adopts Foucault's account.

The debate has also been staged in collective forums. Goldstein (1994) edits a volume assessing, essay by essay, how Foucault's histories of madness, sexuality, and the human sciences hold up against historiographical norms — spanning empirical rejection, rhetorical reclassification, and selective appropriation, and including Megill's essay. Still and Velody (1992) assemble a narrower forum on *Histoire de la folie* specifically, revisiting confinement and the classical age in light of subsequent archival research and situating the English-language controversy within its French context. Earle (2007), writing after the 2006 unabridged translation of *History of Madness*, synthesizes the accumulated critical "reaction-shots" — overstated unity of the classical age, selective sources, romanticized unreason — while asking what remains philosophically valuable once these objections are granted, largely endorsing the genre-reclassification view while taking the empirical objections seriously.

This body of reception resists a binary appropriation/rejection framing: it spans empirical rejection (Windschuttle, Scull, Maclean, Porter via Wilson), genre-reclassification (Megill, White, Han-Pile, Alford, LaCapra), and selective revision (Weymans, Surkis, Forrester). The deepest diagnosis of why these positions so rarely engage one another directly remains Megill's: the dispute over Foucault's "accuracy" is not simply a dispute over facts but a disagreement about which genre — empirical historiography or rhetorically driven philosophical history — the disputants take themselves to be evaluating. Historians applying archival standards and philosophers reading Foucault's texts as diagnostic or transcendental are, on this account, applying incompatible genre expectations to the same texts.
