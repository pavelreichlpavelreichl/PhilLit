## Section 5: The Core Philosophical Debates

This section concentrates the free-standing philosophical objections and defenses that bear on Foucault's historiography as a general epistemological and political project, independent of any particular archival claim. Four controversies recur: whether power/knowledge collapses into self-undermining relativism; whether Foucault's genealogies smuggle in normative commitments his own premises forbid, staged most fully against Habermas; whether discontinuity and the episteme are coherent units of historical explanation; and whether a subject dissolved into anonymous discursive formations can still ground resistance or judgment. These debates are not independent: the relativism and normativity disputes share the self-refutation structure, and the subject debate supplies a resource — subjectivation, critique as self-practice — that several respondents use to answer both.

### 5.1 Relativism, anti-realism, and self-refutation

Taylor's founding statement of the self-refutation charge argues that because Foucault denies any standpoint of truth or freedom external to power/knowledge formations, his genealogies of madness, sexuality, and confession cannot explain why liberation from one discursive regime should count as progress rather than mere substitution of one power-truth configuration for another, leaving Foucault's implicit valorization of resistance without a defensible basis (Taylor 1984). Nola mounts a parallel, more technical prosecution from analytic philosophy of science: Foucault's "Primacy Thesis" — the causal priority of power over knowledge — equivocates between senses of power and fails either as a general explanatory theory of scientific belief or as a coherent alternative to realist accounts of truth, so that insofar as Foucault wants to criticize beliefs circulating as effects of power, he needs the traditional account of truth he officially disavows (Nola 1994).

Two responses contest the terms of this charge rather than concede it. Han-Pile distinguishes "how possible" questions about the enabling conditions of knowledge from "whether permissible" questions about legitimacy, and argues Foucault's most defensible reply to Taylor and Habermas is not to supply the demanded normative foundations but to refuse the demand as question-begging, replacing justification with critique as a "performative practice of the self" (Han-Pile 2016). Tremain instead adopts Foucauldian historicist relativism affirmatively: her feminist philosophy of disability uses genealogy precisely because it avoids the ahistorical, teleological, and transcultural assumptions she finds elsewhere in disability theory, treating the refusal of ahistorical essences as an advantage rather than a liability (Tremain 2015). D'Cruz supplies a third rejoinder, internal to the historiographical tradition Foucault inherits from Canguilhem: rereading genealogy as modeled on empirical inquiry and grounded in Foucault's continued respect for the natural sciences, D'Cruz argues the reduction of knowledge to power rests on a misreading of Foucault's project as a normative epistemology rather than a historico-critical one (D'Cruz 2024).

What remains unresolved is whether these responses answer Taylor and Nola or merely relocate the disagreement. Han-Pile and Tremain both accept that Foucault offers no independent normative foundation and argue this is not a defect, while D'Cruz denies the practice requires one; none produces the external validation Taylor and Nola take to be minimally required for critique to retain force.

### 5.2 Power/knowledge, normativity, and the Foucault–Habermas debate

Fraser's normative-deficit objection applies the self-refutation worry directly to power/knowledge: Foucault's genealogies characterize power with evaluative terms — domination, subjugation, struggle — while insisting power is everywhere, value-neutral, and without an outside, an equivocation that lets Foucault imply political critique of disciplinary institutions without supplying, or being able to supply, the normative criteria that would justify calling disciplinary power domination rather than simply power (Fraser 1985). No citable stand-alone article by Habermas directly engaging Foucault could be resolved for this review; his position is represented here only through the secondary literature. Freundlieb reconstructs Habermas's charge that Foucault belongs to a lineage of "totalizing" critiques of reason — alongside Nietzsche and Heidegger — trapped in performative self-contradiction, since Foucault's genealogies presuppose the rational, truth-oriented standards that power/knowledge relativizes; Freundlieb notes that Habermas's own defense of rationalism, via knowledge-guiding interests, commits him to a comparable anti-realism (Freundlieb 1988).

A mediating cluster resists treating the exchange as flat opposition. Ingram compares the two thinkers' styles of critical theory, arguing Foucault's virtue-ethical aesthetic self-realization is a genuine counterpoint to Habermas's juridical discourse ethics rather than an inferior alternative, with parrhesia as a non-discursive mode of truth-telling resisting assimilation to communicative rationality (Ingram 2005). King argues responses to Habermas have suffered from imprecision about what "normative foundations" means, concluding that while Foucault's political judgments genuinely lack normative foundations in Habermas's strict sense, Foucault nonetheless adheres to the procedural principles of discourse ethics without conflict with his genealogical method (King 2009). Biebricher pushes further, arguing Habermas misconstrues genealogy by assuming it aims at scientific, truth-claiming reconstruction, when genealogy is better read as a rhetorically hybrid mode between science and literature — tracing the misreading to Habermas's reconstruction of Nietzsche and concluding that rhetorically understood genealogy counts as valid even on Habermas's own terms (Biebricher 2005). Allen relocates the debate onto subjectivation — how individuals become subjects — arguing the two frameworks are not too disparate to integrate, and beginning to use Foucauldian genealogy to supplement Habermasian communicative-action theory's account of discursive subject-formation (Allen 2009).

What remains unresolved is whether the reconciling readings dissolve the dispute or redescribe it as genre mismatch. King and Biebricher converge on the diagnosis that much of the apparent conflict stems from imprecision about normative foundations and genre, but neither shows that Fraser's underlying worry — that Foucault's evaluative vocabulary needs grounding it structurally cannot supply — is answered rather than reclassified.

### 5.3 Discontinuity and the status of the episteme

The discontinuity thesis faces an external historical challenge and an internal formal one. Maclean's reassessment of Foucault's Renaissance episteme argues that Foucault's characterization of Renaissance knowledge as governed by resemblance and analogy neglects the Aristotelian logical apparatus actually deployed by Renaissance thinkers, making his periodization and his claimed sharp break between the Renaissance and Classical epistemes historically inaccurate (Maclean 1998). Cutrofello complements this with an internal critique, examining the quasi-structuralist "table" organizing the classical episteme — the grid relating general grammar, natural history, and the analysis of wealth — and asking whether it is complete in its own terms or harbors gaps that complicate Foucault's claim to a single, internally coherent configuration of classical knowledge (Cutrofello 2003).

Two comparative papers clarify rather than directly refute the historical a priori underwriting discontinuity. Carr contrasts Husserl's and Foucault's shared use of the phrase "the historical a priori," arguing the difference tracks a deeper disagreement over whether history has an intrinsic teleological structure — Husserl affirming it, Foucault developing an explicitly anti-teleological, discontinuist alternative (Carr 2016). Vaccarino Bremner situates Foucault's historical a priori within the lineage of "relativized a priori" theorizing running from logical empiricism through Cassirer's symbolic form, arguing Foucault's version is more thoroughly relativized than Cassirer's but that it admits of internal tensions accounting for transitions to a new a priori, with social power playing a role more fully developed than analogous resources in contemporary philosophy of science (Vaccarino Bremner 2025). This reframes discontinuity as one instance of a family of strategies reconciling historically variable frameworks with the a priori's claim to necessity, rather than a sui generis relativism.

What remains unresolved, and is a flagged gap in the literature, is a systematic realist/anti-realist treatment of Foucauldian discontinuity comparable to Kuhn-paradigm debates; existing engagement splits between Maclean's and Cutrofello's targeted critiques of specific epistemes and the largely comparative, expository work of Carr and Vaccarino Bremner, leaving the general ontological status of an episteme unsettled.

### 5.4 The decentering of the subject

Canguilhem — writing as Foucault's own teacher and examiner — supplies the authoritative gloss on the "death of man" announced in The Order of Things: not the biological extinction of humanity, but the historically recent and contingent emergence of "man" as object and subject of knowledge within the modern episteme, and hence the possible dissolution of that configuration. He situates this within Foucault's structuralist displacement of the transcendental, constituting Cartesian cogito by anonymous discursive formations (Canguilhem 2005). The implication is that historical explanation must proceed without a subject standing as the origin of history — one of the four commitments of traditional philosophy of history that Foucault sets out to dismantle.

This raises a problem shared with the normativity debate: can a decentered subject still be an agent of resistance or judgment? Han-Pile's answer, introduced in 5.1, is that critique survives not as foundational justification but as a "performative practice of the self" (Han-Pile 2016). Allen's account of subjectivation, introduced in 5.2, gives a structurally similar answer from the Habermasian side: subjects are formed through discursive and power-laden processes that genealogy and communicative-action theory can jointly illuminate, making subjectivation the pivot at which the decentered subject and the demand for critical agency might be reconciled rather than treated as flatly incompatible (Allen 2009).

What remains unresolved is whether "critique as a practice of the self" and "subjectivation" answer the agency question or relabel it: both trade a constituting subject for a subject constituted through practices that are themselves candidates for the genealogical unmasking Foucault applies to earlier configurations of "man," leaving open whether the resulting account of resistance is stable or defers the problem Canguilhem identifies to a further historical regress.

These four controversies are tightly intertwined: the relativism and Habermas debates share the self-refutation structure, with Han-Pile's and Biebricher's diagnoses that the demand for foundations is itself part of the dispute recurring across both; the discontinuity debate supplies the historical-a-priori vocabulary that Han-Pile and D'Cruz's naturalist reading draw on to characterize Foucauldian normativity; and the subject debate closes the loop by asking who is left to resist once truth and the subject have both been historicized. Critics and defenders remain squarely opposed throughout, though several disputes — most explicitly Foucault–Habermas — are diagnosed in the literature itself as resting on mutual misreading or imprecise framing rather than a substantive incompatibility a single further argument could resolve.
