## Section 1: Foucault's Theory of History Across the Corpus

Foucault never presents his theory of history as a standing doctrine, defended once and then applied. It is instead enacted serially, across a sequence of studies that each redescribe, often retrospectively, what the preceding work had been doing. The result is a corpus that can be read either as a single project accreting vocabulary — archaeology, genealogy, history of the present — or as a set of discontinuous enterprises linked only by family resemblance. This section reconstructs that arc work by work, establishing the textual baseline against which later sections stage the debate over whether the shifts amount to rupture or continuous development.

### Subsection 1.1: The archaeological works and their historiographical claims

*History of Madness* (1961/2013) is the point of origin. Foucault there traces the exclusion and subsequent pathologization of "madness" in Western Europe from the Renaissance to the birth of psychiatry, arguing that the exclusion of unreason is constitutive of, rather than incidental to, Enlightenment reason (foucault2013historymadness). The book's method already works by attention to discontinuities and epistemic breaks, drawing on Bachelard's notion of epistemological rupture and Canguilhem's normal/pathological distinction rather than a continuous narrative of psychiatric progress (foucault2013historymadness). It is also the first text in which the word "archaeology" appears, in the original preface, though at this stage the term names a style rather than a fully worked-out method (foucault2013historymadness).

*The Birth of the Clinic* (1963/1973) tightens the program: Foucault proposes to analyze medical discourse "on its own terms," treating signifiers without reference to any fundamental reality they are taken to represent, and centers the analysis on the emergence of the clinical "gaze" as a historically specific, non-prejudicial mode of medical observation tied to new social and political demands (foucault1973birthclinic). This is the first work to carry "archaeology" in its subtitle, marking the term's transition from an incidental label to a named method — although its stated discourse-only program is not yet fully matched by its actual historical analysis (foucault1973birthclinic).

*The Order of Things* (1966/2005) is where archaeology becomes a fully deliberate methodology. Foucault argues that Western knowledge has passed through discrete "epistemes" — stable, largely unspoken ensembles of rules governing what can be known in a given period — separated by two major ruptures, one at the start of the Classical age and one at the threshold of modernity (foucault2005orderthings). Crucially, Foucault declines to explain why these ruptures occur, restricting archaeology to a description of what changed rather than a causal account of why it changed; the book's closing pages extend this discontinuist logic to anticipate the historically contingent "end of man" as an object of knowledge (foucault2005orderthings).

*The Archaeology of Knowledge* (1969/1972) then consolidates the method theoretically. It is Foucault's only major work that is not itself a historical study, analyzing discourse in terms of discrete "statements" and their relations independently of any subject who transcends or produces them, and introducing the "archive" as the system governing which statements can appear — archaeology is redefined here as the excavation of that archive (foucault1972archaeology). The text asserts the "relative autonomy" of discourse against both transcendental subjectivity and reductive social or economic determination, and retroactively reframes the method used in the three prior monographs (foucault1972archaeology). Together, these four works establish the vocabulary — episteme, discursive formation, statement, archive, historical a priori — and the refusal of causal-developmental explanation that the archaeology-as-method debate (Section 2) and the discontinuity debate (Section 5.3) take as their object.

### Subsection 1.2: The genealogical turn and power/knowledge

"Nietzsche, Genealogy, History" (1971/2019) supplies the manifesto for what follows. Adapting Nietzsche's vocabulary of *Ursprung* (origin), *Herkunft* (descent), and *Entstehung* (emergence), Foucault argues against the search for pure origins in history, proposing instead that genealogy trace the contingent, often violent "play of dominations" through which practices and values emerge, and calling for a *wirkliche Historie* — effective history — that abandons suprahistorical, continuous narratives in favor of disparity, chance, and the body as the surface on which history is inscribed (foucault2019nietzsche). The essay functions as the primary methodological statement of Foucault's genealogical period and is the text later secondary literature on the Nietzsche relation (Section 3.1) and the archaeology–genealogy transition (Section 2.3) takes as its central object.

*Discipline and Punish* (1975/1977) puts this program to work on the emergence of the modern prison. Foucault argues that the prison functions structurally to produce criminality and recidivism, situating it within the broader historical emergence of "discipline" — a form of power first developed in seventeenth-century professional soldiering and subsequently generalized across the factory, the school, and the army to produce controllable "docile bodies" (foucault1977discipline). Its historiographical innovation is the introduction of "power-knowledge" as a replacement for the Marxist category of ideology: knowledge is not distorted by power from outside but constitutively bound up with it (foucault1977discipline). This is Foucault's first genealogical monograph and the primary text through which the archaeology/genealogy relationship becomes concrete rather than programmatic.

*The History of Sexuality, Volume 1* (1976/1978) extends this analysis into a fully elaborated theory of power. Against the "repressive hypothesis" that modernity has silenced discourse on sex, Foucault shows a proliferation of discourses compelling us to speak of it, and rejects a "juridical," monarchical model of power as prohibition in favor of a relational, productive, and strategic conception of power without a single locus or origin, introducing the concepts of biopower and biopolitics (foucault1978sexuality). *"Society Must Be Defended"* (1975–76/2003), the Collège de France lectures delivered in the same year as the writing of *Discipline and Punish*, offers a genealogy of the discourse of race war as an alternative to the sovereign/juridical model of politics, and contains what has been described as the clearest exposition of Foucault's thinking on biopower (foucault2003society). As a lecture course contemporaneous with *Discipline and Punish* and preceding *The History of Sexuality*, it functions as a bridge document for tracing continuity or discontinuity in Foucault's historiographical self-understanding across the mid-1970s (foucault2003society). Together this cluster feeds the transition debate (Section 2.3), the Nietzsche debate (Section 3.1), and the power/knowledge debate (Section 5.2).

### Subsection 1.3: "History of the present" as retrospective self-description

Foucault's latest self-characterization recasts the entire project in Kantian terms. In "What is Enlightenment?" (1984), written as a response to Kant's 1784 essay of the same title, Foucault describes his historiographical enterprise retrospectively as a "history of the present": an inquiry into "what we are" pursued not by searching for universal structures but through critical, historical analysis of the limits imposed on us, undertaken as a possible crossing-over of those limits, and proposes a "critical ontology of ourselves" as an ethos rather than a doctrine (foucault1984enlightenment).

Two early secondary readings treat this self-description as evidence for continuity across the corpus rather than as a late addendum. Roth argues that in *The Birth of the Clinic*, *The Order of Things*, and *Discipline and Punish*, Foucault writes a "history of the present" in which archaeological description of past discursive formations is never separable from a critical, present-oriented political stance — a self-conscious field of power relations rather than a neutral reconstruction of the past (roth1981history). Baert extends this claim further, arguing that Foucault's histories should be understood as a form of self-referential knowledge acquisition — interventions that are themselves constitutive of the present they analyze — and that this concern is common to both the archaeological and genealogical periods, giving the two "more in common than conventionally assumed" (baert1998history). Roth and Baert thus function as early continuity theses, proposing that the history-of-the-present logic unifies archaeology and genealogy beneath their surface vocabulary.

Read across the whole corpus, this arc — from the incidental archaeology of *History of Madness* to the named method of *The Order of Things* and its theoretical self-clarification in *The Archaeology of Knowledge*, through the Nietzschean manifesto and its institutional applications in *Discipline and Punish*, *The History of Sexuality*, and the Collège de France lectures, to the late Kantian recasting as "history of the present" — establishes what Foucault said and did. It does not settle whether that arc is unified or ruptured. Foucault's own retrospective self-descriptions both raise this question and complicate it, since each new methodological label (archaeology, genealogy, history of the present) can be read either as naming a genuine break or as making explicit what was already latent in the earlier work. This continuity/rupture axis is the through-line that Sections 2 and 6 take up directly.
