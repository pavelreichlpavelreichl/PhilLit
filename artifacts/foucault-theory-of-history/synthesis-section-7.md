## Section 6: Legacy and Placement in the Philosophy of History

Foucault's theory of history did not remain a self-contained episode in French intellectual life; it became a durable reference point across historiographical theory, critical theory, and political philosophy. Yet the exact nature of that legacy is itself contested. This section traces three afterlives — Foucault's placement relative to the canonical philosophy-of-history tradition, the "history of the present" as an ongoing research program, and genealogy's diffusion as a still-disputed mode of critique — and shows that in each case the question of what kind of enterprise Foucault bequeathed remains structurally open.

### 6.1 Placement relative to narrativist and analytic philosophy of history

The most sustained attempt to locate Foucault's practice of history within the wider landscape of twentieth-century theories of historical reason is Thomas Flynn's two-volume *Sartre, Foucault, and Historical Reason*. The first volume reconstructs Sartre's *Critique of Dialectical Reason* as an existentialist theory of history grounded in individual and group praxis (Flynn 1997), establishing the baseline against which the second volume reads Foucault's archaeology and genealogy as a "poststructuralist mapping of history" — a nominalist, spatialized, cartographic enterprise that displaces Sartre's subject-centered dialectic while still addressing the same underlying question of what makes history intelligible (Flynn 2005). On this reading, Foucault's histories should not be assimilated to Hayden White's narrativist model of emplotment (a genre-question already introduced through White's own "anti-history" reading of *The Order of Things*; White 1973); rather, Foucault substitutes mapping for narrative, seeking to "render history intelligible without appeal to traditional concepts of evolution, development, or even influence." Flynn's "axial" interpretation — charting Foucault's career along three axes of knowledge-truth, power-governmentality, and subjectification-ethics rather than as a linear development — is offered as a complement to, rather than a replacement for, developmental readings, and it leaves unresolved whether the ethical-subjectivation axis is merely one option among three or the culminating concern that retrospectively unifies Foucault's oeuvre. This tension over the status of the ethical subject within an ostensibly anti-humanist history recurs as an unresolved seam in Flynn's reconstruction rather than a settled result.

A second placement exercise approaches Foucault from the analytic and political-theory side of philosophy of history rather than from narrativist theory. The 2024 *Journal of the Philosophy of History* special issue pairs Foucault with Hannah Arendt, using her as a comparison case internal to twentieth-century political philosophy to ask what "doing genealogy" as history actually requires. Owen (2024) argues that Arendt and Foucault can both be read as practicing genealogy, tracing their shared background in Heidegger and Nietzsche and a common dissatisfaction with the lack of historical groundedness in Heidegger's own philosophy, and stages this convergence as a basis for comparing their diagnoses of political modernity — treating the two thinkers as convergent rather than methodologically opposed practitioners of historically grounded critique. Neill's introduction to the special issue frames Arendt and Foucault as jointly posing a challenge to mainstream philosophy of history, given their shared resistance to standard categories of historical explanation (Neill 2024). The issue's placement of Foucault within a mainstream philosophy-of-history venue, alongside a canonical political philosopher, marks one of the clearer institutional signals of his reception within that field specifically, as opposed to critical theory or history-of-science venues.

What is comparatively thin in this literature is direct, sustained comparison with Frank Ankersmit's narrativist theory of history; most comparative work pairs Foucault with White or with Arendt rather than with Ankersmit, leaving the Foucault–Ankersmit relation an underexplored pairing in the discoverable literature.

### 6.2 "History of the present" as a historiographical program

Foucault's own retrospective self-description of his project as a "history of the present" — introduced in Section 1.3 via Roth's early continuity thesis that archaeology and genealogy share a single logic connecting historical description to present critique (Roth 1981) — has since been reactivated as an active, extendable research method rather than treated as a settled slogan. The edited collection *Foucault and the History of Our Present* tests Foucault's claim that the history of the present should be the starting point for historical enquiry against contemporary domains including freedom, security, war, migration, incarceration, and digital media, asking what it means to write histories of "multiple presents" rather than a single continuous one (Fuggle, Lanci, and Tazzioli 2015). The volume's contributors extend rather than philosophically defend the method, demonstrating its continued viability through uptake across disciplines roughly forty years after Foucault articulated it.

Irrera's more recent archival study deepens this continuity claim by tracing the concept of *actualité* — the "ontology of the present" of Foucault's late lectures — back to his newly published *Le Discours philosophique* and his reflections on the status of philosophical discourse from the mid-1960s onward (Irrera 2024). On this reconstruction, *actualité* was already shaping an archaeological framework for treating philosophy itself as an object of historicization well before the explicitly genealogical "history of the present" texts, supporting a continuity reading against interpretations that treat the history of the present as a late, discontinuous turn — and showing that the history-of-the-present legacy continues to generate new interpretive work as previously unpublished material becomes available.

Hartog's *Regimes of Historicity*, though not a study of Foucault, supplies the wider historiographical-theoretical vocabulary against which Foucault's presentism is now routinely read: a "presentist" regime in which past and future are absorbed into an unending present, following an earlier future-oriented "modern" regime that dominated from the French Revolution to roughly 1989 (Hartog 2015). Read alongside Fuggle et al. and Irrera, Hartog's regimes-of-historicity vocabulary clarifies what is distinctive, and what is not, about Foucault's own history-of-the-present method, situating it within a broader epochal diagnosis of presentism developed independently of Foucauldian scholarship.

### 6.3 Genealogy as critique — the live legacy debate

The third and most contested afterlife concerns whether genealogy, as a mode of tracing contingent historical emergence, constitutes critique at all. Geuss poses the foundational problem: does tracing a concept's, practice's, or institution's contingent historical emergence automatically undermine its authority, or does genealogy require additional normative argument to carry debunking force (Geuss 2002)? Geuss's skeptical, clarificatory position — that genealogy alone may not suffice as critique — functions as the foil against which subsequent, more affirmative reconstructions of genealogy's critical power are framed.

Koopman and Matza diagnose how genealogy has actually traveled across the humanities and social sciences since Foucault, distinguishing between importing his substantive concepts (power, discipline, biopolitics) and genuinely practicing his analytics (the methodological orientations of archaeology, genealogy, and problematization); they argue scholars have far more often adopted the former, producing diminishing methodological returns, and urge a return to analytic practice over conceptual borrowing (Koopman and Matza 2013). This concept-versus-analytic diagnosis of superficial uptake echoes, at the level of reception, the genre-ambiguity problem Megill identifies in Section 4.2 regarding Foucault's own texts: just as historians and philosophers can apply incompatible genre expectations to the same primary texts, later appropriators can adopt Foucauldian vocabulary while bypassing the method that vocabulary was meant to enact.

The normativity pressure this raises is pressed directly against Koopman's own affirmative reconstruction of genealogy as a transformative, problematizing method of critique (Koopman 2013; introduced in Section 2.2). Allen's commentary asks whether that account can secure genuinely normative purchase without covertly relying on the transcendental or quasi-foundational commitments genealogy is supposed to avoid (Allen 2014) — an internal critical dialogue that presses the normativity objection against an affirmative reconstruction rather than resolving it. Harcourt's more recent intervention responds to the resulting proliferation of "genealogy" across critical theory by proposing that the term "critical genealogy" be reserved for genealogical practices that positively contribute to transforming ourselves, others, and society, evaluated by that transformative criterion rather than by formal method alone (Harcourt 2024) — a reformist move that implicitly concedes not every genealogical study advances the goals of critical philosophy.

Genealogy's geographic and disciplinary travel is tested directly by Nigh and Erlenbusch-Anderson, who examine Colombian philosopher Santiago Castro-Gómez's systematic use of Foucauldian inquiry for decolonial study and find that applying the method beyond its European site of articulation requires significant revision of Foucauldian concepts, categories, and methods rather than straightforward transplantation (Nigh and Erlenbusch-Anderson 2020) — affirming genealogy's productive extension into decolonial theory while insisting the transfer necessarily transforms the method. Finally, Lorenzini's *The Force of Truth* offers the most recent synthetic attempt to consolidate these terms, reconstructing critique, genealogy, and parrhēsia (truth-telling) as a unified late-Foucauldian project in which historical critique draws its force from an ethics and politics of truth-telling rather than functioning as a purely descriptive historiographical technique (Lorenzini 2023). This consolidation itself testifies to the absence of settlement: a synthesis is offered precisely because the underlying questions — whether genealogy is properly critical or merely descriptive, and whether it can travel intact — remain live.

Taken together, this cluster demonstrates that genealogy's legitimacy and scope remain structurally contested at the level of legacy just as Megill diagnosed structural ambiguity at the level of Foucault's own practice (Section 4.2): the same text or method can be read as rigorous critique, as diminished conceptual borrowing, as normatively question-begging, or as a portable analytic requiring transformation — and the literature has not converged on which reading is correct, only on naming and refining the terms of the disagreement.

Across all three subsections, Foucault emerges as a reference point whose historiographical status is repeatedly re-litigated rather than fixed: placed alongside Sartre, White, and Arendt as a rival theorist of historical reason; reactivated as an open method for writing histories of the present; and contested as either an authentic or a diluted practice of critique wherever it travels.
