## Introduction

Methodology in the history of philosophy is not a housekeeping matter for specialists. It concerns what philosophical understanding itself requires and what the philosophical past is capable of offering to the present. Richard Rorty (1984, 49) stated the problem with characteristic economy: historians of philosophy face a dilemma between either anachronistically imposing "enough of our problems and vocabulary on the dead to make them conversational partners," or confining interpretive activity to making "their falsehoods look less silly by placing them in the context of the benighted times in which they were written." Every methodological choice in the field negotiates this dilemma, whether or not the practitioner acknowledges it.

The two poles that have organized the debate for four decades are well-established. Appropriationism, or rational reconstruction, treats past philosophers as interlocutors in current debates, assessing their arguments for validity and extracting insights for live philosophical problems; the criterion of success is philosophical illumination rather than historical fidelity. Contextualism, or historical reconstruction, insists that the meaning of a philosophical text is fixed by its linguistic and intellectual context of utterance; to understand a past thinker is to recover what that thinker was doing in saying what they said, not to project present concerns backward onto their texts. Rorty's 1984 essay "The Historiography of Philosophy: Four Genres" gave the appropriationist pole its canonical formulation; Quentin Skinner's 1969 "Meaning and Understanding in the History of Ideas" provided the founding statement of contextualism, diagnosing three systematic "mythologies" — the mythology of doctrines, of coherence, and of prolepsis — that distort historical interpretation when context is ignored (Skinner 1969).

The debate has a canonical origin, a sharp recent flashpoint, and an authoritative retrospective. The founding documents are Rorty (1984) and Skinner (1969); both are collected and extended in the volume Rorty, Schneewind, and Skinner edited together (1984), which established the pluralist framing that subsequent literature has contested or defended. The debate entered a new phase in 2015, when the Journal of the History of Philosophy published an exchange between Daniel Garber and Michael Della Rocca over the interpretation of Spinoza. Della Rocca (2015) defended an unapologetically appropriationist reading organized around the principle of sufficient reason, arguing that contextual information can mislead and that what matters is argument validity. Garber's contextualist response insisted on recovering Spinoza's own conceptual framework rather than recasting it in contemporary terms. Christia Mercer's 2019 retrospective on "the contextualist revolution" in early modern studies provided an authoritative gloss on this exchange, tracing the shift by which contextualism displaced rational reconstruction as the dominant methodology in the field since the 1980s and introducing the concept of the "getting things right constraint" (GTRC) — the methodological norm, broadly shared among contextualists, that forbids attributing claims to historical figures without concern for whether those figures would recognize the claims as their own (Mercer 2019).

This literature review traces four interlocking threads generated by that dispute. The first and central thread is the debate itself: the two poles, the strongest versions of each, and the recent proliferation of hybrid and "third-way" positions that argue the binary is unstable or misconceived. The second thread is the question of aims: why engage with the philosophical past at all, and whether the answer to that question is prior to any answer about method. The third thread is the conceptual map surrounding the debate — the distinctions between history of philosophy, history of ideas, and intellectual history; the analytic–continental divide; and the hermeneutic tradition — which situate the central dispute within a broader methodological terrain. The fourth thread concerns concrete craft problems — the principles of charity and anachronism, and the politics of canon formation — where the abstract debate becomes operational and where feminist and decolonial critiques reveal that the dispute about how to study the philosophical past also implicates the question of whose past is being studied.

Two edited volumes by Mogens Laerke, Justin Smith, and Eric Schliesser function as the gravitational center of contemporary discussion. Philosophy and Its History (Laerke, Smith, and Schliesser 2013) assembled essays from scholars representing the full spectrum of methodological commitments, framing the enterprise as an exercise in pluralism rather than doctrinal resolution. The successor volume, The Methodology of the History of Philosophy (Smith, Schliesser, and Laerke 2022), extends the conversation beyond early modern philosophy to questions of global philosophy and the institutional contexts of historiographical practice. These volumes supply both the field's current center of gravity and the organizing ambition — moving beyond the binary — that the following sections assess critically.
