## Introduction

Methodology in the history of philosophy is not a housekeeping matter for specialists. It concerns what philosophical understanding itself requires and what the philosophical past is capable of offering to the present. Richard Rorty (1984, 49) stated the problem with characteristic economy: historians of philosophy face a dilemma between either anachronistically imposing "enough of our problems and vocabulary on the dead to make them conversational partners," or confining interpretive activity to making "their falsehoods look less silly by placing them in the context of the benighted times in which they were written." Every methodological choice in the field negotiates this dilemma, whether or not the practitioner acknowledges it.

The two poles that have organized the debate for four decades are well-established. Appropriationism, or rational reconstruction, treats past philosophers as interlocutors in current debates, assessing their arguments for validity and extracting insights for live philosophical problems; the criterion of success is philosophical illumination rather than historical fidelity. Contextualism, or historical reconstruction, insists that the meaning of a philosophical text is fixed by its linguistic and intellectual context of utterance; to understand a past thinker is to recover what that thinker was doing in saying what they said, not to project present concerns backward onto their texts. Rorty's 1984 essay "The Historiography of Philosophy: Four Genres" gave the appropriationist pole its canonical formulation; Quentin Skinner's 1969 "Meaning and Understanding in the History of Ideas" provided the founding statement of contextualism, diagnosing three systematic "mythologies" — the mythology of doctrines, of coherence, and of prolepsis — that distort historical interpretation when context is ignored (Skinner 1969).

The debate has a canonical origin, a sharp recent flashpoint, and an authoritative retrospective. The founding documents are Rorty (1984) and Skinner (1969); both are collected and extended in the volume Rorty, Schneewind, and Skinner edited together (1984), which established the pluralist framing that subsequent literature has contested or defended. The debate entered a new phase in 2015, when the Journal of the History of Philosophy published an exchange between Daniel Garber and Michael Della Rocca over the interpretation of Spinoza. Della Rocca (2015) defended an unapologetically appropriationist reading organized around the principle of sufficient reason, arguing that contextual information can mislead and that what matters is argument validity. Garber's contextualist response insisted on recovering Spinoza's own conceptual framework rather than recasting it in contemporary terms. Christia Mercer's 2019 retrospective on "the contextualist revolution" in early modern studies provided an authoritative gloss on this exchange, tracing the shift by which contextualism displaced rational reconstruction as the dominant methodology in the field since the 1980s and introducing the concept of the "getting things right constraint" (GTRC) — the methodological norm, broadly shared among contextualists, that forbids attributing claims to historical figures without concern for whether those figures would recognize the claims as their own (Mercer 2019).

This literature review traces four interlocking threads generated by that dispute. The first and central thread is the debate itself: the two poles, the strongest versions of each, and the recent proliferation of hybrid and "third-way" positions that argue the binary is unstable or misconceived. The second thread is the question of aims: why engage with the philosophical past at all, and whether the answer to that question is prior to any answer about method. The third thread is the conceptual map surrounding the debate — the distinctions between history of philosophy, history of ideas, and intellectual history; the analytic–continental divide; and the hermeneutic tradition — which situate the central dispute within a broader methodological terrain. The fourth thread concerns concrete craft problems — the principles of charity and anachronism, and the politics of canon formation — where the abstract debate becomes operational and where feminist and decolonial critiques reveal that the dispute about how to study the philosophical past also implicates the question of whose past is being studied.

Two edited volumes by Mogens Laerke, Justin Smith, and Eric Schliesser function as the gravitational center of contemporary discussion. Philosophy and Its History (Laerke, Smith, and Schliesser 2013) assembled essays from scholars representing the full spectrum of methodological commitments, framing the enterprise as an exercise in pluralism rather than doctrinal resolution. The successor volume, The Methodology of the History of Philosophy (Smith, Schliesser, and Laerke 2022), extends the conversation beyond early modern philosophy to questions of global philosophy and the institutional contexts of historiographical practice. These volumes supply both the field's current center of gravity and the organizing ambition — moving beyond the binary — that the following sections assess critically.

## Section 1: The Central Debate: Rational Reconstruction, Contextualism, and the Instability of the Binary

### 1.1 The Appropriationist Pole

The appropriationist position holds that past philosophers are best understood as contributors to philosophical problems that are, in some sense, perennial — that the validity of an argument, the soundness of an inference, and the truth of a conclusion are not relativized to the historical moment of their production. Rorty (1984) gave this position its most discussed formulation by distinguishing four genres of historiographical writing: rational reconstruction, historical reconstruction, Geistesgeschichte, and doxography. He defended rational reconstruction — the practice of treating past thinkers as colleagues with whom we can "exchange views" — as philosophically legitimate, even while acknowledging that it necessarily distorts the historical record. The distortion, for Rorty, is the price of philosophical illumination, not a methodological defect. What matters is what a text can be made to yield for live philosophical debate, not what it was originally meant to accomplish.

This position is enacted, with less qualification than Rorty allowed himself, in Della Rocca's (2015) reading of Spinoza in the pages of the Journal of the History of Philosophy. Della Rocca deploys the principle of sufficient reason as a unifying key to unlock Spinoza's entire system — metaphysics, epistemology, moral philosophy — arguing that organizing interpretation around this rationalist principle reveals Spinoza's thought as coherent and philosophically powerful. On his account, contextual recovery of what Spinoza's contemporaries believed can positively mislead: what matters is whether the argument holds, whether the inference from the PSR to Spinoza's idealist and determinist conclusions is valid. The real is the rational. This exchange, read alongside Garber's contextualist response in the same issue, provides the sharpest recent instance of both poles in practice.

Frede (2021) represents a more qualified version of appropriationism that he calls "philosophical history of philosophy." He argues that past philosophers were addressing genuine philosophical problems — problems about knowledge, being, and value — and that these problems are recoverable as philosophical rather than merely historical. Yet Frede is candid about the "inherent dangers and considerable limitations" of retrospective history: reading history through the lens of a currently favored position yields a selective and "somewhat distorted picture." The philosophical historian must choose a contemporary position as a vantage point, and that choice shapes what they find. Frede's concession is structurally significant: appropriationism, on its own most sophisticated account, cannot claim to recover anything unmediated.

### 1.2 The Contextualist Challenge

The opposing position was given systematic form by Skinner (1969), who identified three "mythologies" that distort the history of ideas: the mythology of doctrines (reading past thinkers as having views on questions they never addressed), the mythology of coherence (constructing artificially consistent systems from fragmentary texts), and the mythology of prolepsis (treating earlier thinkers as anticipating later ones). Against all three, Skinner argues that the meaning of a historical text is constituted by the illocutionary act the author performs in producing it — and that act can only be recovered by situating the utterance within the linguistic conventions and intellectual context available to the author. To understand what Hobbes or Locke was doing requires knowing what speech acts were available in their linguistic setting, what questions were live, what positions were in play. Skinner's three mythologies are precisely the errors that result from ignoring this requirement.

In the context of the history of philosophy specifically, Garber (2005) develops the contextualist case with the claim that understanding past philosophers on their own terms is itself a philosophical activity, not merely a scholarly one, because it "reveals the contingency of our current philosophical assumptions." He distinguishes "philosophy done historically" — which he endorses — from "history done philosophically," which he treats as a confusion of aims. For Garber, the appropriationist who reads Spinoza as a present-day rationalist does not encounter Spinoza but a projection of contemporary concerns; what is lost is precisely the philosophical strangeness of a thinker working within radically different conceptual constraints. This strangeness, Garber suggests, is itself philosophically instructive — but only if preserved, not dissolved into the vocabulary of current debate. The Skinner-to-Garber transfer is the hinge at which a thesis about intellectual history becomes a claim about what philosophy specifically stands to gain from attending to its past.

### 1.3 Critiques of Both Poles and the Third Way

The most active recent literature does not divide cleanly between the two positions described above but argues that the dichotomy is unsustainable — and that what replaces it is contested.

Lamb (2009) mounts a focused critique of Skinner's mature contextualism as articulated in Visions of Politics (Skinner 2002), arguing that Skinner's case against treating past texts as contributions to perennial debates is not merely unpersuasive but structurally self-defeating. The arguments Skinner marshals against perennialism, Lamb contends, replicate the very methodological errors Skinner diagnosed in his 1969 critique of earlier approaches. If treating texts as answers to timeless questions commits the mythology of doctrines, Skinner's own prohibition on perennial questions imports a myth of historical discontinuity that is no less dogmatic. The implication is that engaging past philosophy for its bearing on present questions is not only permissible but often illuminating — and that Skinner's anti-perennialism lacks the principled justification it claims.

Blau (2020) traces the dispute to a conceptual ambiguity at its source. He argues that Skinner's 1969 article conflated distinct senses of "meaning," specifically by failing to recognize what Blau calls "extended meaning" — the philosophical or theoretical significance of an argument that runs beyond the author's communicative intention. Contextualists prioritize authorial meaning recoverable from linguistic context; appropriationists prioritize extended meaning assessed by philosophical criteria. Blau's point is not that one type of meaning is correct but that previous frameworks, Skinner's included, have been blind to the full range: "the main types of meaning are not alternatives." By distinguishing them, Blau opens space for a methodology that is neither purely contextual nor purely reconstructive.

A distinct third-way candidate is developed by Hangai (2023), drawing on the model of Frede and Mandelbaum. Hangai argues that an "internal history of philosophy" can acknowledge contextual factors without making them primary determinants of meaning. His objection to contextualism is structural: a contextualism that admits any cultural factor as potentially relevant either obliterates interpretive correctness (by multiplying indeterminate contexts) or collapses into complete historical explanation, dissolving history of philosophy into general history. The alternative he proposes prioritizes philosophical reasoning and appeals to contextual factors in a "principled, supplementary way when it is necessary to supplement philosophical considerations." This is not appropriationism — it does not read past figures as addressing our questions — but neither is it Skinnerian contextualism: philosophical reasons, not linguistic conventions, are the primary determinants of interpretation.

Ferreira da Silva (2022) approaches the debate from an unusual angle, drawing on Brentano's "Portraitmaler" conception of the historian of philosophy. On this view, the historian must accurately portray a past figure's thought — which demands fidelity to what the figure actually held — but portraiture is not mere transcription: it requires the interpretive resources to discern what is philosophically significant in the figure. Ferreira da Silva shows that this Brentanian position is structured around what Mercer (2019) later calls the "Getting Things Right Constraint" (GTRC) — the prohibition on attributing to a historical figure claims they would not recognize as their own — but that the GTRC "conceals a great number of tough questions" about what "getting things right" consists in. The principle that both sides nominally accept turns out to be itself a site of substantive disagreement.

Antognazza (2015) argues for a both/and position in which historical rigor and philosophical engagement are mutually reinforcing rather than competing demands. Deep engagement with the philosophical past trains the mind to think outside current orthodoxies, exposes the contingency of present categories, and demonstrates that "latest is not always best." The history of philosophy has, on her account, an "innovative and subversive potential" — but realizing this potential requires getting the history right, not recasting past figures in contemporary terms.

Two further positions illustrate the range of third-way options. Corkum (2023) argues against the view that the cognitive contributions of historical scholarship are dispensable because replicable by nonhistorical means. Drawing an analogy with scientific cognitive values, he argues that the values of novelty and conservativeness — which influence theory choice, methodology selection, and research agenda — can only be properly assessed with a historically informed evaluation. This role for history is neither contingent nor replicable: it is ineliminable. Corkum's argument is notable for being neither contextualist nor appropriationist but rather a claim about the constitutive cognitive function of historical inquiry within philosophical practice. Beaney (2020) takes a different route, identifying "two dogmas of analytic historiography" — the belief that rational reconstruction is the appropriate method for analytic historians and the belief that contextual reconstruction is the appropriate method for proper historians — and arguing, in a Quinean spirit, that both dogmas are false and mutually dependent. Good historiography requires integrating philosophical and historical concerns because the justification of a philosophical claim and the genetic account of its origin are not neatly separable.

The French "historiographical Spinozism" traced by Laerke (2019) from Delbos through Guéroult and Vernière to Moreau represents a distinct living tradition that does not map onto either Anglophone pole. What Laerke identifies as distinctive about this lineage is the fusion of methodology and object: it uses Spinoza's own philosophical method as a guide to interpreting Spinoza. This is neither Cambridge contextualism nor analytic appropriationism; it is a form of methodological pluralism that has operated independently of the Anglophone debate, illustrating that the binary itself is, in part, a product of a specific disciplinary geography.

Mercer (2019), surveying the outcome of these debates within early modern scholarship, argues that "rational reconstructionism is a thing of the past" and that early modernists are now devoted to deploying all available skills — philosophical and scholarly — to maximize understanding of an increasingly broad range of texts. The norm she identifies as now broadly shared among contextualists is the GTRC, which prohibits attributing to a historical figure claims they would not recognize as their own. Yet Mercer's verdict is itself contestable. Live defenses of appropriationist practice — Della Rocca's Spinoza, Frede's philosophical history, Corkum's cognitive-values argument, Hangai's internal history — are not adequately characterized as the residue of an obsolete methodology. They represent distinct and reasoned positions about what understanding a past text consists in. What Mercer's retrospective does establish is that the field's center of gravity has shifted: the burden of proof now lies with those who would detach philosophical engagement from historical fidelity, not with those who insist on both.

The overall picture that emerges from the recent literature is that the poles are conceptually clear but the dichotomy between them is untenable. The field's frontier is populated by a family of third-way positions that share a rejection of both pure rational reconstruction and pure contextual immersion, while disagreeing substantially about what should replace them. Whether Hangai's internal history, Beaney's integrative methodology, Corkum's cognitive-value argument, or Ferreira da Silva's GTRC-analysis provides the most principled resolution remains an open question — and it is partly because the resolution depends on prior commitments about what the history of philosophy is for that the binary resists settlement by methodological argument alone.

## Section 2: Why Do It At All: Aims as Prior to Method

The appropriationism/contextualism debate is typically presented as a dispute about interpretive method: should the historian of philosophy read past texts as contributions to present argument, or recover their meaning within the author's context? What this framing obscures is that the choice of method is rarely prior to a choice about what history of philosophy is supposed to accomplish. Van Ackeren (2018) makes the logical relationship explicit: the question of function is prior to the question of method. Before asking how to do history of philosophy, we must ask why to do it at all and what we hope to achieve. Different answers generate not just different methods but different criteria of success — and this is precisely why the methodological binary resists principled resolution. The debate about method is, to a significant degree, a proxy for a deeper disagreement about aims.

### 2.1 The Spectrum from Dispensable to Constitutive

Van Ackeren (2018) maps the logical space of functional positions as a three-point spectrum: history of philosophy is either dispensable to current philosophy, useful but not strictly necessary, or constitutive of it. This taxonomy clarifies what is really at stake. If history is merely dispensable — a source of intellectual entertainment or historical curiosity — then methodological laxity is inconsequential. If history is constitutive, however, then how it is done carries direct philosophical weight, because errors in historical practice propagate into philosophical self-understanding.

The constitutive end of the spectrum is defended most forcefully by Williams (2000), who argues that philosophy, unlike the natural sciences, should be understood as a humanistic discipline. The distinction matters because scientific disciplines can outgrow their histories: once a problem is solved, its history becomes merely antiquarian. Humanistic disciplines cannot follow this pattern because their core concepts are themselves historically constituted — philosophy's questions and vocabulary are shaped by the accumulated tradition of inquiry, not by a transparent relationship to a mind-independent domain. Williams draws the implication that understanding contemporary philosophical problems requires understanding the history of the concepts in which those problems are posed. On this view, doing philosophy properly without attending to its history is not merely incomplete but a form of conceptual naivety.

Cohen (1986) presses a similar constitutive claim — that doing philosophy just is doing its history, because philosophical problems are defined through the history of attempts to address them — though this claim must be attributed cautiously since the paper lacks a recoverable abstract. The force of the constitutive position is that it transforms history of philosophy from an optional supplement to philosophy into something internal to philosophical practice itself.

At the opposite end, the instrumentalist reading treats history as advantageous rather than necessary. Glock (2008) offers the most detailed defense of this position, which he calls "pragmatic historicism": history of philosophy is not indispensable to systematic philosophizing, but it is valuable because it expands the range of available positions, clarifies the dialectical landscape of a problem, and guards against the reinvention of already-diagnosed errors. Glock's pragmatic historicism also contains a substantive methodological thesis: it is possible — and philosophically preferable — to approach historical texts in a critical, substantive spirit, assessing argument quality rather than merely reconstructing context, and this analytic engagement need not collapse into anachronism. In this way his functional claim (history is advantageous) directly licenses a methodological one (rational reconstruction, carefully practiced, is legitimate).

Between these poles, Nichols (2006) subjects the standard instrumentalist justifications to critical scrutiny and finds most of them wanting. Arguments that history expands the philosopher's repertoire, or provides dialectical resources for present debate, or illuminates problems through their genealogy are, Nichols argues, typically too weak or too vague to stand as independent justifications. The implication is unsettling: if the standard instrumental justifications fail, the positive case for history of philosophy must rest either on constitutive claims (which are philosophically demanding) or on more specific functional roles that go beyond generic utility.

Watson (2002) stakes out a position that cuts across the instrumental/constitutive division by arguing that history of philosophy is an autonomous discipline with its own methods and aims — neither a branch of philosophy nor a branch of general history, but a distinct enterprise whose subject matter (the historical development of philosophical argument) gives it permanent relevance. The disciplinary-autonomy view has the advantage of insulating history of philosophy from the demand that it justify itself in terms of its contribution to present-day systematic philosophy, but it also weakens the connection between historical practice and philosophical stakes. Frede (2021b) traces how this institutionalization of history of philosophy as a scholarly discipline produced benefits (precision, rigor) alongside costs (the tendency to treat the enterprise as scholarship for its own sake, severed from philosophical engagement). The discipline-as-hybrid view — neither purely philosophy nor purely history — attempts to navigate between scholarly rigor and philosophical relevance, but it defers rather than resolves the question of which aim should govern when the two pull apart.

### 2.2 Distinctive Critical Functions: Defamiliarization, Genealogy, Therapy

Beyond the spectrum from dispensable to constitutive lies a cluster of more specific functional proposals that assign history of philosophy a distinctive critical or therapeutic role. These proposals matter for the methodological debate because they cut across the appropriationist/contextualist binary in instructive ways.

Perler (2018) draws on Brecht's theatrical notion of the Verfremdungseffekt to articulate a defamiliarization function for history of philosophy. When historical inquiry is done well — when it recovers the precise context within which a philosophical problem was framed, the assumptions on which it depended, and the alternatives that were available — it renders present philosophical commitments visible as contingent. What seemed natural or necessary is revealed as one historically conditioned option among others. This defamiliarization is genuinely philosophical: it disrupts the unquestioned acceptance of present frameworks and opens space for critical reflection. Crucially, Perler's defamiliarization function requires contextual accuracy — one cannot make present assumptions look contingent without accurately recovering the historical alternatives — yet it serves an aim that appropriationists recognize as philosophical: illuminating the present by estranging it. The defamiliarization function thus demands contextual rigor in the service of a critical-philosophical goal, complicating any sharp alignment between contextualist method and antiquarian aim.

Williams (2000) and Geuss (2017) develop a related genealogical function. For both, the history of philosophy serves as a tool for critiquing the present by tracing the contingent, often power-laden processes through which current philosophical concepts and practices came to seem natural or inevitable. Geuss (2017) traces the history of philosophy as a series of contextually embedded responses to shifting human situations — responses shaped by relationships to political power, religious practice, and scientific authority — arguing that genealogy reveals the contingency and power-relatedness of philosophical practices in ways that straightforward systematic philosophy cannot. Williams's genealogical method similarly aims not at historical reconstruction for its own sake but at a philosophically productive defamiliarization: tracing how philosophical concepts were historically constituted illuminates their present employment and potential limitations. The genealogical approach thus presupposes contextual sensitivity while remaining oriented toward present philosophical self-understanding — a functional combination that the simple binary cannot capture.

Fischer (2011) identifies a therapeutic function that operates differently again. Drawing on the tradition from the Hellenistic schools through Wittgenstein, therapeutic philosophy uses historical inquiry to dissolve pseudo-problems: tracing the genealogy of a philosophical problem can reveal that it arises from linguistic or conceptual confusion rather than genuine difficulty, and this revelation dissolves rather than solves the problem. Here the historical investigation is philosophically productive precisely because it is diagnostic — it shows how a problem came to seem intractable — and the diagnosis is also the treatment.

Piercey (2009) surveys the landscape of these distinctive uses through readings of Heidegger, Gadamer, MacIntyre, and Rorty, arguing that each embodies a different conception of what the philosophical past is a resource for. Heidegger uses history for Destruktion of metaphysical prejudice; Gadamer treats the tradition as a horizon to be worked through; MacIntyre employs historical comparison as a standard of rational superiority between traditions; Rorty reads the past for occasions of edifying conversation. Piercey's comparative analysis underscores that the "uses of the past" are genuinely plural — there is no single correct answer to the functional question — and that each use implies a different conception of philosophy's aims and audience.

The most recent attempt to draw a meta-philosophical conclusion from this plurality is Ooi (2024), who connects the aims debate to epistemological permissivism — the thesis that, for a given body of evidence, multiple incompatible doxastic attitudes can be equally rational. Ooi argues that the complexity of historical interpretation motivates hermeneutical permissivism about methodological aims: there is no single correct aim for history of philosophy, and rational reconstruction, historical reconstruction, defamiliarization, and therapeutic dissolution may each be equally legitimate objectives for different practitioners pursuing different intellectual goals. On this view, the appropriationism/contextualism debate presupposes a monism about aims that may simply be false.

The force of the permissivist move is also its vulnerability. If multiple aims are equally legitimate, it becomes unclear how to adjudicate disputes within a single interpretive project — disputes about whether a particular reading of Spinoza, say, gets things right or wrong. Haakonssen (2006) approaches the same issue from a different angle, arguing for period-sensitive pluralism: the appropriate method for doing history of philosophy depends on the character of the philosophy being studied. The methodological diversity that eighteenth-century philosophy demands is not the same as what medieval or ancient philosophy demands. This is a more constrained pluralism than Ooi's, but it still resists the idea that a single methodological decision procedure governs all historiographical practice.

### 2.3 Section Summary

The functional spectrum from "dispensable" to "constitutive" maps a set of commitments that silently govern where a scholar lands on the appropriationist/contextualist axis. Williams's constitutivism entails that historical accuracy is not optional; Glock's pragmatic historicism licenses selective reconstruction; the defamiliarization and genealogical functions require contextual rigor in the service of critical philosophical aims. When Perler's defamiliarization needs contextual accuracy to produce a genuinely philosophical effect, and when Geuss's genealogy requires historical sensitivity to serve as a critique of the present, the claim that contextualist method belongs exclusively to antiquarian aims collapses. Conversely, Glock's and Frede's appropriationist-leaning practices are themselves aimed at a kind of philosophical self-understanding that the constitutivists endorse. What remains unresolved — and what sustains the methodological debate — is whether permissivism (Ooi) is a genuine resolution or a deferral of the normative question. If rational reconstruction and historical reconstruction are both permissible because all aims are equally rational, it is unclear what grounds criticism of any particular historiographical practice. The function-priority thesis clarifies the structure of the debate without dissolving it.

## Section 3: The Conceptual Map: History of Ideas, Cambridge School, and the Analytic–Continental Divide

The appropriationism/contextualism debate does not float free of its disciplinary surroundings. It is entangled with older disputes about what kind of enterprise the history of philosophy is — whether it is a branch of intellectual history, a species of philosophical inquiry, or something sui generis — and with the historically contingent sociology of the analytic–continental divide. Mapping these adjacencies is not merely cartographic: it reveals that both poles of the central debate are methodologically underdetermined by their own stated principles, and that some of the most persistent disagreements may be less about technique than about disciplinary identity and scholarly obligation.

### 3.1 History of Ideas vs. History of Philosophy

The demarcation problem predates the contemporary debate. In a foundational article, Mandelbaum (1965) distinguished three enterprises that are often run together: the history of ideas (concerned with tracing particular ideas — unit-ideas, in Lovejoy's vocabulary — across epochs and domains), intellectual history (concerned with the thought of an era in its social and cultural context), and history of philosophy (concerned with philosophical arguments and their validity or failure). Mandelbaum's taxonomy is neutral as between appropriationism and contextualism, but it clarifies what is at stake when historians and philosophers talk past each other: they may be conducting different enterprises while using a shared vocabulary. A historian of ideas asks what became of the concept of natural law; a historian of philosophy asks whether Grotius's arguments were sound; an intellectual historian asks what work the concept of natural law performed for seventeenth-century jurists and theologians. Each question is legitimate, but they are not the same question.

Lovejoy's own position as the founding theorist of the history of ideas turns out to be less stable than his programmatic pronouncements suggested. Catana (2010), examining Lovejoy's engagement with Bruno, argues that the boundary between history of ideas and history of philosophy was partly a strategic construction: Lovejoy's methodological declarations were designed to secure institutional independence for the new field rather than to accurately describe his actual practice, which was considerably closer to nineteenth-century history of philosophy than he acknowledged. The distinction, on Catana's reading, is as much a sociological artifact as a principled methodological divide — a finding that resonates with the broader suspicion, encountered throughout Section 1, that the poles of the historiographical debate are less clearly defined in practice than in theory. Knight (2012) responds to such criticism by proposing a rehabilitated Lovejovian methodology: unit-ideas, properly understood, are not identified by mere familial resemblance (which would be too loose) nor by strict identity across contexts (which would be too rigid), but by a more principled criterion that makes cross-contextual comparison tractable without collapsing into anachronism. Knight's defense thus positions a refined history-of-ideas approach as a middle path between Cambridge contextualism's resistance to cross-contextual comparison and appropriationism's indifference to historical specificity.

The contextualist pole, meanwhile, faces its own internal underdetermination problem. Shogimen (2016) identifies what he calls the elusiveness of context: there is no principled way to specify which context is the relevant one for interpreting a given text, because any text is simultaneously embedded in multiple, often incompatible, contexts — linguistic, institutional, biographical, political, intellectual. The Cambridge School's answer — that the relevant context is the linguistic conventions available to the author at the time of utterance — is one methodological choice, but not the uniquely correct one, and its application requires further judgments that the method itself does not supply. Shogimen's critique is not an argument for appropriationism; it is an argument that contextualism, as a method, does not deliver the determinacy its proponents sometimes claim. Read alongside Mandelbaum's taxonomy, this suggests that the demarcation questions feeding into the appropriationism/contextualism debate remain open: neither history of ideas, intellectual history, nor history of philosophy, as methodologically articulated, can claim to have resolved the question of relevant context.

### 3.2 Analytic, Continental, and Hermeneutic Self-Understandings

The different ways analytic and continental philosophers understand their relationship to their own philosophical past are not merely stylistic variations; they reflect deeper commitments about what philosophy is and what doing it well requires. Beaney (2016) traces how the founding figures of analytic philosophy — Frege, Russell, and Wittgenstein — each developed distinctive and typically dismissive historiographical self-understandings: the past was to be surpassed, not engaged. This early anti-historicism shaped a long tradition of analytic suspicion toward historical methods, even as it coexisted with canonical figure-worship (Kant, Aristotle) in analytic teaching. The "historical turn" in analytic philosophy, which Beaney documents through the debate between Soames and Glock, represents a partial reversal of this stance, producing what Glock (discussed in Section 2) calls a pragmatic historicism — history that is methodologically rigorous and philosophically engaged. Beaney argues for a reinvigorated analytic philosophy of history that combines analytical rigor with genuine historical sensitivity, but his account also reveals how much the analytic tradition's self-image has been constructed through historiographical choices that were never purely scholarly.

Simons (2001) offers a genealogy of the analytic–continental divide itself, tracing its emergence from 1899 and arguing that it was not philosophically inevitable but resulted from a series of historical contingencies — institutional, political, and intellectual. The period of maximum separation, Simons identifies as 1945–1968, followed by partial convergence. The implication for the historiography of philosophy debate is significant: if the analytic–continental rift is partly a sociological artifact rather than a principled philosophical difference, then the different historiographical self-understandings of the two traditions — analytic appropriationism on one side, continental hermeneutic engagement on the other — may reflect disciplinary sociology as much as genuine methodological divergence. Vrahimis (2020) extends this line of argument by examining pragmatism's role in debates about the divide, noting that pragmatism complicates standard narratives precisely because it shares features with both traditions, and that the historiography of the divide itself becomes a site of contestation in which different accounts serve different philosophical interests. This is a meta-level parallel to the appropriationism/contextualism debate: just as different methods of reading historical texts serve different scholarly aims, different narratives about the divide serve different contemporary purposes.

Two more specific interventions from analytic philosophy of language supply resources for mediation. Harrelson (2014) uses Brandom's inferentialist semantics to argue that the polemic between contextualists and presentists rests on a false dichotomy: inferentialism captures what is right in both positions, because texts mean what they normatively commit their authors to, which is partly a function of the inferential role of their claims (a matter of philosophical content) and partly a function of the linguistic conventions available in their context (a matter of historical circumstance). Harrelson concludes that much of Brandom's nuanced defense of presentism can be accepted by contextualists, so that inferentialism supports neither a purely presentist nor a purely contextualist approach. Similarly, Peden (2019) applies Davidson's philosophy of language — particularly his principle of charity and externalist account of meaning — to debates about historical interpretation, arguing that the Davidsonian framework dissolves certain ambiguities in Skinner's account of the relationship between meaning and authorial intention. Both interventions suggest that the binary may be partly a product of inadequate philosophy of language rather than an unavoidable methodological choice.

Perhaps the most reorienting contribution in this cluster is Frazer's (2018) argument that the historical/presentist dispute in the interpretation of classic texts is not fundamentally a methodological disagreement — a disagreement about means to an agreed end — but an ethical one: a disagreement about what responsibilities scholars have toward their historical subjects and toward contemporary audiences. On Frazer's reading, the debate is not resolvable by methodological argument alone because it is not about which approach better recovers meaning; it is about what scholars owe to the dead and to the living. This reframing shifts the terms of the dispute considerably. If Frazer is right, then the "Getting Things Right Constraint" discussed in Section 1, and the various arbitrating tools examined in Section 4, are all ultimately answerable to ethical rather than purely epistemic norms — which would explain why methodological arguments have consistently failed to settle the debate. It also resonates with van Ackeren's priority-of-function thesis: if the ethical question is which scholarly responsibilities matter most, then function is indeed prior, and method follows from that prior normative commitment.

The surrounding conceptual map, viewed as a whole, reveals both poles of the central debate as more porous and contextually dependent than their canonical statements suggest. History of ideas, intellectual history, and history of philosophy shade into one another in practice (Catana 2010; Mandelbaum 1965); contextualism faces an underdetermination problem about which context is relevant (Shogimen 2016); the analytic–continental divide that partly structures the debate is historically contingent rather than philosophically principled (Simons 2001); and the methodological disagreement may be, at its core, an ethical one about scholarly obligation (Frazer 2018). These findings feed back into Section 1's instability claim: the binary resists resolution not only because the poles are philosophically contestable but because the very categories in which the debate is framed carry unacknowledged sociological and normative freight.

## Section 4: Methodology in Practice: Charity, Anachronism, and the Politics of the Canon

The preceding sections have traced the appropriationism/contextualism debate at the level of general methodology and underlying aims. This section descends to the level of craft — to the specific interpretive tools that historians of philosophy deploy — and then escalates to the question of canon formation, where methodological choices reveal their political and epistemic consequences. The two movements are connected: as the analysis of charity and anachronism shows, the tools that are supposed to arbitrate between appropriationism and contextualism are themselves indeterminate; and as canon critique shows, the debate has been conducted over a pre-selected object whose composition is itself contestable. Together, they reinforce the review's organizing claim that the choice of method cannot be separated from prior decisions about what the history of philosophy is for and of.

### 4.1 Charity and Anachronism: Tools That Relocate Rather Than Resolve

The principle of charity is often invoked as though it were a neutral methodological lubricant capable of bridging the gap between what a historical text meant and what philosophical value it holds. The foundational formulation comes from Davidson (1984), who develops charity as a constitutive condition for all interpretation: interpreters must presuppose that speakers are largely rational and largely correct about the world in order for any interpretation to get started at all. On this account, charity is not a methodological preference but a transcendental prerequisite — without it, understanding cannot begin. Grandy (1973) offers a related but importantly different principle. Rather than maximizing truth-attribution, Grandy's "principle of humanity" requires minimizing psychological strangeness: the interpreter should attribute to historical figures the beliefs that we ourselves would hold given their evidence and inferential situation. The contrast between the two principles is significant for historians of philosophy. Davidson's charity pulls interpretation toward present truth-conditions; Grandy's principle of humanity is more sensitive to the contextual differences in evidence base that separate historical agents from modern readers, making it potentially more serviceable for historical work (both entries are INCOMPLETE; claims here follow their verified note content).

Tom Stern (2016) demonstrates why neither formulation can serve as the bridge between historical accuracy and philosophical value that charity is supposed to provide. Analyzing the interpretation of Nietzsche, Stern argues that charity is multiply ambiguous along three independent dimensions: the *unit* of interpretation (should one maximize coherence at the level of the sentence, the text, or the oeuvre?), the *mode* (should interpretation make the text consistent, make it plausible, or make it true?), and the *strength* (how far should the principle be pushed before context overrides it?). Because charity is ambiguous across all three dimensions simultaneously, invoking it does not settle interpretive disputes — it conceals them. The historian who reads charitably and the historian who reads non-charitably may disagree about unit, mode, and strength while both claiming to apply the same principle. Nietzsche is a particularly instructive case because his rhetorical style deliberately resists systematic attribution; but Stern's analysis applies wherever historians of philosophy invoke charity as though its direction were self-evident. The consequence for the appropriationism/contextualism debate is sharp: charity, far from arbitrating between the two poles, merely relocates the disagreement into its own internal ambiguities.

The related problem of anachronism has attracted a parallel range of responses. Jardine (2000), working in the history of science, develops a taxonomy of anachronistic practices and argues that not all anachronism is distorting. Some forms of retrospective attribution are indispensable for establishing the significance of historical episodes and for retrospective causal explanation; the relevant question is not whether one employs anachronism but which forms are productive and which are distorting. Pickave (2020) extends this moderate defense to the history of philosophy directly, arguing that engagement with contemporary conceptual tools need not constitute anachronism properly understood — the claim that "purely historical" reading is possible is, on his account, an illusion. Harari (2025) adds a useful further distinction between contextualization that facilitates *understanding* and contextualization that facilitates *judgment*. Examining Zeller's and Frede's historiographical approaches to ancient philosophy, she argues that even historically sensitive interpretation can produce anachronism when it deploys contextual framing to evaluate, rather than merely to understand, the figures under study. The implication is that situating philosophy in its historical context is not, by itself, a remedy for anachronistic projection — the purpose for which one contextualizes determines whether anachronism is avoided.

Kaukua and Lahteenmaki (2010) identify a third category of interpretive standard that escapes the text/context binary altogether. Working in the history of philosophical psychology, they defend what they call "non-textual standards" — heuristic postulations of features of the world or of experience that the interpreter must assume to be immune to historical variation in order for interpretation to proceed at all. Subjectivity, they argue, is one such standard: even if contemporary philosophy of mind has contested what subjectivity is, historians of philosophical psychology must presuppose some such notion to make sense of the texts they study. Non-textual standards are neither drawn from the contemporary interpreter's conceptual framework (which would be anachronism) nor recoverable from context alone (which would be pure contextualism); they occupy an intermediate position that constrains interpretation without imposing present frameworks wholesale. Schumacher's (2012) case study in the history of economics illustrates the stakes of getting this wrong. His reconstruction of how Smith's theory of international trade was doxographically retrojected as a forerunner of neoclassical theory — "Whig history" in its clearest form — shows what appropriationist anachronism looks like in concrete disciplinary practice and what understanding is lost when it is not arrested.

What emerges from this cluster of debates is that the standard interpretive tools — charity, anachronism, contextual sensitivity — cannot adjudicate the appropriationism/contextualism dispute. Each tool is, on inspection, itself a site of methodological choice. This finding supports the priority-of-aims thesis developed in Section 2: because the tools are indeterminate, the normative direction of interpretation must be supplied from outside them, by a prior commitment about what the history of philosophy is for.

### 4.2 Canon Formation and Its Critics

If interpretive tools are indeterminate, the domain to which those tools are applied is not neutral either. Canon formation is a historiographical act — a selection of texts to be interpreted — and the methodology debate has, until recently, largely presupposed the canon's legitimacy rather than subjecting it to scrutiny. Recent literature has begun to close that gap, arguing that the composition of the canon is itself the outcome of methodological and institutional choices with both epistemic and political consequences.

Shapiro (2016) traces how the entrenchment of the seven-figure early modern canon — Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz, Locke, Berkeley, Hume, Kant — reflects a history of appropriationist selection. Scholars chose these figures because they seemed most productive for live contemporary debates; but that same selection mechanism systematically excluded women philosophers and others whose work did not fit the conceptual agenda of mid-twentieth-century analytic philosophy. Shapiro argues that revisiting the canon requires more than adding omitted figures; it requires reconsidering the purposes the canon serves and the philosophical questions it is designed to illuminate. When the guiding questions change — for example, when the history of seventeenth-century philosophy of mind displaces the epistemology/metaphysics frame — different figures become philosophically central, and the exclusion of women from the standard narrative is revealed as a consequence of methodological choice rather than of historical significance.

Lapointe and Reck (2023) extend this analysis across the analytic tradition as a whole. Their central argument is that canons are historiographical constructions rather than natural reflections of philosophical merit, and that analytic philosophy's self-image as ahistorical is a myth: analytic philosophers have always engaged in canon-formation, just without acknowledging it as such. Both appropriationist and contextualist approaches shape what counts as "the tradition" in different philosophical communities; neither is exempt from the institutional and normative pressures that determine whose work is deemed worthy of study. Smith (2024) traces the specific historical mechanism through which the post-Kantian canon was assembled, focusing on Wilhelm Tennemann's historiography of philosophy in the early nineteenth century. Smith brings together three strands of recent scholarship to show that Tennemann's narrative — which established the "standard story" familiar from twentieth-century textbooks — simultaneously excluded women from the history of philosophy (following O'Neill's earlier research) and excluded non-European thought (following Park). The canon was not simply assembled by selecting the best philosophers; it was assembled by a historian of philosophy working within specific institutional and ideological conditions that determined in advance what counted as philosophy at all.

Mercer (2016) demonstrates the epistemic, not merely political, cost of canonical exclusion through a case study: she argues that Descartes' philosophical development is not fully intelligible without attending to his engagement with Teresa of Avila's work. If this influence network is correct, the exclusion of women from the canon has produced substantively distorted interpretations of canonical figures — the standard history of philosophy is not just incomplete but wrong on matters of intellectual causation (INCOMPLETE; claims follow verified note content, which is specific and attributable).

The feminist literature has also examined the internal logic of the reclamation project — the effort to recover historically excluded women — and found it insufficient on its own terms. Tyson (2014) identifies four argumentative strategies employed in feminist reclamation projects and argues that three of them unintentionally reinforce the exclusion they seek to overcome. Strategies that simply demonstrate that a woman philosopher was "really philosophical" by canonical standards, or that her work anticipates canonical developments, operate within frameworks that already presuppose which questions and which figures count as philosophically central. Only the fourth strategy — attending to the mechanisms of exclusion and transforming philosophical practice accordingly — is genuinely corrective, because it is the only one that does not treat canonical norms as fixed. Dotson (2012) connects this to the lived conditions of professional philosophy. Diagnosing a "culture of justification" that requires practitioners from marginalized groups to continually demonstrate that their work is philosophical to those who control canonical norms, she argues for a "culture of praxis" that does not treat the discipline's self-understanding as given. Dotson's analysis locates canon maintenance not primarily in methodological choices but in institutional structures that reproduce exclusion regardless of stated methodological commitments.

The decolonial and cosmopolitan challenges extend the canon-critical argument beyond the exclusion of women to the exclusion of non-European traditions. Van Norden (2017) argues that the restriction of anglophone philosophy departments to European philosophy is intellectually indefensible and constitutes epistemic injustice — an exclusion reflecting historical and political contingency rather than considered judgment about philosophical merit (INCOMPLETE; claims follow verified note content). Ganeri (2025) proposes a "cosmopolitan philosophy" framework that goes beyond comparative philosophy — which juxtaposes traditions without genuine philosophical engagement — by treating philosophical traditions from multiple cultures as equally valid objects and resources of philosophical inquiry. Ganeri's typology of cosmopolitan philosophical skills (assimilative, autonomous, comparative, bicultural, interjacent, and others) articulates what it would take for the discipline to function in a culturally polycentric world. Crucially, Ganeri's proposal is not merely additive — it is not a matter of expanding the reading list — but structural: it requires rethinking what kinds of institutionalization will best serve philosophical inquiry when that inquiry is genuinely cosmopolitan.

The section's overall finding is that the appropriationism/contextualism debate has been conducted largely over a pre-selected, contestable object. Choosing a method does not choose the canon; and the canon, once scrutinized, turns out to be the outcome of prior historiographical acts that are themselves methodologically and politically laden. An open question raised by Smith (2024) and Shapiro (2016) deserves emphasis: there is reason to think that contextualism's own historical rigor, properly applied, would resist the Eurocentric canon rather than underwrite it. If contextualism is committed to recovering the full intellectual environment of historical texts and to avoiding anachronistic projections of later frameworks onto earlier periods, then it should be committed to recovering the full range of figures who participated in those environments — including women and, on a broader scale, non-European interlocutors. The methodology that was developed to constrain appropriationist distortion may, on this reading, be the most powerful internal resource for canon expansion.

## Conclusion

The foregoing review has traced a trajectory that begins with a sharp binary and ends with a field that has largely outgrown it — though not resolved it. When Rorty (1984) and Skinner (1969) provided the canonical framings, they appeared to offer a genuine dilemma: either treat past philosophers as conversation partners whose arguments can be freely reconstructed for present purposes, or recover what those philosophers actually meant by situating their texts in the linguistic and intellectual contexts that gave them meaning. The 2015 exchange between Della Rocca and Garber showed that this dilemma retains its grip on working historians of philosophy. Yet the preponderance of recent scholarship — from Blau's (2020) diagnosis of conceptual conflation in Skinner's original article to Hangai's (2023) defense of internal history as a principled alternative to both poles — has converged on the view that the binary cannot be made to bear the normative weight assigned to it.

The most clarifying single insight to emerge from this body of literature is van Ackeren's (2018) thesis that function is prior to method. If the question of how to do history of philosophy is downstream of the question of what history of philosophy is for, then the persistence of the appropriationism-contextualism dispute is no longer puzzling: scholars who disagree about function will reach for different methods and will talk past one another when they argue at the methodological level. The aims canvassed across this literature are genuinely plural — defamiliarization and the exposure of contingency (Perler 2018), genealogical critique of the present (Williams 2000), the non-replicable cognitive role of historical evaluation (Corkum 2023), and the subversive, innovative potential of deep historical engagement (Antognazza 2015) — and these aims do not map cleanly onto one side of the binary. Perler's Brechtian defamiliarization, for instance, requires contextual accuracy to produce its philosophical payoff, yet serves a critical function that is in some respects closer to the appropriationist's aspiration to make the past speak to the present. The function-prior-to-method thesis explains why the debate resists resolution without settling what resolution would look like.

Two further findings compound this explanatory picture. First, the tools that are routinely invoked to arbitrate the debate — charity and contextual sensitivity — turn out to be indeterminate rather than decisive. Stern (2016) demonstrates that charity is multiply ambiguous along at least three dimensions (unit, mode, and strength), so that invoking it conceals rather than resolves the interpretive choices at stake. Shogimen's (2016) point that the contextualist pole faces its own underdetermination problem — which context is the relevant one? — runs parallel to Stern's critique of charity: neither arbitrating tool delivers a verdict on its own. What remains, as Frazer (2018) suggests, is an ethical rather than purely technical disagreement, a dispute about scholarly responsibility that cannot be settled by neutral methodological principle alone.

Second, canon critique has revealed that the debate has been conducted over a pre-selected and contestable object. Shapiro (2016) shows that the early modern canon of seven core figures was fixed partly by appropriationist scholarship selecting for what appeared relevant to contemporary debates, thereby systematically bypassing women and figures whose contributions did not map onto those debates. Smith (2024) traces the post-Kantian historiographical mechanisms by which the "standard story" of Western philosophy came to exclude women and non-European traditions simultaneously. Ganeri's (2025) cosmopolitan proposal goes furthest in drawing out the implication: if the discipline is to be genuinely philosophical rather than parochially institutional, it must reconstruct its object, not merely refine its methods for studying a pre-given one. The critical force of this line of argument is that choosing between contextualism and appropriationism does not choose the corpus to which those methods are applied. Choosing the corpus is a prior act — and, as Mercer's (2019) retrospective assessment implies, contextual rigor can itself be turned against Eurocentric canon formation, since attending carefully to the intellectual settings of historically marginalized figures is precisely what contextualism demands.

Three tensions remain genuinely open. The first concerns the standing of methodological permissivism. If aims are plural and each aim licenses a different method, the appropriate response may be Ooi's (2024) permissivism — the view that multiple methodological orientations are equally rationally defensible given different purposes. But permissivism risks being an evasion rather than a resolution: it licenses methodological plurality without providing tools for adjudicating within a single project when contextual and philosophical aims pull in different directions, as they commonly do. The second tension concerns Mercer's (2019) verdict that "rational reconstructionism is a thing of the past" within early modern studies. The live defenses of unapologetic appropriationism by Della Rocca (2015), the sophisticated "internal history" position of Frede (2021) and Hangai (2023), and Corkum's (2023) argument for the ineliminable cognitive role of historically informed evaluation all suggest that, whatever the dominant practice in early modern scholarship, the theoretical question of whether appropriationism can be principled has not been put to rest. The third tension — and perhaps the most productive — is whether canon expansion should be understood as external pressure on the historiographical tradition or as a demand internal to its own commitments. If contextual rigor genuinely requires attending to the intellectual worlds in which past philosophers worked, then the exclusion of Teresa of Ávila from the account of Descartes, or the exclusion of Sanskrit philosophical traditions from accounts of early modern epistemology, is a failure by contextualism's own standards (Mercer 2016; Ganeri 2025). On this reading, the decolonial and feminist challenges are not competitors to rigorous historiography but expressions of it.

What emerges from the survey as a whole is that the appropriationism-contextualism axis, the aims debate, and the canon debate are not three distinct topics requiring separate treatment. Each is a way of asking what the history of philosophy is for and of what it is a history. Progress in the field will require holding these questions together rather than pursuing methodological refinement in isolation from questions about which traditions of inquiry count as philosophy and why.
