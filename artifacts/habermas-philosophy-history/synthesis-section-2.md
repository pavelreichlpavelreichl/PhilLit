## Section 1: Social Evolution and the Reconstructive Alternative

### Subsection 1.1: Reconstructing Historical Materialism

Habermas' philosophy of history begins as an act of disciplined reconstruction rather than wholesale rejection. In *Communication and the Evolution of Society* (1979) and the essays collected in *Zur Rekonstruktion des historischen Materialismus* (1976), he retains Marx's ambition of a theoretically grounded account of social development while abandoning the explanatory framework that grounds it. The replacement is far-reaching: where Marx anchors historical change in the development of productive forces and the contradictions of relations of production, Habermas anchors it in the progressive differentiation and institutionalization of communicative competences. The model is drawn not from political economy but from developmental psychology — specifically from Piaget's genetic epistemology and Kohlberg's moral stage theory, adapted to the level of social formations rather than individual subjects. Social evolution, on this account, consists in a rationally reconstructible sequence of structural learning stages, each requiring and enabling more complex forms of communicative rationality.

The conceptual nerve of this proposal is the distinction between developmental logic and developmental dynamics, articulated most explicitly in Habermas's 1979 essay "History and Evolution." Developmental logic designates the normatively ordered sequence of structural possibilities — the logical relations that make one stage the precondition of another. Developmental dynamics designates the contingent causal and motivational mechanisms through which transitions actually occur: crises, class conflicts, institutional disruptions. Habermas is careful to insist that the logic cannot derive the dynamics; the rational reconstruction of what stages are possible and in what order does not explain why any particular society moves from one stage to the next at any particular historical moment. This distinction is intended to preserve both the normative ambition of the framework — progress can be measured — and its methodological modesty — the theory does not predict history.

The secondary literature has pressed hard on the sustainability of this distinction. Browne (2019) identifies what he calls the antinomies of the reconstruction: by displacing the paradigm of production with a theory of communication, Habermas gains conceptual clarity about the normative dimension of social development but loses engagement with the internal conflicts and creative dynamics of material production. The communication paradigm, Browne argues, generates an inflexible developmental logic that cannot accommodate the institutional variation and social creativity that a fully historical account would require. The framework orders social formations along a single normative-structural axis, with the consequence that the diversity of historical paths and the reversibility of historical achievements are difficult to conceptualize within it.

Oh (2023) presses a different internal tension: the model relies on an analogy between ontogenetic learning (individual cognitive-moral development, as theorized by Piaget and Kohlberg) and phylogenetic development (species-level learning across social formations), but the analogy is unstable. Individual learning is non-cumulative in a way that cultural learning at the phylogenetic level is not — each individual must undergo developmental stages anew, and the competences achieved are not simply inherited or stored. If learning at the species level operates differently from learning at the individual level, as Habermas himself acknowledges, then the normative sequence drawn from individual developmental psychology cannot straightforwardly ground claims about the direction of social evolution. Oh argues that Habermas underestimates this tension, with the consequence that the learning-process model is less determinate than his theory requires.

Owen (1998) raises a further challenge from a different angle: the criteria used to rank social formations in the developmental sequence — the degree of communicative rationalization, the institutionalization of discourse — are drawn from a specifically Western experience of modernity and cannot be extrapolated to non-Western societies without illicit universalization. The developmental logic, on this reading, is not a cross-cultural rational reconstruction but a Eurocentric projection of Western institutional history onto a universal scale.

Against these challenges, Kirkpatrick (2003) offers a partial defense. He argues that critics mischaracterize the nature of Habermas' claims: the developmental logic is not an empirical theory of evolutionary dynamics but a normative-rational reconstruction, more closely analogous to Kant's philosophy of history than to biological theories of evolution. On this reading, the framework is more modest than its critics suppose — it articulates the logical conditions for the realization of communicative rationality rather than predicting which societies will achieve it or when. Mejía Fernández and Romero (2022) pursue a related rehabilitation, arguing that Habermas' mature position on social evolution represents a "weak anthropological naturalism" that incorporates insights from evolutionary biology and cognitive science without reducing normative structures to natural selection. The communicative competences that anchor developmental logic have evolutionary roots, but they cannot be explained reductively: they retain a normative dimension that escapes both Kantian idealism and Darwinian naturalism.

The debate between these positions turns on a structural question that Habermas never fully resolves: whether a theory of social evolution can simultaneously claim normative universality (the developmental sequence is rationally reconstructible and cross-culturally valid) and acknowledge contingent dynamics (the sequence does not determine historical outcomes). The critics, beginning with Oh and Browne, identify this as the framework's central vulnerability; the defenders, from Kirkpatrick to Mejía Fernández and Romero, argue that the framework survives once its modesty is properly understood. This tension is not merely internal to the social evolution essays of the 1970s; it runs through *The Theory of Communicative Action* (1984; 1987), where the rationalization of the lifeworld as a historical process — a selective institutionalization of communicative reason at the expense of traditional authority — is simultaneously presented as a normative achievement and an empirical diagnosis of Western modernity.

### Subsection 1.2: The Critique of Speculative Philosophy of History

The positive theory of social evolution is constructed in the shadow of a critique. Habermas' reconstruction of historical materialism is not simply an improvement on Marx; it is part of a broader rejection of the speculative philosophy of history that runs from Hegel through Marx to the first generation of the Frankfurt School. *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity* (1987) is the locus of this critique, but its roots extend through the 1973 "Postscript to Knowledge and Human Interests" and the methodological essays of the 1970s. The critique proceeds along three axes — against Hegel's philosophy of the subject, against Marx's labour paradigm, and against Adorno and Horkheimer's total critique of reason — and in each case the argument follows the same pattern: the tradition forecloses the normative resources that critical theory requires.

Against Hegel, Habermas' charge is that the "philosophy of the subject" — the attempt to make Geist the carrier of a self-reflexive historical process — generates an antinomy. By subsuming historical multiplicity under a single self-realizing spirit, Hegel can narrate history as progress toward self-knowledge, but only at the cost of collapsing the plurality of social actors into a single metaphysical subject. The result is a philosophy of history that can neither ground normative critique (since Geist realizes itself in whatever exists) nor acknowledge the genuine contingency of historical outcomes. Communicative reason, by contrast, is irreducibly intersubjective: it cannot be attributed to a single subject, and its realization remains genuinely open. Habermas thus presents the paradigm shift from subject-centred to communicative reason as the condition for any legitimate successor to the speculative tradition.

Against Marx, the critique is more intricate. Habermas' argument, developed through the 1970s and synthesized in *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity*, is that the labour paradigm cannot ground the normative critique it is supposed to support. If the paradigmatic form of human self-constitution is labour — the transformation of nature through purposive activity — then there is no internal standard by which deformations of communicative practice can be identified and criticized. As Tang (2022) reconstructs the argument, Habermas correctly identifies this normative deficit but his own communicative-rationality alternative may reproduce some of Marx's idealist tendencies: the normative foundation in communicative reason is insufficiently attentive to the material conditions under which communication takes place. Tang's critique is that Habermas has not fully escaped the idealism he diagnoses in Marx; he has displaced it rather than eliminated it.

The most charged dimension of the critique concerns Adorno and Horkheimer. *Dialectic of Enlightenment* (Horkheimer and Adorno 2002) argues that the Enlightenment's drive toward domination of nature through instrumental reason generates the conditions for its own self-destruction: myth and enlightenment are intertwined, and the rationalization of the world produces new forms of barbarism rather than emancipation. For Habermas, this is precisely the impasse that communicative reason is designed to overcome. His charge against Adorno and Horkheimer is that by collapsing all rationality into instrumental reason, they foreclose the normative standpoint from which critique could be mounted: the total critique of reason is performatively self-contradictory, since it presupposes the validity of the critique it is making. Hohendahl (1985) traces the conceptual architecture of this disagreement in detail, examining whether Habermas' communicative rationality can do the critical work that Adorno's negative dialectics cannot, and identifies residual tensions in Habermas' account of the transition from the first to the second generation of the Frankfurt School.

Freyenhagen (2023) introduces an important revisionary element into this narrative. He argues that Horkheimer and Adorno anticipated a "linguistic turn" in critical theory before Habermas' communicative reorientation, drawing on later Wittgenstein's picture of language. If this is right, the standard narrative of a clean paradigm shift — with Habermas introducing language and intersubjectivity into a tradition that had no resources for them — requires revision. The transition involves continuity as well as rupture, and Habermas' self-presentation as inaugurating a new paradigm through his critique of Adorno and Horkheimer is at least partially contested.

Cobben (2021) introduces a further complication from the opposite direction: the late Habermas of *Auch eine Geschichte der Philosophie* (2019) partially rehabilitates Hegel as a contributor to the "paradigm of speech-philosophy." On Cobben's reading, Habermas credits Hegel with transforming Kant's noumenal subject into a free subject related to the lifeworld through the medium of ethical life, thereby broadening the concept of reason in ways that anticipate communicative philosophy. This partial rehabilitation complicates the earlier critique in *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity*: the late Habermas acknowledges a dimension of Hegel's thought that his earlier position had foreclosed. Whether this constitutes a revision of the critique or merely a more discriminating application of it is a question that runs through the secondary literature on Habermas' late work (Czingon et al. 2020; Verovšek 2020).

The broader pattern that emerges from these debates is that Habermas' critique of speculative philosophy of history is itself contested along each of its three axes. The critique of Hegel is challenged by those who argue that Hegel's concept of ethical life already contains intersubjective dimensions that Habermas claims to have introduced (Cobben 2021). The critique of Marx is challenged by those who argue that the communicative alternative reproduces idealist tendencies in a new register (Tang 2022). The critique of Adorno and Horkheimer is challenged by those who argue that the first generation of the Frankfurt School was closer to the linguistic turn than the standard narrative acknowledges (Freyenhagen 2023). These challenges do not nullify Habermas' reconstructive project, but they indicate that the negative condition for it — the critique of its predecessors — is more contested than the project's self-presentation suggests. The communicative alternative to speculative philosophy of history rests on a set of critical readings whose adequacy is itself a matter of ongoing dispute.
