## Introduction

The philosophy of history bequeaths a double legacy to its inheritors. On one side stands the ambition of the tradition: the claim that history has a rational structure, that social formations can be ranked by their achievement of freedom or reason, and that this ranking is not merely evaluative but explanatory. On the other side stands the embarrassment: Hegel's Geist, Marx's laws of history, and the teleological machinery that underwrites these claims have not survived philosophical scrutiny. The question for any successor project is whether the normative ambition can survive the collapse of its metaphysical vehicle.

Jürgen Habermas' engagement with this question spans more than five decades and constitutes one of the most sustained attempts in contemporary social philosophy to answer it affirmatively. His project is distinctive in refusing both available exits. He does not retreat to a purely descriptive social science that abandons normative-historical claims altogether, nor does he seek to repair the speculative tradition from within. Instead, he argues that the normative content of philosophy of history — the idea that societies can learn, that some social orders are more rational than others, that modernity carries unrealized emancipatory potential — can be reconstructed on entirely different foundations: not the philosophy of the subject, but the theory of communicative action.

This review traces that project from its earliest formulation to its culmination. In the essays collected in *Communication and the Evolution of Society* (Habermas 1979), Habermas first proposes replacing Marx's productive-forces model with a competence-based theory of social evolution grounded in learning processes — a move that simultaneously preserves and transforms the normative stakes of historical materialism. The full theoretical context for this account is worked out in *The Theory of Communicative Action* (Habermas 1984), where the rationalization of the lifeworld emerges as the central historical process of modernity. *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity* (Habermas 1987) provides the negative counterpart: a sustained critique of Hegel's philosophy of the subject, Marx's labour paradigm, and the Frankfurt School's total critique of reason, each of which, Habermas argues, forecloses rather than grounds normative critique. The arc culminates in *Also a History of Philosophy* (Habermas 2023), in which the entire history of Western philosophy from the Greek axial age through early modernity is reconstructed as a learning process oriented toward "rational freedom" — a project that prompts the question of whether Habermas has, at the end, reintroduced the teleological elements he spent his career criticizing. McCarthy (1978) remains the standard guide to the theoretical development through the late 1970s and establishes the coordinates within which the subsequent literature has operated.

The review proceeds in three sections. Section 1 examines the reconstructive framework itself: Habermas' theory of social evolution, the methodological distinction between developmental logic and developmental dynamics, and the critique of speculative predecessors that is its negative condition. Section 2 turns to the normative architecture — the theory of modernity as an unfinished project, discourse ethics as a proceduralist standard for assessing historical progress, and the late philosophy of history in *Auch eine Geschichte der Philosophie* — asking whether the formal-procedural core can sustain the universalist claims placed on it. Section 3 surveys the major critical challenges: postmodern and hermeneutic objections to the communicative-rationality framework, and the postcolonial charge that Habermas' developmental logic is covertly Eurocentric. Running across all three sections is a single question: whether the reconstructive method can preserve the normative ambitions of philosophy of history while genuinely abandoning its metaphysical and teleological apparatus, or whether the attempt to have it both ways generates tensions that no revision can finally resolve.

## Section 1: Social Evolution and the Reconstructive Alternative

### 1.1 Reconstructing Historical Materialism

Habermas' philosophy of history begins as an act of disciplined reconstruction rather than wholesale rejection. In *Communication and the Evolution of Society* (1979) and the essays collected in *Zur Rekonstruktion des historischen Materialismus* (1976), he retains Marx's ambition of a theoretically grounded account of social development while abandoning the explanatory framework that grounds it. The replacement is far-reaching: where Marx anchors historical change in the development of productive forces and the contradictions of relations of production, Habermas anchors it in the progressive differentiation and institutionalization of communicative competences. The model is drawn not from political economy but from developmental psychology — specifically from Piaget's genetic epistemology and Kohlberg's moral stage theory, adapted to the level of social formations rather than individual subjects. Social evolution, on this account, consists in a rationally reconstructible sequence of structural learning stages, each requiring and enabling more complex forms of communicative rationality.

The conceptual nerve of this proposal is the distinction between developmental logic and developmental dynamics, articulated most explicitly in Habermas's 1979 essay "History and Evolution." Developmental logic designates the normatively ordered sequence of structural possibilities — the logical relations that make one stage the precondition of another. Developmental dynamics designates the contingent causal and motivational mechanisms through which transitions actually occur: crises, class conflicts, institutional disruptions. Habermas is careful to insist that the logic cannot derive the dynamics; the rational reconstruction of what stages are possible and in what order does not explain why any particular society moves from one stage to the next at any particular historical moment. This distinction is intended to preserve both the normative ambition of the framework — progress can be measured — and its methodological modesty — the theory does not predict history.

The secondary literature has pressed hard on the sustainability of this distinction. Browne (2019) identifies what he calls the antinomies of the reconstruction: by displacing the paradigm of production with a theory of communication, Habermas gains conceptual clarity about the normative dimension of social development but loses engagement with the internal conflicts and creative dynamics of material production. The communication paradigm, Browne argues, generates an inflexible developmental logic that cannot accommodate the institutional variation and social creativity that a fully historical account would require. The framework orders social formations along a single normative-structural axis, with the consequence that the diversity of historical paths and the reversibility of historical achievements are difficult to conceptualize within it.

Oh (2023) presses a different internal tension: the model relies on an analogy between ontogenetic learning (individual cognitive-moral development, as theorized by Piaget and Kohlberg) and phylogenetic development (species-level learning across social formations), but the analogy is unstable. Individual learning is non-cumulative in a way that cultural learning at the phylogenetic level is not — each individual must undergo developmental stages anew, and the competences achieved are not simply inherited or stored. If learning at the species level operates differently from learning at the individual level, as Habermas himself acknowledges, then the normative sequence drawn from individual developmental psychology cannot straightforwardly ground claims about the direction of social evolution. Oh argues that Habermas underestimates this tension, with the consequence that the learning-process model is less determinate than his theory requires.

Owen (1998) raises a further challenge from a different angle: the criteria used to rank social formations in the developmental sequence — the degree of communicative rationalization, the institutionalization of discourse — are drawn from a specifically Western experience of modernity and cannot be extrapolated to non-Western societies without illicit universalization. The developmental logic, on this reading, is not a cross-cultural rational reconstruction but a Eurocentric projection of Western institutional history onto a universal scale.

Against these challenges, Kirkpatrick (2003) offers a partial defense. He argues that critics mischaracterize the nature of Habermas' claims: the developmental logic is not an empirical theory of evolutionary dynamics but a normative-rational reconstruction, more closely analogous to Kant's philosophy of history than to biological theories of evolution. On this reading, the framework is more modest than its critics suppose — it articulates the logical conditions for the realization of communicative rationality rather than predicting which societies will achieve it or when. Mejía Fernández and Romero (2022) pursue a related rehabilitation, arguing that Habermas' mature position on social evolution represents a "weak anthropological naturalism" that incorporates insights from evolutionary biology and cognitive science without reducing normative structures to natural selection. The communicative competences that anchor developmental logic have evolutionary roots, but they cannot be explained reductively: they retain a normative dimension that escapes both Kantian idealism and Darwinian naturalism.

The debate between these positions turns on a structural question that Habermas never fully resolves: whether a theory of social evolution can simultaneously claim normative universality (the developmental sequence is rationally reconstructible and cross-culturally valid) and acknowledge contingent dynamics (the sequence does not determine historical outcomes). The critics, beginning with Oh and Browne, identify this as the framework's central vulnerability; the defenders, from Kirkpatrick to Mejía Fernández and Romero, argue that the framework survives once its modesty is properly understood. This tension is not merely internal to the social evolution essays of the 1970s; it runs through *The Theory of Communicative Action* (1984; 1987), where the rationalization of the lifeworld as a historical process — a selective institutionalization of communicative reason at the expense of traditional authority — is simultaneously presented as a normative achievement and an empirical diagnosis of Western modernity.

### 1.2 The Critique of Speculative Philosophy of History

The positive theory of social evolution is constructed in the shadow of a critique. Habermas' reconstruction of historical materialism is not simply an improvement on Marx; it is part of a broader rejection of the speculative philosophy of history that runs from Hegel through Marx to the first generation of the Frankfurt School. *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity* (1987) is the locus of this critique, but its roots extend through the 1973 "Postscript to Knowledge and Human Interests" and the methodological essays of the 1970s. The critique proceeds along three axes — against Hegel's philosophy of the subject, against Marx's labour paradigm, and against Adorno and Horkheimer's total critique of reason — and in each case the argument follows the same pattern: the tradition forecloses the normative resources that critical theory requires.

Against Hegel, Habermas' charge is that the "philosophy of the subject" — the attempt to make Geist the carrier of a self-reflexive historical process — generates an antinomy. By subsuming historical multiplicity under a single self-realizing spirit, Hegel can narrate history as progress toward self-knowledge, but only at the cost of collapsing the plurality of social actors into a single metaphysical subject. The result is a philosophy of history that can neither ground normative critique (since Geist realizes itself in whatever exists) nor acknowledge the genuine contingency of historical outcomes. Communicative reason, by contrast, is irreducibly intersubjective: it cannot be attributed to a single subject, and its realization remains genuinely open. Habermas thus presents the paradigm shift from subject-centred to communicative reason as the condition for any legitimate successor to the speculative tradition.

Against Marx, the critique is more intricate. Habermas' argument, developed through the 1970s and synthesized in *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity*, is that the labour paradigm cannot ground the normative critique it is supposed to support. If the paradigmatic form of human self-constitution is labour — the transformation of nature through purposive activity — then there is no internal standard by which deformations of communicative practice can be identified and criticized. As Tang (2022) reconstructs the argument, Habermas correctly identifies this normative deficit but his own communicative-rationality alternative may reproduce some of Marx's idealist tendencies: the normative foundation in communicative reason is insufficiently attentive to the material conditions under which communication takes place. Tang's critique is that Habermas has not fully escaped the idealism he diagnoses in Marx; he has displaced it rather than eliminated it.

The most charged dimension of the critique concerns Adorno and Horkheimer. *Dialectic of Enlightenment* (Horkheimer and Adorno 2002) argues that the Enlightenment's drive toward domination of nature through instrumental reason generates the conditions for its own self-destruction: myth and enlightenment are intertwined, and the rationalization of the world produces new forms of barbarism rather than emancipation. For Habermas, this is precisely the impasse that communicative reason is designed to overcome. His charge against Adorno and Horkheimer is that by collapsing all rationality into instrumental reason, they foreclose the normative standpoint from which critique could be mounted: the total critique of reason is performatively self-contradictory, since it presupposes the validity of the critique it is making. Hohendahl (1985) traces the conceptual architecture of this disagreement in detail, examining whether Habermas' communicative rationality can do the critical work that Adorno's negative dialectics cannot, and identifies residual tensions in Habermas' account of the transition from the first to the second generation of the Frankfurt School.

Freyenhagen (2023) introduces an important revisionary element into this narrative. He argues that Horkheimer and Adorno anticipated a "linguistic turn" in critical theory before Habermas' communicative reorientation, drawing on later Wittgenstein's picture of language. If this is right, the standard narrative of a clean paradigm shift — with Habermas introducing language and intersubjectivity into a tradition that had no resources for them — requires revision. The transition involves continuity as well as rupture, and Habermas' self-presentation as inaugurating a new paradigm through his critique of Adorno and Horkheimer is at least partially contested.

Cobben (2021) introduces a further complication from the opposite direction: the late Habermas of *Auch eine Geschichte der Philosophie* (2019) partially rehabilitates Hegel as a contributor to the "paradigm of speech-philosophy." On Cobben's reading, Habermas credits Hegel with transforming Kant's noumenal subject into a free subject related to the lifeworld through the medium of ethical life, thereby broadening the concept of reason in ways that anticipate communicative philosophy. This partial rehabilitation complicates the earlier critique in *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity*: the late Habermas acknowledges a dimension of Hegel's thought that his earlier position had foreclosed. Whether this constitutes a revision of the critique or merely a more discriminating application of it is a question that runs through the secondary literature on Habermas' late work (Czingon et al. 2020; Verovšek 2020).

The broader pattern that emerges from these debates is that Habermas' critique of speculative philosophy of history is itself contested along each of its three axes. The critique of Hegel is challenged by those who argue that Hegel's concept of ethical life already contains intersubjective dimensions that Habermas claims to have introduced (Cobben 2021). The critique of Marx is challenged by those who argue that the communicative alternative reproduces idealist tendencies in a new register (Tang 2022). The critique of Adorno and Horkheimer is challenged by those who argue that the first generation of the Frankfurt School was closer to the linguistic turn than the standard narrative acknowledges (Freyenhagen 2023). These challenges do not nullify Habermas' reconstructive project, but they indicate that the negative condition for it — the critique of its predecessors — is more contested than the project's self-presentation suggests. The communicative alternative to speculative philosophy of history rests on a set of critical readings whose adequacy is itself a matter of ongoing dispute.

## Section 2: The Normative Architecture: Communicative Reason, Modernity, and Moral Progress

Habermas' reconstructive alternative to speculative philosophy of history is not only a methodological proposal but carries a substantive normative architecture. Communicative reason, the diagnosis of modernity as an unfinished project, and discourse ethics together constitute an account of historical normativity that claims to preserve universalist aspirations while abandoning teleological metaphysics. Two questions organize this architecture and define the tensions running through Habermas' work from the 1980s to his late magnum opus: whether the "unfinished project" thesis can bear the diagnostic weight placed on it, and whether the proceduralist standard of discourse ethics is genuinely content-free or covertly substantive.

### 2.1 Modernity as Unfinished Project

The pivotal statement of Habermas' philosophy of modernity is his 1980 Adorno Prize lecture (Habermas 2001). There he argues that Weber's account of value-sphere differentiation — the separation of science, morality, and art into autonomous but institutionally isolated domains — has not been matched by the reconnection of these spheres to communicative practice in the lifeworld. The result is not the exhaustion of modernity's normative potential but its incomplete realization. Against postmodern diagnoses and neoconservative retreats, Habermas insists that the project of modernity must be carried forward rather than abandoned. The differentiation of reason is an achievement, not a catastrophe; the pathological side is the selective institutionalization of cognitive-instrumental reason at the expense of communicative and moral-practical reason.

This thesis draws on the theoretical infrastructure of *The Theory of Communicative Action*. Volume 1 (Habermas 1984) reconstructs Western rationalization as a process in which the full spectrum of validity claims — truth, normative rightness, and truthfulness — has been progressively differentiated, though unevenly institutionalized. Volume 2 (Habermas 1987) translates this into historical diagnosis through the colonization thesis: the system media of money and power have penetrated communicative domains — family, culture, public deliberation — producing the characteristic pathologies of modernity. The two volumes thus provide the theoretical backbone for the "unfinished project": rationalization has generated normative resources, but their connection to everyday communicative practice has been systematically blocked.

The social-theoretic infrastructure of this diagnosis has been contested. Chernilo (2002) argues that Habermas' account of modernization as differentiation and colonization retains more of Parsons than he acknowledges — the concept of generalized symbolic media through which system and lifeworld are articulated is borrowed from Parsons and Luhmann in ways that constrain the critical potential of the framework. If the theory of differentiation is Parsonian in its deep structure, then the normative diagnosis of colonization may be less radical than it appears.

Kirkpatrick (2003) offers a partial defense from a different angle. Against critics who read the developmental logic as an implausible evolutionary biology of society, he argues that the framework is better understood as a theory of progress in the regulative sense — a rational reconstruction of the logical preconditions of communicative rationalization rather than a prediction of historical dynamics. On this reading, the "unfinished project" thesis is more modest and more defensible than its detractors assume, claiming only that the normative achievements of modernity set a standard against which present arrangements can be critically measured, not that history is moving inevitably toward their realization.

Perhaps the most consequential contemporary extension of the colonization thesis is Condon's (2020) reframing of Habermas' account for neoliberal conditions. Condon argues that neoliberalism does not simply colonize the lifeworld in the classical sense — system imperatives overriding communicative norms through money and power — but operates through what he calls "relinguistification": market rationality penetrates communicative domains by adopting the language of communicative norms, presenting transparency, accountability, and performativity as communicative values. This means colonization is no longer visible as an external intrusion but is disguised as the enhancement of communication. Condon's reframing shows both the ongoing diagnostic relevance of the system/lifeworld distinction and the need to revise the original framework to capture how power can operate through communicative forms rather than despite them.

The D'Entrèves and Benhabib (1996) collection assembles the sustained critical engagement with Habermas' unfinished project thesis in its original context, bringing together responses to the *Philosophical Discourse of Modernity*. These critical essays show that the thesis has been simultaneously defended as indispensable and challenged as too confident about the normative surplus of Western modernity. That tension — between the diagnostic ambition and the resources available to sustain it — defines the problem that discourse ethics and the late philosophy of history attempt to resolve.

### 2.2 Discourse Ethics, Rational Freedom, and the Late Philosophy of History

Discourse ethics provides the proceduralist normative standard by which historical progress can be measured without presupposing a fixed telos. In *Justification and Application* (Habermas 1993), Habermas distinguishes the justification of moral norms — a matter of universal discourse, in which all affected parties must in principle be able to assent — from their application, which requires context-sensitive judgment. The claim is that this proceduralist framework avoids both teleological naturalism (progress as a predetermined destination) and Kantian apriorism (moral principles derived independently of communicative practice). Progress consists in the expansion of the conditions under which communicative discourse can take place uncoerced; it is measured against the presuppositions of discourse itself, not against an independently specified end-state.

*Auch eine Geschichte der Philosophie* (Habermas 2023) raises the stakes of this account dramatically. The two-volume work reconstructs the history of Western philosophy from the Greek axial age through Kant as a learning process oriented toward "rational freedom" — a normative ideal integrating discourse ethics, communicative action, and an account of the rational content carried by religious and metaphysical traditions. The "occidental constellation" of faith and knowledge — the encounter between Greek philosophy and the Abrahamic religions — is presented as the historically specific source of the conceptual resources through which communicative reason developed. As Verovšek (2020) argues, this constitutes a partial revision of Habermas' earlier postmetaphysical stance: rather than simply dismissing metaphysical and religious traditions, the late Habermas acknowledges that they carry normative content that cannot be translated away but must be dialogically appropriated. Harrington (2007) similarly reads the post-secular turn as a development within Habermas' philosophy of history, in which the learning processes embedded in religious traditions are not simply superseded by secular modernity but recognized as resources for normative reflection.

The critical reception of *Auch eine Geschichte* has centered on whether this reconstruction successfully avoids the teleological commitments Habermas criticizes in Hegel. Benhabib (2021) describes the work as a "new Phenomenology of Spirit" — a characterization that is as much diagnosis as compliment. She argues that Habermas' account of the learning process as "rational freedom" is more committed to a substantive telos than it acknowledges: the reconstruction of Western philosophy as a progressive movement toward communicative reason presupposes the very normative destination it claims only to reconstruct. Her further criticism concerns the treatment of non-Western philosophical traditions, which she argues receive insufficient attention — a problem given that the claim to universality requires engagement with the conceptual resources of traditions not shaped by the "occidental constellation."

Forst (2021) develops a precise philosophical objection to the concept of "rational freedom" at the center of *Auch eine Geschichte*. He asks whether this concept can simultaneously serve as the standard for evaluating historical learning processes and as the outcome of those processes without generating a circularity that undermines the account. If "rational freedom" is defined as the capacity for autonomous self-determination realized through communicative reason, and the history of philosophy is reconstructed as the progressive development toward this capacity, then the reconstruction already assumes the normative destination it claims only to discover. Forst frames this as a tension between the autonomy of the normative standard and the historical genesis through which it is supposed to emerge — a tension that, he argues, is present in Habermas' work from the beginning but becomes fully visible only in the late synthesis.

Ferrara (2021) illuminates a related tension through Habermas' treatment of Rawls in *Auch eine Geschichte*. Examining Habermas' critique of Rawls' concept of "the reasonable," Ferrara argues that the exchange reveals a proceduralism in Habermas that is more substantive than its self-presentation allows. Habermas criticizes Rawls for grounding political norms in a "reasonable" that is culturally particular rather than universally discursive, but Ferrara argues that Habermas' own proceduralism — its embedding in a specific reconstruction of the Western philosophical tradition — faces an analogous problem. The historical trajectory through which communicative reason emerged is not neutral between competing accounts of reasonableness.

Nascimento (2024) maps the conceptual plurality of *Auch eine Geschichte*, identifying at least five distinct philosophical projects woven together in the work: a history of philosophy as learning process, a theory of moral progress, an account of secularization, a reconstruction of faith and knowledge, and a philosophy of history grounded in communicative reason. This plurality may be read as the strength of a genuinely synthetic project, or as a tension between ambitions that do not fully cohere. The question of whether the work achieves systematic unity — or whether its different dimensions pull in incompatible directions — bears directly on the coherence of Habermas' normative architecture as a whole.

Together, these critical engagements converge on what is perhaps the central tension in Habermas' normative philosophy of history: the claim that communicative reason has universal validity is made from within a historically particular reconstruction of the Western philosophical tradition. Habermas' consistent response is that the "occidental constellation" is the contingent historical source of communicative reason, not its justification — the universality of the normative claims must be assessed on proceduralist grounds, not by their genealogy. Whether that response is adequate to the critical pressure applied by Benhabib, Forst, and Ferrara is the question that structures the Eurocentrism debate examined in Section 3.

## Section 3: Critiques and Prospects

Habermas' reconstructive philosophy of history has attracted sustained criticism from several directions. Postmodern and hermeneutic critics challenge whether the communicative-rationality framework is itself a form of the foundationalism it claims to have overcome. Postcolonial critics press the deeper charge that the developmental logic of social evolution is Eurocentric in its very structure. Neo-Habermasian responses argue that these critiques, however instructive, do not demonstrate the failure of the reconstructive project — only the need for its revision.

### 3.1 Postmodern and Hermeneutic Challenges

The most direct confrontation between Habermas' philosophy of history and the postmodern tradition is staged in Rorty's triangulation of the Habermas-Lyotard debate. Rorty (1991) argues that both parties are mistaken: Habermas retains too much of the "philosophy of representation" he claims to have displaced, while Lyotard's anti-foundationalism provides no basis for political solidarity. Rorty's pragmatist alternative — solidarity without universal foundations — poses the sharpest version of the challenge: whether any philosophy of history is necessary for emancipatory politics, or whether appeals to communicative reason are simply a more sophisticated form of the grounding gesture that postmodernism rightly rejects.

The challenge is developed along distinct lines by critics of Habermas' engagement with Foucault and Heidegger in *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity*. Both Fleming (1996) and Visker (1992) argue that Habermas systematically misreads these thinkers by assimilating them to the philosophy of the subject — the very tradition he criticizes. Fleming contends that Foucault's genealogical account of historical transformation is more nuanced than Habermas allows, and that by reducing genealogy to a covert continuation of subject-centred reason, the "unfinished project" thesis fails to engage Foucault's strongest arguments. Visker makes a parallel case regarding Heidegger, showing that Habermas' reconstruction cannot sustain its claim that *Sein und Zeit*'s concept of Dasein is simply another variant of the philosophy of the subject. These exegetical objections have theoretical stakes: if Habermas' dismissal of his postmodern interlocutors depends on misreadings, then his argument that communicative rationality is the only viable successor to speculative philosophy of history is weakened at its foundations.

Gürsoy (2018) approaches the same fault line through the lens of psychoanalysis. Both Habermas and Foucault draw on Freudian resources, but in divergent ways: Habermas uses the psychoanalytic model as a figure for ideology-critique — the emancipatory undistortion of communication — while Foucault's genealogy reveals reason itself as constituted through power relations. This difference is not merely methodological but maps onto incompatible philosophies of history. For Habermas, reason contains its own corrective; for Foucault, the very criteria by which correction is measured are always already effects of power. Steuerman (2003) extends this psychoanalytic dimension by bringing Melanie Klein's object-relations theory to bear on the Habermas-Lyotard debate, arguing that both positions neglect the affective and psychodynamic dimensions of social learning — the ways in which the constitution of rational subjects involves processes that neither formal pragmatics nor anti-foundationalism can adequately theorize.

The hermeneutic tradition poses a structurally different challenge. Mendelson (1979) reconstructs the Habermas-Gadamer debate around the question of whether hermeneutic understanding is inherently conservative or can serve emancipatory ends. Habermas' critique of Gadamer — that the rehabilitation of tradition cannot distinguish legitimate from ideologically distorted forms of transmitted authority — remains defensible, but Gadamer's counter-claim that all critique is embedded within traditions that cannot themselves be subjected to fully transparent rational reconstruction presses on a real vulnerability in Habermas' quasi-transcendental standpoint. Piercey (2004) proposes a Ricoeurian mediation: Ricoeur's account of narrative identity, in which historical understanding involves both belonging to a tradition and critical distanciation from it, dissolves the sharp opposition between hermeneutics and ideology-critique. On this reading, the philosophy of history cannot proceed by subordinating tradition to rational reconstruction, as Habermas tends to do, but must hold the two poles in productive tension.

### 3.2 The Eurocentrism Debate and Contemporary Prospects

The most consequential challenge to Habermas' philosophy of history in recent decades is the postcolonial critique of its developmental logic. Allen (2016) argues that the learning-process model at the heart of Habermas' reconstructive theory of social evolution is a crypto-teleological account that unreflectively privileges the Western developmental trajectory. Drawing on postcolonial theory and Foucauldian genealogy, Allen contends that the criteria by which Habermas ranks social formations — the degree to which communicative rationalization has penetrated normative structures — are derived from the specifically Western experience of modernity and cannot be universalized without an illegitimate extrapolation. The result is a philosophy of history that presents as a universal developmental sequence what is in fact a particular, historically contingent path.

Owen (1998) raises a cognate challenge at the level of the developmental logic itself: the criteria used to order social formations — principally the extent of communicative rationalization — are constructed from within the Western historical experience and cannot claim cross-cultural validity without circularity. Vázquez-Arroyo (2017), assessing Allen's argument, notes both its force and the difficulty of articulating an emancipatory critical theory without any concept of progress whatsoever — a tension that Allen's proposed postcolonial alternative does not fully resolve.

Ahmad (2025) connects the theoretical critique to a concrete test case. Habermas' public interventions on the Israel-Palestine conflict, Ahmad argues, reveal that the nominally universalist norms of his framework are in practice applied selectively in ways that grant normative primacy to the "occidental constellation." This is not, on Ahmad's account, merely a personal failure of application but reflects a structural feature of Habermas' reconstructive account of modernity, which grants constitutive normative weight to the Western trajectory while treating non-Western claims as requiring the mediation of that standard.

The neo-Habermasian response to these challenges takes two main forms. Payrow Shabani (2017) argues that the postcolonial critique conflates the concept of progress with particular Eurocentric narratives of progress. A "thin" concept of progress — one that identifies the expansion of communicative freedom without specifying a single cultural path through which this expansion must occur — can be defended without the problematic features the critics identify. The concept of progress is, on this account, ineliminable from any normative political theory: without some notion of better and worse social arrangements, the emancipatory commitment of critical theory is groundless. Wolkenstein (2025) demonstrates the continuing productivity of the social evolution framework for contemporary political philosophy by applying it to democratic backsliding. His "dialectic of backsliding" proposes that regressions mobilize the normative resources that communicative rationalization has deposited in the lifeworld, generating counter-movements — a theorization that requires precisely the developmental logic the postcolonial critics contest.

Strydom (2014) offers a revisionary internal development of the framework. He argues that Habermas' theory of communicative action contains a "latent cognitive sociology" — an empirically grounded account of social cognition that is more adequate to the actual mechanisms of historical learning than the official formal-pragmatic presentation. Developing this latent dimension would, Strydom suggests, produce a more empirically sensitive theory of historical processes that is less vulnerable to the charge of abstract universalism. Taken together, these neo-Habermasian responses suggest that the framework retains productive resources for contemporary philosophy of history, but that their deployment requires revising the more assertive universalist claims that generate the most serious objections.

The critiques surveyed in this section converge on a single question: whether the formal-procedural core of communicative reason can be disentangled from the specific Western genealogy through which it has been articulated and defended. The postmodern critics doubt that any reconstructive framework can avoid reproducing the foundationalist gesture; the postcolonial critics doubt that the particular reconstruction Habermas offers can carry the universalist weight he places on it. Neither doubt has been decisively answered, though both have been met with responses that suggest revision rather than abandonment of the project.

## Conclusion

Habermas' philosophy of history is not a single theory but a layered achievement constructed across five decades. Its positive core is a reconstructive theory of social evolution: the claim that history can be understood as a sequence of learning processes — developmental logics that specify the structural possibilities available to social formations — without presupposing a metaphysically guaranteed endpoint. Its negative core is a sustained critique of the speculative tradition from Hegel through Marx to Adorno, arguing that each predecessor forecloses normative critique by binding it to a philosophy of the subject or a paradigm of labor that communicative reason can replace. The normative capstone is the theory of modernity as an unfinished project and, in *Also a History of Philosophy* (Habermas 2023), the reconstruction of Western philosophy's long development toward "rational freedom" as the historical ground of that communicative reason.

Three tensions, however, run unresolved through the entire corpus. The first is the gap between developmental logic and developmental dynamics: Habermas provides an account of what sequences of social organization are structurally possible but, as Oh (2023) argues, the mechanism connecting normative sequences to actual historical change remains theoretically underdetermined, particularly because individual learning — the model for collective progress — is non-cumulative in ways that strain the analogy. The second tension concerns proceduralism and substance. Discourse ethics is designed to measure moral progress without presupposing a fixed telos, but Forst (2021) presses the question of whether "rational freedom" can simultaneously serve as the standard for evaluating historical learning and as its outcome without circularity — a version of the concern that the normative architecture both grounds and is grounded by the historical narrative it underwrites. The third tension is the universalism/Eurocentrism question that Allen (2016) identifies in its sharpest form: whether the "occidental constellation" Habermas traces in the late work is the historically contingent source of communicative reason, as Habermas insists, or whether the reconstruction covertly privileges that particular trajectory by treating it as the only path to universally valid normative resources.

The literature reviewed here has developed sophisticated tools for engaging each of these tensions, and recent work suggests that the framework remains productive. Wolkenstein (2025) demonstrates that the social evolution theory can be applied to contemporary democratic backsliding in ways that illuminate both regression and the resources for normative critique, indicating that the framework is more adaptable than the strongest critiques suggest. Benhabib (2021) maps the proximity of *Auch eine Geschichte der Philosophie* to Hegel's *Phenomenology* with precision, making visible both the ambition and the risks of Habermas' late synthesis.

Several gaps in the existing literature remain noteworthy. The relationship between Habermas' early social evolution essays of the 1970s and the late *Auch eine Geschichte* is underexplored: most commentators treat them as distinct projects rather than examining whether the late work resolves or sidesteps the antinomies of the early theory. Engagement between Habermas' philosophy of history and analytic philosophy of history — narrative theory, historical explanation, the epistemology of historiography — is sparse, leaving a potentially productive dialogue largely undeveloped. Non-Western philosophical engagements with the framework remain limited, which is particularly consequential given that the Eurocentrism critique is among the most pressing challenges the tradition faces. Finally, contemporary challenges — ecological crisis, digital communication ecologies, and the global wave of democratic backsliding — are only beginning to be theorized within the Habermasian framework, and the adequacy of the social evolution model to these conditions remains an open question.

The research project whose scope this review maps is positioned to address the first of these gaps directly, tracing the philosophical continuity and revision between Habermas' early and late contributions to the philosophy of history, and assessing whether the reconstructive method is sufficiently self-critical to meet the challenges that its most searching interlocutors have posed.
