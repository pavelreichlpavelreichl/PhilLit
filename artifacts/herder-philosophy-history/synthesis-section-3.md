## Section 2: Relativism, Pluralism, and Incommensurability — The Berlin Debate

No debate has done more to shape the Anglophone reception of Herder's philosophy of history than the controversy over whether his celebration of cultural particularity entails relativism, pluralism, or something in between. Three broad camps have organized the discussion. The first reads Herder as a cultural relativist who denies the possibility of genuinely trans-cultural moral judgment. The second, decisively framed by Isaiah Berlin, treats Herder as a value pluralist: values are many, incommensurable, yet universally human, so pluralism avoids full relativism. The third — associated with Sonia Sikka, F. M. Barnard, and Michael Forster — reconstructs Herder as an "enlightened" or "qualified" relativist who combines a thin universal human nature with a robust contextualism about how virtue, happiness, and the good life are culturally specified. As Zammito, Menges, and Menze (2010) document, a decisive shift in Herder scholarship over the past quarter century has moved the field away from the older nationalist-relativist reading toward this more philosophically nuanced terrain — but the shift has not resolved the underlying disagreement.

### Subsection 2.1: Berlin's Pluralist Reading and the Relativist Alternative

The locus classicus for the pluralist interpretation is Berlin's *Vico and Herder* (1976), subsequently revised and expanded in *Three Critics of the Enlightenment* (Berlin 2014). Berlin argues that Herder is the founding figure of value pluralism: each culture possesses its own "center of gravity" (*Schwerpunkt*), its own incommensurable standard of achievement, and no overarching metric can rank these against one another. Crucially for Berlin, this pluralism does not collapse into relativism, because the values realized in different cultures are authentically human values — expressions of a shared human nature — rather than merely parochial preferences. The incommensurability thesis thus operates within a universal frame: cultures cannot be ranked on a single scale, but they all fall within the space of genuinely human possibilities.

Against this, Whitton (1988) advances a contrasting communitarian-relativist reading. Whitton argues that Herder's fundamental critique of Enlightenment rationalism — his insistence that reason and morality are meaningful only when embedded in the particular cultural communities that give them content — actually undermines the universalist backstop on which Berlin relies. If the norms of a culture are intelligible only from within, and if the *Humanität* ideal provides no independent leverage over how those norms develop, then Herder's position yields a form of cultural relativism in practice, whatever its formal universalist commitments. Whitton further notes an internal instability: Herder's positive vision of cultural community itself borrows normative resources from the Enlightenment project he critiques, leaving his anti-universalism without a stable foundation of its own. This reading anticipates later concerns about whether the *Schwerpunkt* doctrine can coherently be combined with any cross-cultural evaluative standard.

### Subsection 2.2: Enlightened and Qualified Relativism

The most sustained alternative to Berlin's pluralism is the "enlightened relativism" developed by Sikka across her 2005 article and her 2011 monograph. Sikka (2011) argues that Herder holds both that a common human nature grounds basic goods and that virtue and happiness are irreducibly culturally specific. This combination blocks both imperial universalism — the imposition of a single cultural model — and thoroughgoing moral relativism — the view that no trans-cultural criteria for judgment exist at all. *Humanität*, on Sikka's reading, functions as a genuine but thin cross-cultural standard: it identifies the goods any human life requires without specifying a single cultural template for realizing them. Sikka (2005) makes the philosophical structure explicit: Herder presupposes a set of basic human goods while insisting that "the happiness and virtue of individuals can only be measured in relation to their specific values." The position is not a compromise but a principled alternative to both poles.

Barnard (2003) develops a complementary account focused on the relationship between cultural particularity and universal humanity. For Barnard, Herder's nationalism is grounded in the conviction that each culture preserves incommensurable distinctiveness not despite *Humanität* but as a contribution to it: diverse cultural realizations collectively constitute the full expression of human possibility. This reading frames incommensurability not as a block to evaluation but as a condition of human flourishing — the diversity of peoples is constitutive of the human good rather than an obstacle to it. The critical question Barnard's account must answer is whether this integrative function of *Humanität* supplies a substantive trans-historical standard or merely a formal telos that cannot support any particular cross-cultural judgment.

Forster (2018) addresses the same question from a systematic philosophical perspective. His reconstruction of Herder's full philosophical system acknowledges the relativistic impulses in Herder's texts — the claim that "each epoch carries its standard within itself" — while arguing they are partially restrained by an anthropological universalism concerning basic human capacities. Forster does not claim the tensions are fully resolved; rather, he argues they are tensions within a sophisticated philosophical position rather than signs of incoherence. Spencer (2007) adds a further dimension by arguing that Herder's pluralism is not only compatible with cross-cultural exchange but actually requires it: cultures learn from one another, and Herder's opposition to cultural domination is distinct from any opposition to cultural contact. This reading directly challenges the interpretive tendency to treat Herder's *Schwerpunkt* doctrine as licensing cultural closure.

### Subsection 2.3: Challenges to the Berlin and Counter-Enlightenment Framing

The past two decades have seen sustained challenges not just to Berlin's specific characterization of Herder's pluralism but to the entire narrative framework within which that characterization operates. Norton (2007) argues that the "Counter-Enlightenment" is Berlin's intellectual construction rather than a genuine historical movement. The thinkers Berlin groups together — Herder, Hamann, Vico — did not share a unified opposition to the Enlightenment, and Berlin's numerous accounts of their thought, Norton contends, reflect not their own positions but late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century German historicist ideology that sought to legitimate anti-democratic politics through a false genealogy. If Norton is right, the narrative structure that gives Berlin's pluralist reading its force — Herder as the great opponent of Enlightenment monism — is significantly undermined.

Piirimäe (2015) offers a more focused critique, arguing that Berlin's placement of Herder within a Counter-Enlightenment framework distorts his actual political and historical thought in specific ways. Berlin overemphasizes cultural particularism and nationalism while underplaying Herder's cosmopolitan commitments, his anti-colonialism, and his substantive engagement with Enlightenment ideals. The result, for Piirimäe, is a picture of Herder that is "deeply misleading." This argument is developed at length in Piirimäe (2023), which reconstructs Herder's political thought as a response to Enlightenment political debates — concerning patriotism, commerce, and peace — rather than as their negation. The Berlin framing, on this account, has caused scholars to misread Herder as a cultural rather than political thinker and to underestimate the extent to which his pluralism operates within rather than against an Enlightenment framework.

Yack (2012) offers a different angle of critique. He accepts that Berlin is right to draw attention to Herder's significance as an adversary of the *philosophes*, but argues that Berlin misidentifies the real significance of Herder's critique. The importance of Herder's pluralism, Yack contends, lies not in stopping an ancient monist creed but in rehabilitating prejudice and cultural particularity as sources of human virtue and creativity — a critique that directly targeted the Enlightenment's core mission of removing obstacles to knowledge. Berlin's misidentification matters because it leads him to mischaracterize both the Enlightenment he opposes to Herder and the nature of Herder's pluralist alternative. Yack's reframing suggests that what Herder was criticizing was not universalism per se but a specific Enlightenment project of cultural rationalization.

Denby (2005) contributes yet another revisionist angle: comparing Herder with the natural historian Buffon, he argues that Herder's concept of *Kultur* as the process through which humanity develops is closer to the Enlightenment's unitary model of civilization than the Counter-Enlightenment reading allows. Herder's relativism, Denby concludes, is unstable because he retains universal criteria for transcultural judgment — an observation that supports Sikka's enlightened-relativism thesis while challenging the picture of Herder as a thinker systematically opposed to Enlightenment universalism.

Taken together, these challenges reveal that the debate over relativism and pluralism in Herder scholarship cannot be separated from a methodological debate about how the Enlightenment itself should be characterized. Whether Herder is a relativist, a pluralist, or an enlightened relativist depends substantially on what concept of universalism his position is measured against, and critics of Berlin's framework argue that framework imported a distorted picture of both Herder and the Enlightenment from a politically tendentious German historicist tradition. What remains unresolved is precisely the status of *Humanität* as a normative concept: whether it functions as a substantive trans-historical standard or merely as a thin regulative ideal is the pivotal question, and the answer determines whether Herder's incommensurability doctrine succeeds in blocking relativism or merely relocates it. This question passes directly to the examination of *Humanität*, providence, and the normative telos in Section 3.
