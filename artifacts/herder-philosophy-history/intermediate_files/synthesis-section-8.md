## Section 7: Reception — Romanticism, Historicism, and the Human Sciences

### Subsection 7.1: Romanticism, Idealism, and Hegel

Herder's downstream influence on German Romanticism and post-Kantian Idealism was extensive, though largely unacknowledged by those who benefited from it. Forster (2018) argues that this systematic underattribution was not accidental but structural: Herder's alienation from both the Kantian and Goethean intellectual factions by the early nineteenth century meant that successors such as Schleiermacher, Schlegel, Schelling, Hegel, and Humboldt absorbed Herderian ideas while publicly distancing themselves from their source. What was transmitted ranged from the thought–language dependence thesis and cultural holism to the expressivist metaphysics of *Kraft* that animated Romantic *Naturphilosophie*.

Hampton (2015) identifies a specific mechanism of that transmission. Herder's revision of Spinoza in the Pantheism Controversy — replacing Spinoza's inert single substance with the active principle *Kraft*, drawing on the Cambridge Platonist Ralph Cudworth's concept of "plastic nature" — provided the metaphysical catalyst that inaugurated the Romantic movement. This revised Spinozism, with its active, self-organizing force immanent in nature and culture alike, was taken up by Schelling and became a foundation for Romantic *Naturphilosophie*'s organic conception of natural development. The transmission was thus not merely of broad cultural themes but of specific doctrinal moves that later became canonical.

The Herder–Hegel relationship has attracted particular scrutiny because it marks the point at which Herderian historicism was systematized and teleologized in ways that arguably distorted its original pluralism. Gjesdal (2006) argues that Herder developed a philosophically coherent account of the historicity of art and reason some sixty years before Hegel's Berlin lectures, but without the essentialist framework in which art's historical development culminates in its own supersession. Where Herder's account is resolutely non-essentialist — cultures and epochs carry their standards within themselves, and no external endpoint licenses a hierarchy among them — Hegel's idealism reintroduced a teleological structure in which history's end is both knowable and legitimating. The "end of art" thesis that follows from Hegel's essentialism is, on Gjesdal's reading, precisely what Herder's historicism resists. The Herderian inheritance in Hegel is thus real but filtered through a transformation that inverts one of its most distinctive commitments.

### Subsection 7.2: Historicism, Dilthey, Gadamer, and Boasian Anthropology

The transmission from Herder to the nineteenth-century historicist tradition — Ranke, Droysen, Dilthey — constitutes the second major line of reception, and it is through this lineage that Herder came to be identified with the *Geisteswissenschaften* and their distinctive interpretive methods. Beiser (2011) traces German historicism as a philosophically substantive program — the claim that human thought, values, and institutions are historically conditioned and can only be understood through historical inquiry — and places Herder at its origins, crediting him with establishing the anti-naturalist and particularist foundations that Ranke and Droysen elaborated in historiographic practice. For Beiser, historicism is not mere relativism but a positive methodological commitment with Herder as its originating figure.

The Herder–Dilthey connection is more complex than this foundationalist picture suggests. Makkreel (1969) situates Dilthey as the crucial mediator between Herderian historicism and twentieth-century hermeneutics, noting that Dilthey's insistence on historical individuality and the irreducibility of human expression to natural-scientific categories carries a recognizably Herderian inheritance. Yet Hamid (2023) complicates this picture by showing that in Dilthey's earliest work of the 1860s–70s, the new empirical science of anthropology pulled him toward naturalistic positions contrary to the historicist hermeneutics of his teachers. The Herderian inheritance was thus not smoothly received but negotiated against rival tendencies: the same ambiguity between empirical naturalism about human development and cultural-historical particularism that marked Herder's own project reappeared in Dilthey's struggle to ground the *Geisteswissenschaften*.

Gadamer's relationship to this line of transmission has been critically assessed. Gjesdal (2008) argues that Gadamer's account of the origins of modern hermeneutics systematically undervalues Herder while overvaluing Schleiermacher, a distortion shaped by Gadamer's own philosophical agenda. Harrington (2000) further shows that Gadamer's and Habermas's shared critique of Dilthey as covertly "objectivist" — for grounding interpretive validity in psychological re-experiencing (*Nacherleben*) rather than normative dialogue — misreads Dilthey's later work and, by extension, the Herderian tradition from which it descends. The hermeneutic tradition did not simply inherit Herder's *Einfühlung*; it debated and revised it, sometimes by misidentifying what the inheritance actually contained.

The Herder–Boas transmission represents a third distinct reception line, running through the institutional founding of cultural anthropology in the early twentieth century. Denby (2005) argues that Herder's insistence on cultural particularity, organic cultural unity, and the incommensurability of cultural values provided the conceptual vocabulary — mediated through German Romanticism — that Boas and his school institutionalized as cultural relativism and participant-observation methodology. Forster (2018) extends this argument, tracing specific conceptual borrowings from Herder through Schlegel and Humboldt to Boas and Malinowski, and contending that Herder's own versions of the enabling principles were in important respects superior to what the disciplines subsequently developed. Across all three reception lines, the pattern is consistent: Herder's successors selectively inherited his commitments, often resolving or suppressing the tensions — between particular worth and universal telos, between naturalistic anthropology and cultural particularism — that give his philosophy of history its distinctive difficulty.
