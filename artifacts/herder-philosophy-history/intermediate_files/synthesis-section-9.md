## Conclusion

The secondary literature on Herder's philosophy of history is organized, almost without exception, by a single structural tension: the commitment to the intrinsic and equal worth of each culture and epoch stands in apparent conflict with a directional, universal history aiming at *Humanität*. Wells (1960) named this the problem of "two philosophies," and subsequent scholarship has never fully escaped the question he posed. What has changed significantly over the past two decades is the range of positions considered viable. Zammito, Menges, and Menze (2010) documented a major historiographical shift away from the older nationalist, irrationalist, and relativist readings — the picture inherited from a selective Rankean appropriation of Herder — toward accounts that foreground his Enlightenment commitments, his thin universalism, and the providential architecture of the *Ideen*. The revisionist consensus, developed by Sikka (2011), Barnard (2003), Forster (2010), and Piirimäe (2023), treats Herder as a qualified or enlightened relativist who combines robust cultural contextualism with a normative core that blocks thoroughgoing relativism.

Yet this reconciliationist turn remains contested on multiple fronts. Whitton (1988) argues for a communitarian-relativist reading that Berlin's pluralism fails to displace. Norton (2007) challenges the very concept of the Counter-Enlightenment that structures Berlin's framing of Herder, a challenge extended by Piirimäe (2023) who argues that Berlin's interpretive lens systematically distorts Herder's actual political and philosophical commitments. Reichert (2023) presses three internal limits on the *Einfühlung* methodology — relativism, reductionism, and the absence of any external criterion of adequacy — that resist easy dissolution. Most pointedly, Layne and Norberg (2023) argue that *Humanität* in the *Briefe* is structurally underwritten by a self-effacing representation of the enslaved Black person, locating an exclusion internal to the universalism rather than merely contingently attached to it. These critical voices do not simply recycle older objections; they identify structural problems within the positions most favored by the revisionist consensus.

Several questions remain genuinely open. First, the normative status of *Humanität* is unresolved: whether it functions as a substantive trans-historical standard capable of grounding cross-cultural moral judgment, as Crowe (2012) maintains on theistic-perfectionist grounds, or as a thin regulative horizon that cannot bear that weight, remains disputed. Second, the Spinozist metaphysics that grounds Herder's account of *Kraft* and development raises unresolved questions about whether his providentialism is ultimately theistic or naturalizable — a question with direct consequences for whether the telos is genuinely universal or merely the projection of a particular theological commitment. Third, the "father of historicism" attribution, while complicated by Beiser (2011) and Zammito's revisionist account (2009) of how the Rankean tradition selectively appropriated Herder, has not been replaced by a stable alternative characterization of his legacy. Fourth, the epistemological coherence of *Einfühlung* — whether the hermeneutic program Forster (2010) attributes to Herder can satisfy the demands Reichert (2023) identifies — turns on issues in the philosophy of understanding that the literature has not resolved.

The scholarship also flags several underexplored areas. The specific transmission path from Herder to Ranke and the early historicist school remains less studied than the Herder–Dilthey or Herder–Gadamer lines documented by Gjesdal (2008) and Harrington (2000). Non-Western and postcolonial reception of Herder is largely absent from the current literature, a gap that Layne and Norberg's (2023) analysis of race and *Humanität* makes newly pressing. The integration of Herder's natural-scientific interests — climate, physiology, epigenesis — with his philosophy of history has been advanced by work on *Kraft* and analogy but remains unevenly treated in the philosophical literature. And Forster's (2018) claim that Herder founds the tradition of cultural anthropology running to Boas, while documented in outline, invites more systematic treatment than it has so far received.

What the literature establishes beyond reasonable dispute is that Herder cannot be reduced to either the nationalist ideologue of older polemics or the benign cosmopolitan pluralist of some rehabilitative readings. The debates reviewed here — over relativism and pluralism, *Humanität* and telos, *Einfühlung* and method, *Volk* and politics — each return to the same unresolved core: whether a philosophy of history that insists on the intrinsic worth of every particular form of human life can simultaneously articulate a universal normative standard without suppressing or distorting what it claims to honor.
