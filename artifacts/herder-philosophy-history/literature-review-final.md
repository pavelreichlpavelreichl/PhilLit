## Introduction

Johann Gottfried Herder (1744–1803) occupies an unusual position in the history of philosophy: a thinker who is simultaneously indispensable and contested, claimed by incompatible traditions, and credited — or blamed — for ideas he may not have held. His two major contributions to the philosophy of history, *Auch eine Philosophie der Geschichte zur Bildung der Menschheit* (1774) and the four-volume *Ideen zur Philosophie der Geschichte der Menschheit* (1784–91), together constitute one of the most ambitious attempts in the modern period to think through how particular human communities and a universal human destiny can belong within a single coherent account of historical development. That ambition generates the tension which has organized Herder scholarship for more than a century: the commitment to the individuality and equal worth of each culture and epoch sits uneasily alongside an equally strong commitment to a directional history oriented toward *Humanität*. Wells (1960) named this the problem of Herder's "two philosophies of history," and nearly every major debate in the secondary literature — about relativism and pluralism, about the Counter-Enlightenment, about nationalism and cosmopolitanism, about providence and telos — can be read as a variation on whether and how that tension can be resolved.

The secondary literature has produced four broad interpretive positions, all of which inform the discussions that follow. The first, associated with Friedrich Meinecke's account of the origins of historicism and systematized in English by Beiser (2011), reads Herder as the founding figure of the historicist tradition: his insistence on cultural individuality, organic development, and the immanence of historical standards constitutes a philosophical program that defines historicism as a distinctive mode of inquiry. Beiser reconstructs three central commitments — individuality, development, and immanence — as the core of Herder's historicist achievement, while acknowledging that his providential theology and the teleological structure of *Humanität* sit uneasily with the later positivist Rankean tradition that claimed him as ancestor. A second and more widely influential position in the Anglophone literature is Isaiah Berlin's, elaborated in *Vico and Herder* (1976) and the subsequent collection *Three Critics of the Enlightenment* (2000). For Berlin, Herder inaugurates value pluralism: genuine human values are multiple, incommensurable, and irreducibly diverse, yet because they are authentically human they do not license pure relativism. Herder thus appears as the "most formidable adversary" of Enlightenment monism — a figure of the Counter-Enlightenment who nonetheless preserves a thin universalism through his concept of shared humanity.

The third position, developed independently by Sikka (2011), Barnard (2003), and Forster (2018), reconstructs Herder as a qualified or enlightened relativist. On this reading, *Humanität* combines a thin universal conception of human nature and basic goods with a robust insistence that their realization is always culturally shaped and contextually specific. This position blocks both imperial universalism and thoroughgoing moral relativism without collapsing into Berlin's pluralism. A fourth position, associated with Zammito, Menges, and Menze (2010), Piirimäe (2023), and Denby (2005), emphasizes Herder's continuity with rather than opposition to Enlightenment values, situating his cultural thought within rather than against the Enlightenment tradition of natural science, anthropology, and political reform.

The last quarter century has seen a significant revision in Herder scholarship. Zammito, Menges, and Menze (2010) document a "tidal shift" away from the older nationalist-relativist-irrationalist picture that long dominated both German and Anglophone reception, toward a philosophically more nuanced account that recognizes the coherence of Herder's positions on language, history, and culture. The earlier reading drew on selective appropriations by nineteenth-century German nationalists; the revisionist scholarship has worked to disentangle Herder's own arguments from their subsequent ideological instrumentalizations. This does not, however, mean that the revisionist picture has gone uncontested. Critics including Norton (2007), Whitton (1988), and Layne and Norberg (2023) have pressed questions about the limits and contradictions of the benign cosmopolitan Herder — whether by challenging Berlin's historical narrative, by arguing for a genuinely relativist reading, or by identifying structural exclusions within *Humanität* itself.

This review traces these debates across seven interconnected domains: the model of historical development and the historicism question; the relativism and pluralism debate generated by Berlin's reading; the conceptual status of *Humanität* and its theological-metaphysical foundations; Herder's relation to Kant and the Enlightenment; his hermeneutic method and the epistemology of historical understanding; his theory of *Volk* and the politics of his reception; and the transmission of his ideas to subsequent traditions in historicism, the human sciences, and anthropology. Throughout, the aim is to track not only the dominant reconciliationist readings but the voices of dissent and critique that prevent those readings from becoming settled orthodoxy.

## Section 1: Historical Development, Kraft, and the Historicism Question

### 1.1 The Genetic Method and *Kraft*

Herder's account of how cultures develop rests on a distinctive explanatory framework — what he calls the "genetic" method — in which historical change is understood through the internal forces (*Kraft*) immanent in peoples, languages, and institutions rather than through external mechanical causes or abstract rational standards. The philosophical genealogy of this concept is more complex than it might initially appear. Hampton (2015) argues that *Kraft* cannot be reduced to Herder's well-documented debts to Leibniz and Spinoza alone. Drawing on the Cambridge Platonist Ralph Cudworth's concept of "plastic nature" — an immanent organizing principle mediating between mechanism and transcendent teleology — Herder introduced a revised Spinozism in which force is active and self-organizing. Cudworth's *plastik* became the model for a middle principle capable of directing development from within without appealing to external design, and this English source, Hampton argues, has been largely overlooked in the German-idealism literature. The Pantheism Controversy (*Gott*, 1787) was the occasion at which Herder made this revision explicit, and his version of Spinoza's substance doctrine was subsequently absorbed into Romantic *Naturphilosophie*.

The explanatory work done by *Kraft* in Herder's historical writing is clarified when its metaphysical underpinnings are brought into view. Englander (2013) reconstructs what he terms Herder's "expressivist" metaphysics — the doctrine that reality consists of forces expressing themselves through particular natural and cultural forms without being exhausted by any of them — and connects this to the origins of German Idealism. On this reading, if the divine force expressing itself through history is identified with *Humanität*, then expressivist metaphysics supplies the ontological grounding for why the universal and the particular are mutually constituting rather than in tension: no single culture exhausts the divine force, but each genuinely expresses it. This philosophical architecture, Englander argues, was a significant if underacknowledged source for Hegel's absolute idealism, even as Herder's methodological naturalism set limits that Hegel's system had to overcome.

Herder's use of organic and analogical reasoning in the *Ideen* also brought him into direct methodological conflict with Kant. Nassar (2015) traces Kant's 1785 critical reviews of the *Ideen* to a fundamental dispute about the legitimacy of analogical inference in natural history: Herder bridges the gap between empirical observation and philosophical explanation through analogies between organisms and cultures, while Kant insists that such analogies exceed the bounds of legitimate empirical science. Nassar argues that Herder's analogical method is a philosophically serious alternative rather than mere speculative excess, and that Kant's response to it forced transformations within the critical philosophy itself — particularly in the account of teleological judgment. The organic metaphors in Herder are not ornamental; they carry the explanatory weight of a developmental model in which each culture grows like a living thing from a seed through determinate stages, driven by internal *Kraft* rather than external constraint.

### 1.2 Herder as Founder of Historicism: Meinecke's Thesis and Its Critics

The attribution of historicism's origins to Herder runs through Friedrich Meinecke's *Die Entstehung des Historismus* (1936) and was consolidated in the Anglophone literature by Beiser (2011). Beiser reconstructs the genesis of Herder's historicism by identifying three constitutive commitments: individuality (each culture and epoch has its own internal standard), development (cultures grow organically through time), and immanence (no appeal to transcendent or transhistorical norms). On Beiser's account, these commitments emerged from Herder's early confrontation with Enlightenment rationalism and natural law theory, and they define a historicist programme that is philosophically coherent rather than merely an impressionistic celebration of the past. Beiser does not ignore the theological dimension — Herder's providential framework sits alongside, and in tension with, the immanentist commitments — but he treats Herder as the founding figure of historicism proper, not merely a precursor.

Zammito (2009) challenges this genealogy from a revisionist direction. He argues that the standard "father of historicism" attribution involved what he calls a retrospective redemption: after the rise of the Rankean and Droysen tradition of disciplinary historiography, Herder was selectively appropriated as a founding ancestor, with his emphases on individuality and *Einfühlung* foregrounded and his providential teleology and metanarrative ambitions suppressed. What Herder was philosophically engaged in — offering a metanarrative about the purpose and direction of all history toward *Humanität* — is, Zammito contends, foreign to the positivist historicism that claimed him. The "father of historicism" is thus partly a construction of the tradition that needed a founder. Ankersmit (2010) approaches the question differently, defending historicism as a philosophically necessary framework for historical understanding — the claim that historical phenomena can only be understood in their own terms — and connecting this to the Herderian insistence on entering a culture's own conceptual world. For Ankersmit, the philosophical core of historicism survives and requires rehabilitation, even if the Rankean form of it had ideological distortions.

Together, these debates establish the developmental model at the center of Herder's philosophy of history and expose the tension that subsequent sections must adjudicate. *Kraft*, organic development, and analogical explanation account for how cultures are individuated and internally driven. But whether Herder's commitment to the intrinsic worth of each culture is compatible with a universal teleological direction — whether immanence and providence can be coherently combined — remains the central unresolved problem. The historicism debate is itself a symptom of that tension: which Herder to inherit depends on whether the metanarrative of *Humanität* or the particularist insistence on each epoch's self-sufficiency is taken as fundamental.

## Section 2: Relativism, Pluralism, and Incommensurability: The Berlin Debate

No debate has done more to shape the Anglophone reception of Herder's philosophy of history than the controversy over whether his celebration of cultural particularity entails relativism, pluralism, or something in between. Three broad camps have organized the discussion. The first reads Herder as a cultural relativist who denies the possibility of genuinely trans-cultural moral judgment. The second, decisively framed by Isaiah Berlin, treats Herder as a value pluralist: values are many, incommensurable, yet universally human, so pluralism avoids full relativism. The third — associated with Sonia Sikka, F. M. Barnard, and Michael Forster — reconstructs Herder as an "enlightened" or "qualified" relativist who combines a thin universal human nature with a robust contextualism about how virtue, happiness, and the good life are culturally specified. As Zammito, Menges, and Menze (2010) document, a decisive shift in Herder scholarship over the past quarter century has moved the field away from the older nationalist-relativist reading toward this more philosophically nuanced terrain — but the shift has not resolved the underlying disagreement.

### 2.1 Berlin's Pluralist Reading and the Relativist Alternative

The locus classicus for the pluralist interpretation is Berlin's *Vico and Herder* (1976), subsequently revised and expanded in *Three Critics of the Enlightenment* (Berlin 2014). Berlin argues that Herder is the founding figure of value pluralism: each culture possesses its own "center of gravity" (*Schwerpunkt*), its own incommensurable standard of achievement, and no overarching metric can rank these against one another. Crucially for Berlin, this pluralism does not collapse into relativism, because the values realized in different cultures are authentically human values — expressions of a shared human nature — rather than merely parochial preferences. The incommensurability thesis thus operates within a universal frame: cultures cannot be ranked on a single scale, but they all fall within the space of genuinely human possibilities.

Against this, Whitton (1988) advances a contrasting communitarian-relativist reading. Whitton argues that Herder's fundamental critique of Enlightenment rationalism — his insistence that reason and morality are meaningful only when embedded in the particular cultural communities that give them content — actually undermines the universalist backstop on which Berlin relies. If the norms of a culture are intelligible only from within, and if the *Humanität* ideal provides no independent leverage over how those norms develop, then Herder's position yields a form of cultural relativism in practice, whatever its formal universalist commitments. Whitton further notes an internal instability: Herder's positive vision of cultural community itself borrows normative resources from the Enlightenment project he critiques, leaving his anti-universalism without a stable foundation of its own. This reading anticipates later concerns about whether the *Schwerpunkt* doctrine can coherently be combined with any cross-cultural evaluative standard.

### 2.2 Enlightened and Qualified Relativism

The most sustained alternative to Berlin's pluralism is the "enlightened relativism" developed by Sikka across her 2005 article and her 2011 monograph. Sikka (2011) argues that Herder holds both that a common human nature grounds basic goods and that virtue and happiness are irreducibly culturally specific. This combination blocks both imperial universalism — the imposition of a single cultural model — and thoroughgoing moral relativism — the view that no trans-cultural criteria for judgment exist at all. *Humanität*, on Sikka's reading, functions as a genuine but thin cross-cultural standard: it identifies the goods any human life requires without specifying a single cultural template for realizing them. Sikka (2005) makes the philosophical structure explicit: Herder presupposes a set of basic human goods while insisting that "the happiness and virtue of individuals can only be measured in relation to their specific values." The position is not a compromise but a principled alternative to both poles.

Barnard (2003) develops a complementary account focused on the relationship between cultural particularity and universal humanity. For Barnard, Herder's nationalism is grounded in the conviction that each culture preserves incommensurable distinctiveness not despite *Humanität* but as a contribution to it: diverse cultural realizations collectively constitute the full expression of human possibility. This reading frames incommensurability not as a block to evaluation but as a condition of human flourishing — the diversity of peoples is constitutive of the human good rather than an obstacle to it. The critical question Barnard's account must answer is whether this integrative function of *Humanität* supplies a substantive trans-historical standard or merely a formal telos that cannot support any particular cross-cultural judgment.

Forster (2018) addresses the same question from a systematic philosophical perspective. His reconstruction of Herder's full philosophical system acknowledges the relativistic impulses in Herder's texts — the claim that "each epoch carries its standard within itself" — while arguing they are partially restrained by an anthropological universalism concerning basic human capacities. Forster does not claim the tensions are fully resolved; rather, he argues they are tensions within a sophisticated philosophical position rather than signs of incoherence. Spencer (2007) adds a further dimension by arguing that Herder's pluralism is not only compatible with cross-cultural exchange but actually requires it: cultures learn from one another, and Herder's opposition to cultural domination is distinct from any opposition to cultural contact. This reading directly challenges the interpretive tendency to treat Herder's *Schwerpunkt* doctrine as licensing cultural closure.

### 2.3 Challenges to the Berlin and Counter-Enlightenment Framing

The past two decades have seen sustained challenges not just to Berlin's specific characterization of Herder's pluralism but to the entire narrative framework within which that characterization operates. Norton (2007) argues that the "Counter-Enlightenment" is Berlin's intellectual construction rather than a genuine historical movement. The thinkers Berlin groups together — Herder, Hamann, Vico — did not share a unified opposition to the Enlightenment, and Berlin's numerous accounts of their thought, Norton contends, reflect not their own positions but late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century German historicist ideology that sought to legitimate anti-democratic politics through a false genealogy. If Norton is right, the narrative structure that gives Berlin's pluralist reading its force — Herder as the great opponent of Enlightenment monism — is significantly undermined.

Piirimäe (2015) offers a more focused critique, arguing that Berlin's placement of Herder within a Counter-Enlightenment framework distorts his actual political and historical thought in specific ways. Berlin overemphasizes cultural particularism and nationalism while underplaying Herder's cosmopolitan commitments, his anti-colonialism, and his substantive engagement with Enlightenment ideals. The result, for Piirimäe, is a picture of Herder that is "deeply misleading." This argument is developed at length in Piirimäe (2023), which reconstructs Herder's political thought as a response to Enlightenment political debates — concerning patriotism, commerce, and peace — rather than as their negation. The Berlin framing, on this account, has caused scholars to misread Herder as a cultural rather than political thinker and to underestimate the extent to which his pluralism operates within rather than against an Enlightenment framework.

Yack (2012) offers a different angle of critique. He accepts that Berlin is right to draw attention to Herder's significance as an adversary of the *philosophes*, but argues that Berlin misidentifies the real significance of Herder's critique. The importance of Herder's pluralism, Yack contends, lies not in stopping an ancient monist creed but in rehabilitating prejudice and cultural particularity as sources of human virtue and creativity — a critique that directly targeted the Enlightenment's core mission of removing obstacles to knowledge. Berlin's misidentification matters because it leads him to mischaracterize both the Enlightenment he opposes to Herder and the nature of Herder's pluralist alternative. Yack's reframing suggests that what Herder was criticizing was not universalism per se but a specific Enlightenment project of cultural rationalization.

Denby (2005) contributes yet another revisionist angle: comparing Herder with the natural historian Buffon, he argues that Herder's concept of *Kultur* as the process through which humanity develops is closer to the Enlightenment's unitary model of civilization than the Counter-Enlightenment reading allows. Herder's relativism, Denby concludes, is unstable because he retains universal criteria for transcultural judgment — an observation that supports Sikka's enlightened-relativism thesis while challenging the picture of Herder as a thinker systematically opposed to Enlightenment universalism.

Taken together, these challenges reveal that the debate over relativism and pluralism in Herder scholarship cannot be separated from a methodological debate about how the Enlightenment itself should be characterized. Whether Herder is a relativist, a pluralist, or an enlightened relativist depends substantially on what concept of universalism his position is measured against, and critics of Berlin's framework argue that framework imported a distorted picture of both Herder and the Enlightenment from a politically tendentious German historicist tradition. What remains unresolved is precisely the status of *Humanität* as a normative concept: whether it functions as a substantive trans-historical standard or merely as a thin regulative ideal is the pivotal question, and the answer determines whether Herder's incommensurability doctrine succeeds in blocking relativism or merely relocates it. This question passes directly to the examination of *Humanität*, providence, and the normative telos in Section 3.

## Section 3: *Humanität*, Providence, and the Normative Telos

### 3.1 The Telos and the Equal Worth of Epochs

*Humanität* is the central normative category of Herder's philosophy of history, yet its conceptual status has remained contested from the mid-twentieth century to the present. The interpretive fault line runs through Wells's (1960) observation that Herder's corpus contains what appear to be two incompatible philosophies: the *Auch eine Philosophie der Geschichte* (1774) insists on the intrinsic worth of each epoch and resists ranking cultures by proximity to any modern ideal, while the *Ideen zur Philosophie der Geschichte der Menschheit* (1784–91) posits *Humanität* as a genuine developmental goal toward which history advances. For Wells, these commitments represent a genuine inconsistency rather than a productive tension, and no interpretive sleight of hand can reconcile them.

Much of the subsequent literature has been organized around contesting or refining this verdict. James (2022) argues that the equal worth of epochs and a weak directional telos are not only compatible but mutually required in Herder's account. Each epoch, on James's reading, contributes something qualitatively distinctive to the full development of humanity; this distinctive contribution is precisely what confers equal standing, not despite the teleological framework but through it. The Enlightenment itself, Herder's favored target of criticism, makes an irreplaceable contribution — abstract thinking and the critique of prejudice — without this making it superior to ages it has superseded. Bollacher (2017) approaches the same problem through what he calls Herder's "globe ontology": the spherical earth, in Herder's *Ideen*, is a structuring image for the relation between cultural particularity and universal humanity. *Humanität* is not a standard external to the diversity of peoples but something realized only through it; the globe contains the multitude of particular cultures as a matrix rather than subordinating them to a hierarchy. Bristow (2025) proposes the most sustained recent reconciliation: the tension between the incomparability thesis and the history thesis is dissolved not through a philosophical argument but through the character of Herder's practice. His universal history is empirical, critical, skeptical, and open-ended — a philosophical inquiry into the meaning of human existence comparable in structure, though not in content, to Hegel's *Phenomenology of Spirit*. On this reading, *Humanität* functions as a regulative horizon motivating inquiry rather than a determinative endpoint foreclosing it.

### 3.2 Spinozism, Providence, and Perfectionist Ethics

Behind the interpretive question about the conceptual status of *Humanität* lies a deeper metaphysical one: what theological or ontological framework underwrites the claim that history has any normative direction at all? Herder's *Gott: Einige Gespräche* (1787) provides the most concentrated answer. Hampton (2015) shows that Herder's contribution to the Pantheism Controversy involved a creative transformation of Spinoza rather than simple adoption: drawing on the Cambridge Platonist Ralph Cudworth's concept of "plastic nature" — a middle principle mediating between transcendent reason and mechanical materialism — Herder replaced Spinoza's static substance-monism with the dynamic concept of *Kraft* (active force). The resulting metaphysics is a teleological naturalism in which a divine power pervades all things as an immanent organizing force, enabling genuine development rather than mere mechanistic unfolding.

Forster (2012) reinforces this interpretation, arguing that Herder's engagement with Spinoza introduced developmental and teleological elements absent from Spinoza's own system. Far from being a conventional Spinozist, Herder transformed the Spinozist framework into something capable of grounding a philosophy of historical development: divine immanence, reconceived through *Kraft*, becomes the source of the directional energy that moves cultures and peoples toward *Humanität*. Solanki (2024) extends this analysis to Herder's comparative method, showing how the Spinozist theology of *Gott* grounds his analogical approach to both nature and culture. Herder's comparativism posits phenomena as distinct instantiations of a common divine-natural creative power (*poesis*), resisting both the assimilation of differences and the imposition of static hierarchies. This framework illuminates how the telos of *Humanität* can orient historical understanding without producing a ranked hierarchy of cultures: all are expressions of the same underlying *Kraft*, each in its own singular manner.

Crowe (2012) argues that this metaphysical framework is not merely background but constitutive of Herder's moral philosophy. Herder's perfectionism — the thesis that human beings have a nature that is to be developed through Bildung and cultural life — requires a divine providential order to supply its normative force. Without theistic grounding, the perfectionist telos would float free as a merely human aspiration, liable to be contested or revised. Crowe's reading places Herder's perfectionist ethics within what he identifies as a trinitarian structure: perfectionism (the human good as developmental achievement), sentimentalism (moral motivation through sympathy and feeling), and theism (the providential framework that justifies and deepens the first two elements). *Humanität* is thus neither a free-floating regulative ideal nor a vestigially theological residue, but the proper object of a theistically grounded moral perfectionism.

### 3.3 A Critical Limit: Race and the Structure of *Humanität*

The reconciliationist readings examined above share a broadly benign assessment of *Humanität* as a pluralist universal. Layne and Norberg (2023) challenge this consensus through a close reading of Herder's "Negro Idylls" in the tenth collection of the *Briefe zur Beförderung der Humanität*, Herder's most sustained late engagement with the concept. Their argument is structural: Herder's hope for the future redemption of humanity requires that Black slaves renounce retribution for historical wrongs — a demand that his concept of *Humanität* be "shored up" by a self-effacing conception of the Black subject. Despite Herder's genuine anti-slavery commitments and his repeated denunciations of colonialism, the *Briefe*'s account of humanity rests, on Layne and Norberg's reading, on the shoulders of Black subjects precisely by depriving those subjects of full humanity. The universalism of *Humanität* thus conceals a structural exclusion that benign cosmopolitan readings have not adequately confronted.

This critique matters beyond its specific textual target. The question of whether *Humanität* can do the normative work Herder assigns to it — blocking relativism while respecting particularity, grounding a universal ethics while denying the superiority of any epoch — is precisely the question that organizes the debates surveyed above. Layne and Norberg's analysis suggests that even in Herder's own late formulation of the concept, its universalism is purchased at a cost that his theological and metaphysical framework does not acknowledge. Whether that failure is internal to *Humanität* as Herder conceived it, or a failure of his application of an otherwise coherent concept, remains the core unresolved problem of this domain.

## Section 4: The Enlightenment and the Kant Controversy

### 4.1 Counter-Enlightenment or Enlightenment Reformer?

The dominant framework for situating Herder within the history of ideas has been Isaiah Berlin's "Counter-Enlightenment" thesis. In *Three Critics of the Enlightenment*, Berlin presents Herder as one of the founders of an intellectual current that rejected the core Enlightenment commitments to universal reason, scientific method applied to human life, the uniformity of human nature, and the ideology of unilinear progress (Berlin 2014). On Berlin's reading, Herder's cultural pluralism, his doctrine of incommensurable *Volksgeisters*, and his polemic against the French *philosophes* constitute a decisive break with the Enlightenment project. The reception of this framing has been so extensive that assessing it is virtually inseparable from assessing Herder's philosophy of history as a whole.

Revisionist scholarship has subjected Berlin's thesis to sustained challenge on both historiographical and textual grounds. Norton (2007) argues that the "Counter-Enlightenment" is a conceptual invention without adequate basis in the primary sources: the thinkers Berlin groups under this label — Herder, Hamann, and others — did not share a unified opposition to the Enlightenment but had complex, often continuous relationships to its themes. On Norton's account, Berlin's reading reflects not Herder's actual positions but the ideological constructions of late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century German historians who wished to legitimate anti-democratic politics through a false genealogy. Lestition (2007), responding to Norton in the same journal issue, defends a qualified version of the Counter-Enlightenment concept, conceding weaknesses in Berlin's formulation while insisting that genuinely oppositional patterns in German thought cannot be dissolved into seamless Enlightenment continuity. The exchange marks the current methodological limit of the dispute: what minimal content the Counter-Enlightenment concept must retain to be analytically useful, and whether that content maps onto Herder at all.

Piirimäe (2015) argues that Berlin's framework is deeply misleading about Herder specifically: by overemphasizing cultural particularism and proto-nationalism, Berlin obscures Herder's cosmopolitan commitments, his critique of colonialism, and his positive engagement with Enlightenment ideals of *Humanität* and progress through diversity. In her book-length reconstruction, Piirimäe (2023) situates Herder's political thought within Enlightenment debates on patriotism, commerce, and peace, showing Herder in substantive dialogue with Rousseau, Montesquieu, and Ferguson — dialogue that reveals a thinker reforming the Enlightenment's political categories from within rather than repudiating them. Revisionist readings of this kind converge with Sikka's (2011) "enlightened relativism" and Denby's (2005) argument that Herder's concept of *Kultur* is closer to a unitary Enlightenment model of civilization than is generally acknowledged, with Herder retaining universal criteria for transcultural judgment even while attacking Eurocentric teleology.

James (2022) offers a philosophically precise account of Herder's dialectical relationship to the Enlightenment: rather than rejecting it, Herder subjects it to the same genetic and contextual analysis he applies to any cultural epoch, treating it as one historical formation among others that makes a distinctive but not superior contribution to the development of humanity. This meta-historical stance explains how Herder can criticize Enlightenment abstraction and linear progress while acknowledging the legitimate aspirations of the Enlightenment project. Yack (2012) reframes the significance of Berlin's thesis by arguing that Berlin was engaged in a political as much as a historical project, mobilizing the Counter-Enlightenment thinkers as resources for value pluralism against liberal universalism and totalitarianism. Beiser (2011), while accepting that Herder is the founder of historicism and its systematic alternative to Enlightenment universalism, also situates this within a philosophically principled critique of Voltairian historiography rather than mere irrationalism. Lifschitz (2016) adds a genealogical dimension, tracing Berlin's Counter-Enlightenment concept to its structural debt to Friedrich Meinecke's *Die Entstehung des Historismus*, which contrasted French rationalism with German historicism and Romanticism — suggesting that Berlin's framing inherits a German philosophical tradition that compromises its historical objectivity.

The weight of recent scholarship thus positions Herder as an internal critic of the Enlightenment rather than its opponent, though debate persists about how much universalism his critique actually preserves.

### 4.2 The Kant–Herder Controversy over the *Ideen*

The deepest intellectual fault line in Herder's philosophy of history is marked by Kant's hostile reviews of the first two parts of the *Ideen* in the *Allgemeine Literatur-Zeitung* (1785). These reviews did more than register a personal rupture between a former student and his teacher: they inaugurated a fundamental dispute over methodology, teleology, and the proper scope of philosophical inquiry into human history. Zammito's (2002) study traces the full arc of this confrontation, arguing that the Kant–Herder controversy was generative of the conceptual space in which both anthropology and biology emerged as modern disciplines. The dispute turned on irreconcilable differences over empirical inquiry, the concept of race, and what Zammito calls the clash between Herder's organismic and genetic approach to history and Kant's transcendental framework.

At the philosophical core of the controversy lay the question of teleology. Nassar (2015) provides a precise account of how the two thinkers deploy analogical reasoning differently in their accounts of organic nature and history. Herder uses analogy to establish a continuum between organic wholes and historical cultures, treating teleology as constitutive — built into the structure of natural and historical life. Kant insists, by contrast, that teleological judgments are merely regulative: they are heuristic tools for organizing inquiry, not claims about the objective structure of things. Kant's objection in the first *Ideen* review — documented in close philosophical detail by Zuckert (2014), whose analysis is noted here — is that Herder illegitimately hypostasizes what should remain a heuristic analogy between organisms and souls, thereby making constitutive metaphysical claims that exceed the bounds of legitimate cognition. Herder's philosophical formation under Kant in Königsberg (1762–64) gave him intimate acquaintance with the pre-critical philosophy; his subsequent departures from it were, on Beiser's (1987) reading, a principled philosophical response to the crisis generated by Kantian critique, situating Herder as a philosophically serious critic of transcendental philosophy whose naturalism anticipates later historicist and Romantic responses rather than simply representing a pre-critical holdover.

Gjesdal (2017) connects Herder's hermeneutical approach — attending to the particular, the historical, and the embodied — with his resistance to Enlightenment rationalism, showing that the methodological disagreement with Kant is not merely technical but reflects a deep divergence over what understanding human cultural life requires. For Herder, adequate understanding of history demands immersion in the particular rather than the application of transcendental categories; Kant's framework, with its a priori structures of cognition, is precisely what Herder's genetic and analogical method contests. The controversy over the *Ideen* thus marks the site where the master tension in Herder's philosophy — between particular worth and universal telos — becomes a technical philosophical dispute: Herder's constitutive teleology attempts to ground both the individuality of cultures and their directional development within nature itself, while Kant's regulative restriction of teleological judgment makes such a grounding illegitimate.

The Kant–Herder dispute is, in this sense, terminological as much as substantive on the question of Enlightenment affiliation, but metaphysically fundamental on the question of historical understanding. Whether Herder's naturalistic teleology constitutes a coherent philosophical alternative to Kantian critique or an unguarded speculation beyond permissible bounds remains unresolved — and the resolution bears directly on whether *Humanität* can function as anything more than a regulative idea in Herder's own terms.

## Section 5: Hermeneutics, *Einfühlung*, and Historical Understanding

Herder's claim that each culture carries "its center of gravity within itself" is not merely a metaphysical thesis about cultural individuality; it is also a methodological demand on the interpreter of history. To grasp an epoch genuinely, one must enter its self-understanding from the inside — a capacity Herder calls *Einfühlung*, empathetic feeling-into. The epistemology underwriting this method is grounded in his philosophy of language, particularly in the *Treatise on the Origin of Language* (1772), and has generated a sustained debate about both its philosophical foundations and its limits.

### 5.1 Language, *Einfühlung*, and the Forster–Gjesdal Axis

The most influential reconstruction of these foundations is Michael Forster's. In *After Herder* (2010), Forster argues that Herder established three principles that transform philosophy of language and, derivatively, hermeneutics: thought is essentially dependent on language, languages differ deeply in their conceptual contents rather than merely their sounds, and understanding an alien culture therefore requires imaginative immersion in its linguistic-conceptual world rather than translation into one's own scheme. Because meaning is language-specific and culturally embedded, interpretation demands what Forster calls a "genetic" approach — grasping how a culture grew into its particular forms of expression. This argument connects the *Treatise* directly to the method of historical understanding deployed in the *Auch eine Philosophie* and the *Ideen*: the same dependence of thought on language that makes conceptual diversity a fact about human cognition is what makes *Einfühlung* methodologically necessary.

Kristin Gjesdal's *Herder's Hermeneutics* (2017) challenges this systematic reading by resituating Herder within a richer intellectual context: mid-eighteenth-century historical, anthropological, and poetic discourse, rather than a philosophical program awaiting technical development. For Gjesdal, Herder's interpretive philosophy is constitutively shaped by aesthetic experience, cross-cultural encounter, and the limits of reason as explored in Enlightenment anthropology and literary culture. The effect is to displace Forster's "strong Herder" — Herder as the founder of a technical theory of interpretation — with a figure whose contribution is inseparable from a specific historical moment and set of debates. Gjesdal also argues, in earlier work, that Gadamer's canonical narrative of hermeneutics systematically undervalues Herder's role while overvaluing Schleiermacher's, attributing this distortion to Gadamer's own philosophical agenda (Gjesdal 2008). Recovering Herder's priority thus demands revising the standard genealogy of the hermeneutic tradition.

The two positions are not simply incompatible. Niels Wildschut (2020) analyzes how Herder deploys analogy and *Einfühlung* as complementary rather than competing strategies for overcoming the incommensurability between epochs. Analogy supplies structural bridges across cultural difference — by identifying shared patterns in how different human forms develop — while *Einfühlung* provides the imaginative-affective dimension needed to grasp an epoch's inner self-understanding. Wildschut argues that incommensurability in Herder functions primarily as a critical tool for delegitimizing ethnocentric interpretations rather than as a positive thesis about the impossibility of understanding. Eva Piirimäe's introduction to the volume *Herder on Empathy and Sympathy* (2020) further demonstrates that *Einfühlung* and *Sympathie* are not peripheral rhetorical devices but permeate Herder's entire philosophy — from aesthetics and poetics through historiography to moral education — and are transformed from their sources in Hutcheson, Hume, and Smith through Herder's expressivist philosophy of language.

Recent work has extended the analysis to the *Treatise* itself. Tanvi Solanki (2022) argues that the *Treatise* develops an "aural theory of cultural diversity" grounded in attentive listening to the sounds and phonological structures of diverse languages. Through Herder's critical readings of colonial travelogues, Solanki shows that the philosophy of language is rooted in embodied, situated listening practices rather than abstract linguistic universalism — and that this attentiveness to sonic difference is precisely what European visual and scriptural paradigms systematically suppress. Paulo Alexandre e Castro (2025) reconstructs Herder's four natural laws of language in the *Treatise*, emphasizing the constitutive role of *Besonnenheit* (reflective awareness) and the priority of audition, and argues that Herder's naturalistic-anthropological approach to the emergence of language anticipates contemporary philosophy of mind in ways that remain underappreciated.

### 5.2 The Limits of *Einfühlung* and the Human-Sciences Legacy

The epistemological coherence of *Einfühlung* has not gone unchallenged. Roey Reichert (2023) accepts the broad alignment of Herder's method with the Enlightenment tradition but identifies three limits that sympathetic reconstructions leave unaddressed. First, unlimited *Einfühlung* risks collapsing into relativism: if every epoch is understood purely on its own terms, cross-epochal evaluation becomes incoherent. Second, the method can be reductive, eliminating rather than preserving difference by assimilating the alien to the familiar through projection. Third, relying solely on *Einfühlung* is speculative and lacks an external truth criterion against which empathetic interpretations could be assessed. These limits bear directly on the wider question this review has tracked: Herder's claim that each epoch carries its standard within itself may be hermeneutically necessary but epistemologically insufficient on its own, and the relativism Reichert identifies as a risk is precisely the charge that the pluralism debate of Section 2 turns on.

The broader tradition downstream of Herder provides context for evaluating these limits. Karsten Stueber (2012) traces the debate about *Verstehen* as the distinctive method of the human sciences from Droysen and Dilthey to contemporary philosophy of mind and action, arguing that understanding other agents requires both imaginative re-enactment and the application of rational interpretive norms — a more nuanced position than either strict *Verstehen* advocates or naturalist critics typically allow. Herder figures as an upstream contributor to this tradition, prior to Dilthey's systematic formulation, supplying the intuition that cultural-historical understanding is irreducible to causal explanation while leaving unresolved the epistemological question of how such understanding is validated. The coherence of "each epoch carries its standard within itself" thus remains an open problem linking Herder's hermeneutics directly back to the relativism/pluralism debate with which the present review is centrally concerned.

## Section 6: Volk, Nation, and the Politics of Herder's Legacy

### 6.1 Cosmopolitan-Pluralist vs. Nationalist-Precursor Readings

The question of whether Herder's *Volk* concept makes him a resource for pluralist cosmopolitanism or an inadvertent seed of ethnic nationalism is among the most politically charged in the secondary literature, and it maps directly onto the master tension examined throughout this review: the relationship between cultural particularity and the universal telos of *Humanität*.

The dominant interpretation in the Anglophone literature, established by Barnard (2003) and developed by Spencer (2012), holds that Herder's nationalism is cultural and expressivist rather than ethnic or statist. For Barnard, Herder's core conviction is that human creativity is necessarily embedded in the particular culture of a communal language, and that each culture's incommensurable distinctiveness is a condition of universal human flourishing rather than a threat to it. Particular peoples are not rivals in a zero-sum contest but distinct realizations of a shared *Humanität*; cultural pluralism is, on this reading, the very mechanism through which the universal telos of history is achieved. Barnard also argues that Herder was alert to the dangers of ethnic fanaticism and what we would now call globalization, making him a prescient critic of cultural homogenization rather than a proto-chauvinist. Spencer (2012) extends this account through an expressivist theory of language: because language constitutes individual identity and communal culture alike, legitimate political community must enable the flourishing of the linguistic-cultural *Volk*. Spencer defends Herder against the charge that his pluralism issues in cultural closure, arguing instead that it supports a multicultural liberal model in which cultural interaction and exchange are not merely tolerated but required (Spencer 2007).

Isaiah Berlin (2014) occupies a more ambivalent position. He credits Herder with inventing the concepts of *Volksgeist*, cultural belonging, and incommensurability — insights Berlin regarded as philosophically genuine — but he also identifies Herder as a transitional figure whose vocabulary was susceptible to radicalization by later nationalists. Berlin does not condemn Herder; he reads him as a benign pluralist. Yet by framing Herder as a forerunner of modern nationalism, Berlin simultaneously opens the question of misappropriation that Barnard and Spencer seek to close. Whitton (1988) offers a more critical analysis: Herder's attack on Enlightenment cosmopolitanism as "abstract" and his privileging of cultural embeddedness over universal reason, Whitton argues, create an internal instability in his position, since his positive vision of cultural community still borrows normative resources from the Enlightenment he critiques. This instability, rather than any explicit ethnic politics, is where the risks of appropriation are concentrated.

Piirimäe (2023) advances the discussion by arguing that Herder has been systematically underread as a political thinker. Approaching him primarily as a philosopher of culture and history, prior commentators missed that his programme for *Volk*, language, and historical development was driven by a coherent political agenda responding to the specific challenges of the Holy Roman Empire and Enlightenment modernity — debates on natural law, commercial society, and modern patriotism. On this reading, the cultural-nationalist versus cosmopolitan dichotomy that has structured the Barnard–Berlin axis is a false choice: Herder's cultural commitments were always already political. Patten's analytical reconstruction, which argues that Herder defends both cultural and political nationalism grounded in an individuality principle and a nationality principle, represents a further challenge to Barnard's consensus, though it remains incompletely integrated into the discussion.

### 6.2 Race, Colonialism, and Non-European Peoples

The question of Herder's anti-colonialism introduces a distinct strand into the debate about his political legacy, one where the cosmopolitan reading draws its strongest evidential support — and its sharpest recent challenge.

Spencer (2015) argues, through a sustained comparison with Kant, that Herder's insistence on the integrity and equal value of each *Volk* generates a more principled and consistent opposition to colonial domination than Kant's universalism. Where Kant's coupling of nation and state creates resources for justifying "civilising" interventions, Herder's separation of cultural community from political sovereignty, combined with his rejection of racial taxonomy, yields a proto-anti-colonial politics. Sikka (2011) reinforces this by showing that Herder's views on non-European peoples are more respectful and less condescending than those of his contemporaries: his "enlightened relativism" resists Eurocentric hierarchies while retaining a common *Humanität* that prevents cultural difference from licensing indifference to others' suffering. Denby (2005) complicates this picture by pointing out that Herder's concept of *Kultur* as the process through which humanity develops operates as a quasi-Enlightenment category — his critique of colonial progress is unstable because he holds onto universal criteria for transcultural judgment that could in principle support the very hierarchical thinking he opposes.

The sharpest challenge to the anti-colonial reading comes from Layne and Norberg (2023), whose analysis of Herder's "Negro Idylls" in the *Briefe zur Beförderung der Humanität* argues that the structure of Herder's political philosophy is "shored up" by a particular conception of the Black slave as a figure who willingly renounces retribution for historical wrongs, thereby facilitating the future redemption of humanity. Despite Herder's genuine commitment to the abolition of slavery, Layne and Norberg contend that his hope for *Humanität* as a whole rests on the self-effacement of Black subjects — a structural exclusion within the universalism, not merely an incidental failure of historical perspective. This argument does not straightforwardly vindicate the ethnic-nationalist reading, but it complicates any comfortable rehabilitation of Herder as a multicultural pluralist, and it raises the broader question of whether the telos of *Humanität* is genuinely egalitarian or implicitly hierarchical in its demands.

Whether Herder is a resource for pluralist cosmopolitanism or a seed of *völkisch* ideology thus turns, as the section summary of the outline anticipates, on precisely the same telos/particularity tension that runs through every other debate in this literature: what *Humanität* can actually underwrite, and at whose expense the universal is made to cohere.

## Section 7: Reception: Romanticism, Historicism, and the Human Sciences

### 7.1 Romanticism, Idealism, and Hegel

Herder's downstream influence on German Romanticism and post-Kantian Idealism was extensive, though largely unacknowledged by those who benefited from it. Forster (2018) argues that this systematic underattribution was not accidental but structural: Herder's alienation from both the Kantian and Goethean intellectual factions by the early nineteenth century meant that successors such as Schleiermacher, Schlegel, Schelling, Hegel, and Humboldt absorbed Herderian ideas while publicly distancing themselves from their source. What was transmitted ranged from the thought–language dependence thesis and cultural holism to the expressivist metaphysics of *Kraft* that animated Romantic *Naturphilosophie*.

Hampton (2015) identifies a specific mechanism of that transmission. Herder's revision of Spinoza in the Pantheism Controversy — replacing Spinoza's inert single substance with the active principle *Kraft*, drawing on the Cambridge Platonist Ralph Cudworth's concept of "plastic nature" — provided the metaphysical catalyst that inaugurated the Romantic movement. This revised Spinozism, with its active, self-organizing force immanent in nature and culture alike, was taken up by Schelling and became a foundation for Romantic *Naturphilosophie*'s organic conception of natural development. The transmission was thus not merely of broad cultural themes but of specific doctrinal moves that later became canonical.

The Herder–Hegel relationship has attracted particular scrutiny because it marks the point at which Herderian historicism was systematized and teleologized in ways that arguably distorted its original pluralism. Gjesdal (2006) argues that Herder developed a philosophically coherent account of the historicity of art and reason some sixty years before Hegel's Berlin lectures, but without the essentialist framework in which art's historical development culminates in its own supersession. Where Herder's account is resolutely non-essentialist — cultures and epochs carry their standards within themselves, and no external endpoint licenses a hierarchy among them — Hegel's idealism reintroduced a teleological structure in which history's end is both knowable and legitimating. The "end of art" thesis that follows from Hegel's essentialism is, on Gjesdal's reading, precisely what Herder's historicism resists. The Herderian inheritance in Hegel is thus real but filtered through a transformation that inverts one of its most distinctive commitments.

### 7.2 Historicism, Dilthey, Gadamer, and Boasian Anthropology

The transmission from Herder to the nineteenth-century historicist tradition — Ranke, Droysen, Dilthey — constitutes the second major line of reception, and it is through this lineage that Herder came to be identified with the *Geisteswissenschaften* and their distinctive interpretive methods. Beiser (2011) traces German historicism as a philosophically substantive program — the claim that human thought, values, and institutions are historically conditioned and can only be understood through historical inquiry — and places Herder at its origins, crediting him with establishing the anti-naturalist and particularist foundations that Ranke and Droysen elaborated in historiographic practice. For Beiser, historicism is not mere relativism but a positive methodological commitment with Herder as its originating figure.

The Herder–Dilthey connection is more complex than this foundationalist picture suggests. Makkreel (1969) situates Dilthey as the crucial mediator between Herderian historicism and twentieth-century hermeneutics, noting that Dilthey's insistence on historical individuality and the irreducibility of human expression to natural-scientific categories carries a recognizably Herderian inheritance. Yet Hamid (2023) complicates this picture by showing that in Dilthey's earliest work of the 1860s–70s, the new empirical science of anthropology pulled him toward naturalistic positions contrary to the historicist hermeneutics of his teachers. The Herderian inheritance was thus not smoothly received but negotiated against rival tendencies: the same ambiguity between empirical naturalism about human development and cultural-historical particularism that marked Herder's own project reappeared in Dilthey's struggle to ground the *Geisteswissenschaften*.

Gadamer's relationship to this line of transmission has been critically assessed. Gjesdal (2008) argues that Gadamer's account of the origins of modern hermeneutics systematically undervalues Herder while overvaluing Schleiermacher, a distortion shaped by Gadamer's own philosophical agenda. Harrington (2000) further shows that Gadamer's and Habermas's shared critique of Dilthey as covertly "objectivist" — for grounding interpretive validity in psychological re-experiencing (*Nacherleben*) rather than normative dialogue — misreads Dilthey's later work and, by extension, the Herderian tradition from which it descends. The hermeneutic tradition did not simply inherit Herder's *Einfühlung*; it debated and revised it, sometimes by misidentifying what the inheritance actually contained.

The Herder–Boas transmission represents a third distinct reception line, running through the institutional founding of cultural anthropology in the early twentieth century. Denby (2005) argues that Herder's insistence on cultural particularity, organic cultural unity, and the incommensurability of cultural values provided the conceptual vocabulary — mediated through German Romanticism — that Boas and his school institutionalized as cultural relativism and participant-observation methodology. Forster (2018) extends this argument, tracing specific conceptual borrowings from Herder through Schlegel and Humboldt to Boas and Malinowski, and contending that Herder's own versions of the enabling principles were in important respects superior to what the disciplines subsequently developed. Across all three reception lines, the pattern is consistent: Herder's successors selectively inherited his commitments, often resolving or suppressing the tensions — between particular worth and universal telos, between naturalistic anthropology and cultural particularism — that give his philosophy of history its distinctive difficulty.

## Conclusion

The secondary literature on Herder's philosophy of history is organized, almost without exception, by a single structural tension: the commitment to the intrinsic and equal worth of each culture and epoch stands in apparent conflict with a directional, universal history aiming at *Humanität*. Wells (1960) named this the problem of "two philosophies," and subsequent scholarship has never fully escaped the question he posed. What has changed significantly over the past two decades is the range of positions considered viable. Zammito, Menges, and Menze (2010) documented a major historiographical shift away from the older nationalist, irrationalist, and relativist readings — the picture inherited from a selective Rankean appropriation of Herder — toward accounts that foreground his Enlightenment commitments, his thin universalism, and the providential architecture of the *Ideen*. The revisionist consensus, developed by Sikka (2011), Barnard (2003), Forster (2010), and Piirimäe (2023), treats Herder as a qualified or enlightened relativist who combines robust cultural contextualism with a normative core that blocks thoroughgoing relativism.

Yet this reconciliationist turn remains contested on multiple fronts. Whitton (1988) argues for a communitarian-relativist reading that Berlin's pluralism fails to displace. Norton (2007) challenges the very concept of the Counter-Enlightenment that structures Berlin's framing of Herder, a challenge extended by Piirimäe (2023) who argues that Berlin's interpretive lens systematically distorts Herder's actual political and philosophical commitments. Reichert (2023) presses three internal limits on the *Einfühlung* methodology — relativism, reductionism, and the absence of any external criterion of adequacy — that resist easy dissolution. Most pointedly, Layne and Norberg (2023) argue that *Humanität* in the *Briefe* is structurally underwritten by a self-effacing representation of the enslaved Black person, locating an exclusion internal to the universalism rather than merely contingently attached to it. These critical voices do not simply recycle older objections; they identify structural problems within the positions most favored by the revisionist consensus.

Several questions remain genuinely open. First, the normative status of *Humanität* is unresolved: whether it functions as a substantive trans-historical standard capable of grounding cross-cultural moral judgment, as Crowe (2012) maintains on theistic-perfectionist grounds, or as a thin regulative horizon that cannot bear that weight, remains disputed. Second, the Spinozist metaphysics that grounds Herder's account of *Kraft* and development raises unresolved questions about whether his providentialism is ultimately theistic or naturalizable — a question with direct consequences for whether the telos is genuinely universal or merely the projection of a particular theological commitment. Third, the "father of historicism" attribution, while complicated by Beiser (2011) and Zammito's revisionist account (2009) of how the Rankean tradition selectively appropriated Herder, has not been replaced by a stable alternative characterization of his legacy. Fourth, the epistemological coherence of *Einfühlung* — whether the hermeneutic program Forster (2010) attributes to Herder can satisfy the demands Reichert (2023) identifies — turns on issues in the philosophy of understanding that the literature has not resolved.

The scholarship also flags several underexplored areas. The specific transmission path from Herder to Ranke and the early historicist school remains less studied than the Herder–Dilthey or Herder–Gadamer lines documented by Gjesdal (2008) and Harrington (2000). Non-Western and postcolonial reception of Herder is largely absent from the current literature, a gap that Layne and Norberg's (2023) analysis of race and *Humanität* makes newly pressing. The integration of Herder's natural-scientific interests — climate, physiology, epigenesis — with his philosophy of history has been advanced by work on *Kraft* and analogy but remains unevenly treated in the philosophical literature. And Forster's (2018) claim that Herder founds the tradition of cultural anthropology running to Boas, while documented in outline, invites more systematic treatment than it has so far received.

What the literature establishes beyond reasonable dispute is that Herder cannot be reduced to either the nationalist ideologue of older polemics or the benign cosmopolitan pluralist of some rehabilitative readings. The debates reviewed here — over relativism and pluralism, *Humanität* and telos, *Einfühlung* and method, *Volk* and politics — each return to the same unresolved core: whether a philosophy of history that insists on the intrinsic worth of every particular form of human life can simultaneously articulate a universal normative standard without suppressing or distorting what it claims to honor.
