## Introduction

Johann Gottfried Herder (1744–1803) occupies an unusual position in the history of philosophy: a thinker who is simultaneously indispensable and contested, claimed by incompatible traditions, and credited — or blamed — for ideas he may not have held. His two major contributions to the philosophy of history, *Auch eine Philosophie der Geschichte zur Bildung der Menschheit* (1774) and the four-volume *Ideen zur Philosophie der Geschichte der Menschheit* (1784–91), together constitute one of the most ambitious attempts in the modern period to think through how particular human communities and a universal human destiny can belong within a single coherent account of historical development. That ambition generates the tension which has organized Herder scholarship for more than a century: the commitment to the individuality and equal worth of each culture and epoch sits uneasily alongside an equally strong commitment to a directional history oriented toward *Humanität*. Wells (1960) named this the problem of Herder's "two philosophies of history," and nearly every major debate in the secondary literature — about relativism and pluralism, about the Counter-Enlightenment, about nationalism and cosmopolitanism, about providence and telos — can be read as a variation on whether and how that tension can be resolved.

The secondary literature has produced four broad interpretive positions, all of which inform the discussions that follow. The first, associated with Friedrich Meinecke's account of the origins of historicism and systematized in English by Beiser (2011), reads Herder as the founding figure of the historicist tradition: his insistence on cultural individuality, organic development, and the immanence of historical standards constitutes a philosophical program that defines historicism as a distinctive mode of inquiry. Beiser reconstructs three central commitments — individuality, development, and immanence — as the core of Herder's historicist achievement, while acknowledging that his providential theology and the teleological structure of *Humanität* sit uneasily with the later positivist Rankean tradition that claimed him as ancestor. A second and more widely influential position in the Anglophone literature is Isaiah Berlin's, elaborated in *Vico and Herder* (1976) and the subsequent collection *Three Critics of the Enlightenment* (2000). For Berlin, Herder inaugurates value pluralism: genuine human values are multiple, incommensurable, and irreducibly diverse, yet because they are authentically human they do not license pure relativism. Herder thus appears as the "most formidable adversary" of Enlightenment monism — a figure of the Counter-Enlightenment who nonetheless preserves a thin universalism through his concept of shared humanity.

The third position, developed independently by Sikka (2011), Barnard (2003), and Forster (2018), reconstructs Herder as a qualified or enlightened relativist. On this reading, *Humanität* combines a thin universal conception of human nature and basic goods with a robust insistence that their realization is always culturally shaped and contextually specific. This position blocks both imperial universalism and thoroughgoing moral relativism without collapsing into Berlin's pluralism. A fourth position, associated with Zammito, Menges, and Menze (2010), Piirimäe (2023), and Denby (2005), emphasizes Herder's continuity with rather than opposition to Enlightenment values, situating his cultural thought within rather than against the Enlightenment tradition of natural science, anthropology, and political reform.

The last quarter century has seen a significant revision in Herder scholarship. Zammito, Menges, and Menze (2010) document a "tidal shift" away from the older nationalist-relativist-irrationalist picture that long dominated both German and Anglophone reception, toward a philosophically more nuanced account that recognizes the coherence of Herder's positions on language, history, and culture. The earlier reading drew on selective appropriations by nineteenth-century German nationalists; the revisionist scholarship has worked to disentangle Herder's own arguments from their subsequent ideological instrumentalizations. This does not, however, mean that the revisionist picture has gone uncontested. Critics including Norton (2007), Whitton (1988), and Layne and Norberg (2023) have pressed questions about the limits and contradictions of the benign cosmopolitan Herder — whether by challenging Berlin's historical narrative, by arguing for a genuinely relativist reading, or by identifying structural exclusions within *Humanität* itself.

This review traces these debates across seven interconnected domains: the model of historical development and the historicism question; the relativism and pluralism debate generated by Berlin's reading; the conceptual status of *Humanität* and its theological-metaphysical foundations; Herder's relation to Kant and the Enlightenment; his hermeneutic method and the epistemology of historical understanding; his theory of *Volk* and the politics of his reception; and the transmission of his ideas to subsequent traditions in historicism, the human sciences, and anthropology. Throughout, the aim is to track not only the dominant reconciliationist readings but the voices of dissent and critique that prevent those readings from becoming settled orthodoxy.
