## Section 4: The Enlightenment and the Kant Controversy

### Subsection 4.1: Counter-Enlightenment or Enlightenment Reformer?

The dominant framework for situating Herder within the history of ideas has been Isaiah Berlin's "Counter-Enlightenment" thesis. In *Three Critics of the Enlightenment*, Berlin presents Herder as one of the founders of an intellectual current that rejected the core Enlightenment commitments to universal reason, scientific method applied to human life, the uniformity of human nature, and the ideology of unilinear progress (Berlin 2014). On Berlin's reading, Herder's cultural pluralism, his doctrine of incommensurable *Volksgeisters*, and his polemic against the French *philosophes* constitute a decisive break with the Enlightenment project. The reception of this framing has been so extensive that assessing it is virtually inseparable from assessing Herder's philosophy of history as a whole.

Revisionist scholarship has subjected Berlin's thesis to sustained challenge on both historiographical and textual grounds. Norton (2007) argues that the "Counter-Enlightenment" is a conceptual invention without adequate basis in the primary sources: the thinkers Berlin groups under this label — Herder, Hamann, and others — did not share a unified opposition to the Enlightenment but had complex, often continuous relationships to its themes. On Norton's account, Berlin's reading reflects not Herder's actual positions but the ideological constructions of late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century German historians who wished to legitimate anti-democratic politics through a false genealogy. Lestition (2007), responding to Norton in the same journal issue, defends a qualified version of the Counter-Enlightenment concept, conceding weaknesses in Berlin's formulation while insisting that genuinely oppositional patterns in German thought cannot be dissolved into seamless Enlightenment continuity. The exchange marks the current methodological limit of the dispute: what minimal content the Counter-Enlightenment concept must retain to be analytically useful, and whether that content maps onto Herder at all.

Piirimäe (2015) argues that Berlin's framework is deeply misleading about Herder specifically: by overemphasizing cultural particularism and proto-nationalism, Berlin obscures Herder's cosmopolitan commitments, his critique of colonialism, and his positive engagement with Enlightenment ideals of *Humanität* and progress through diversity. In her book-length reconstruction, Piirimäe (2023) situates Herder's political thought within Enlightenment debates on patriotism, commerce, and peace, showing Herder in substantive dialogue with Rousseau, Montesquieu, and Ferguson — dialogue that reveals a thinker reforming the Enlightenment's political categories from within rather than repudiating them. Revisionist readings of this kind converge with Sikka's (2011) "enlightened relativism" and Denby's (2005) argument that Herder's concept of *Kultur* is closer to a unitary Enlightenment model of civilization than is generally acknowledged, with Herder retaining universal criteria for transcultural judgment even while attacking Eurocentric teleology.

James (2022) offers a philosophically precise account of Herder's dialectical relationship to the Enlightenment: rather than rejecting it, Herder subjects it to the same genetic and contextual analysis he applies to any cultural epoch, treating it as one historical formation among others that makes a distinctive but not superior contribution to the development of humanity. This meta-historical stance explains how Herder can criticize Enlightenment abstraction and linear progress while acknowledging the legitimate aspirations of the Enlightenment project. Yack (2012) reframes the significance of Berlin's thesis by arguing that Berlin was engaged in a political as much as a historical project, mobilizing the Counter-Enlightenment thinkers as resources for value pluralism against liberal universalism and totalitarianism. Beiser (2011), while accepting that Herder is the founder of historicism and its systematic alternative to Enlightenment universalism, also situates this within a philosophically principled critique of Voltairian historiography rather than mere irrationalism. Lifschitz (2016) adds a genealogical dimension, tracing Berlin's Counter-Enlightenment concept to its structural debt to Friedrich Meinecke's *Die Entstehung des Historismus*, which contrasted French rationalism with German historicism and Romanticism — suggesting that Berlin's framing inherits a German philosophical tradition that compromises its historical objectivity.

The weight of recent scholarship thus positions Herder as an internal critic of the Enlightenment rather than its opponent, though debate persists about how much universalism his critique actually preserves.

### Subsection 4.2: The Kant–Herder Controversy over the *Ideen*

The deepest intellectual fault line in Herder's philosophy of history is marked by Kant's hostile reviews of the first two parts of the *Ideen* in the *Allgemeine Literatur-Zeitung* (1785). These reviews did more than register a personal rupture between a former student and his teacher: they inaugurated a fundamental dispute over methodology, teleology, and the proper scope of philosophical inquiry into human history. Zammito's (2002) study traces the full arc of this confrontation, arguing that the Kant–Herder controversy was generative of the conceptual space in which both anthropology and biology emerged as modern disciplines. The dispute turned on irreconcilable differences over empirical inquiry, the concept of race, and what Zammito calls the clash between Herder's organismic and genetic approach to history and Kant's transcendental framework.

At the philosophical core of the controversy lay the question of teleology. Nassar (2015) provides a precise account of how the two thinkers deploy analogical reasoning differently in their accounts of organic nature and history. Herder uses analogy to establish a continuum between organic wholes and historical cultures, treating teleology as constitutive — built into the structure of natural and historical life. Kant insists, by contrast, that teleological judgments are merely regulative: they are heuristic tools for organizing inquiry, not claims about the objective structure of things. Kant's objection in the first *Ideen* review — documented in close philosophical detail by Zuckert (2014), whose analysis is noted here — is that Herder illegitimately hypostasizes what should remain a heuristic analogy between organisms and souls, thereby making constitutive metaphysical claims that exceed the bounds of legitimate cognition. Herder's philosophical formation under Kant in Königsberg (1762–64) gave him intimate acquaintance with the pre-critical philosophy; his subsequent departures from it were, on Beiser's (1987) reading, a principled philosophical response to the crisis generated by Kantian critique, situating Herder as a philosophically serious critic of transcendental philosophy whose naturalism anticipates later historicist and Romantic responses rather than simply representing a pre-critical holdover.

Gjesdal (2017) connects Herder's hermeneutical approach — attending to the particular, the historical, and the embodied — with his resistance to Enlightenment rationalism, showing that the methodological disagreement with Kant is not merely technical but reflects a deep divergence over what understanding human cultural life requires. For Herder, adequate understanding of history demands immersion in the particular rather than the application of transcendental categories; Kant's framework, with its a priori structures of cognition, is precisely what Herder's genetic and analogical method contests. The controversy over the *Ideen* thus marks the site where the master tension in Herder's philosophy — between particular worth and universal telos — becomes a technical philosophical dispute: Herder's constitutive teleology attempts to ground both the individuality of cultures and their directional development within nature itself, while Kant's regulative restriction of teleological judgment makes such a grounding illegitimate.

The Kant–Herder dispute is, in this sense, terminological as much as substantive on the question of Enlightenment affiliation, but metaphysically fundamental on the question of historical understanding. Whether Herder's naturalistic teleology constitutes a coherent philosophical alternative to Kantian critique or an unguarded speculation beyond permissible bounds remains unresolved — and the resolution bears directly on whether *Humanität* can function as anything more than a regulative idea in Herder's own terms.
