### C. Discourse Theory of Law, Democracy, and Deliberation

At the center of *Between Facts and Norms* stands Habermas's co-originality thesis: private and public autonomy are mutually presupposing, since citizens can regard themselves as authors of the laws that bind them as addressees only if a "system of rights" already secures the communicative liberties presupposed by legitimate opinion- and will-formation (Habermas 1996). Law thereby mediates the "facts" of coercive, positive enactment and the "norms" of rational acceptability, and Habermas proposes a proceduralist paradigm of law as an alternative to liberal and welfare-state models. The critical-exchange anthology edited by Rosenfeld and Arato (1998) gathers the first wave of Anglophone responses to this argument from pluralist, contractarian, systems-theoretic, and critical-theoretic standpoints, framing the terms within which subsequent debate has proceeded.

That subsequent debate has probed both halves of the co-originality pairing. Cooke (2020) mounts an immanent critique, arguing that Habermas's account of private autonomy is unsatisfactory and proposing a dialogical conception of autonomy as ethically self-determining agency to better secure the thesis he intends. Duke (2023) presses the public-autonomy side, contending that Habermas's reconstruction of popular sovereignty, by denying normative force to collective citizen action, amounts to a renunciation rather than a rehabilitation of the classical doctrine. Pedersen (2011) situates co-originality comparatively, showing that Rawls's political liberalism exhibits a structurally similar pairing of rights and popular sovereignty, and locating the real disagreement between the two thinkers in their differing conceptions of political legitimacy itself. Zurn (2002) extends the framework to constitutional review, arguing Habermas's account is superior to those of Ely, Perry, and Dworkin while needing revision toward a more participatory, procedural model. Shoikhedbrod (2020) reads Habermas's legality/revolution dichotomy against Marx and against Habermas's ongoing debate with Streeck over democracy and capitalism, while Hedrick (2018) raises a Frankfurt-internal objection: Habermas avoids Adorno's reification pessimism only by abstracting from psychoanalytic concerns, leaving the proceduralist paradigm too formal to support the substantive identification genuine legitimacy requires.

A distinct, sociological line of challenge concerns whether law can be normatively — rather than merely functionally — grounded at all. Kjaer (2021) argues that the Habermas-Luhmann debate, often treated as a 1970s episode, in fact continued until Luhmann's death and ended in convergence, with Habermas's discourse theory absorbed as a normative superstructure over Luhmann's descriptive systems theory; Jovanoski and Rustemi (2021) offer a more compact statement of the same underlying clash between law as autonomous, self-referential system and law as a bearer of normative validity claims. Baxter (2014), replying to his own critics, presses this scrutiny onto Habermas's concept of the lifeworld itself, asking whether a circulation-of-power model can be mapped onto the system/lifeworld framework at all. Ingram (2016) approaches the positivism question from another angle, arguing that Habermas and Kelsen both defend a "morally enlightened" proceduralism straddling positivism and natural law, against Schmittian decisionism.

Habermas's two-track model — informal public deliberation feeding formal, legally binding will-formation — is also the operational theory tested by the wider deliberative-democracy field. Cohen (1989) offers an independently developed proceduralist account of deliberative legitimacy, while Dryzek (2002) proposes a pluralist "discursive democracy" that de-centers formal consensus in favor of contestation across civil society. Chambers (2003) maps how these positions, alongside Gutmann and Thompson's (2004) reciprocity-based model, converge on publicity and accountability while diverging over consensus versus reasonable disagreement, and how the field's "systemic turn" (Mansbridge et al. 2012) redistributes deliberative quality across an entire system rather than any single forum. Empirical work asks whether ordinary deliberation can approximate these assumptions: Fishkin (2018) reports on Deliberative Polling across more than two dozen countries, and Smith and Setälä (2018) assess evidence on mini-publics' design and their possible role in decision-making. Lafont (2019) counters that such institutional "shortcuts," including empowered mini-publics, require citizens to defer blindly to decisions they cannot control, undermining rather than realizing democratic self-government.

The Habermas-Rawls exchange sharpens what is distinctive in the two-track model: Habermas (1995) argues Rawls's original position smuggles substantive moral commitments into a purportedly procedural device, while Finlayson (2018) shows that assimilating Habermas's "institutional translation proviso" to Rawls's proviso is a common but mistaken move that wrongly transfers objections from one theorist to the other. Finally, the agonistic challenge presses the model's consensus orientation directly: Mouffe (2000) argues both Rawlsian and Habermasian deliberative democracy rest on an untenable rationalist assumption that occludes passion and antagonism, and Russell and Montin (2015) reconstruct Rancière's rival "dissensus" model as a genuinely distinct departure from communicative rationality rather than a variant of Mouffe's position. These challenges meet active internal rejoinders rather than standing as settled defeats: Brady (2004) argues discourse theory already accommodates contestation as constitutive of the public sphere, and Erman (2009) contends discourse theory in fact theorizes conflict more accurately than agonism does, since it treats deliberation as constitutive of, not opposed to, conflict. Kaltsas (2019) extends this consensus/agonism question into the post-secular public sphere, defending a Habermasian reading against Taylor's and Connolly's criticisms while conceding the need to incorporate an agonistic dimension.
