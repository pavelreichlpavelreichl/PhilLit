## Conclusion

Read across six decades, Habermas's political philosophy pursues a single wager: that legitimacy — of speech acts, publics, law, democratic will-formation, political identity, and ultimately global order — is traceable to communicative rather than strategic reason. *Between Facts and Norms* (Habermas 1996) stands as the pivotal text in which this wager is cashed out institutionally, translating the discourse principle first elaborated for language and morality into the co-originality of private and public autonomy and the proceduralist paradigm of law. Susen (2017) traces the same thread from language theory through democratic theory, treating Habermas's apparent turns — from communicative action to discourse ethics, from the public sphere to discourse theory of law, from constitutional patriotism to cosmopolitanism — not as ruptures but as successive institutional tests of one commitment. The review has followed this arc deliberately, organizing itself around Habermas's own developmental stages rather than around a topical or purely critical taxonomy.

What this organization makes visible is that the secondary literature's objections, though addressed to different institutional sites, recur in structurally similar form. The idealization charge appears first as a motivational and epistemic worry about discourse ethics (Section A), then as a historical and feminist argument that the bourgeois public sphere's claim to status-blindness was itself exclusionary (Section B), then as a sociological and agonistic challenge to whether proceduralist law and consensus-oriented deliberation can accommodate systemic complexity and ineradicable antagonism (Section C), then as a Eurocentrism charge against the philosophy of history underwriting constitutional patriotism and cosmopolitan law (Section D), and finally as a foundational challenge asking whether communicative rationality itself, rather than any particular application of it, builds power and exclusion into its starting premises (Section E). Forst's (2007) argument that relations of justification are already relations of power, and Allen's (2016) charge that Habermas, Honneth, and Forst alike remain committed to a Eurocentric normative philosophy of progress, both target this deepest layer — suggesting that the idealization/exclusion/power complex identified piecemeal across Sections A through D is not incidental to particular applications but a recurring feature of the discourse-theoretic project as such.

Several tensions remain unresolved rather than adjudicated by this literature: whether the lifeworld/system distinction survives Habermas's own legal turn; whether consensus and agonism are as opposed as the Mouffe-Habermas exchange suggests once internal rebuttals (Brady, Erman) are weighed; whether constitutional patriotism can generalize beyond its West German origins given that its principal theorist, Müller, could not be verified for direct citation here; and whether Honneth's and Forst's revisions supersede discourse theory or merely supplement it, as Stahl's immanent-critique reading suggests. Organizing this review by developmental stage, with critique integrated at each stage rather than confined to a closing chapter, has aimed to represent both the coherence of Habermas's project and the genuine plurality of its reception more adequately than either a purely expository or a purely critical treatment would.
