### D. Political Identity and Its Global Extension: Constitutional Patriotism and Cosmopolitanism

Constitutional patriotism responds to a tension Habermas locates within the nation-state form itself: its historical success rested on fusing a republican, legal-constitutional order of citizenship with a "nation" understood as a pre-political community of shared ethnicity, language, or culture (Habermas 2018). Multicultural differentiation and globalization, he argues, expose the limits of this fusion, motivating solidarity grounded in shared constitutional norms rather than ethnic-national culture. Jan-Werner Müller's work is widely treated in this literature as its reference point, tracing the concept's origins and defending it against standard objections; because a dedicated re-verification pass confirmed that Müller's texts (along with Cronin's and Pensky's defenses and the Rosenfeld/Hayward Constellations symposium pieces) have no retrievable abstract in any accessible source, his position is presented here only as reported within the papers that engage him directly, not as a directly cited claim.

Three such engagements structure the debate. Markell (2000) examines the post-1989 revival of the civic/ethnic nationalism distinction, questioning whether "civic" attachment can actually escape the affective dynamics of belonging it claims to purify, rather than merely relocating them. Michelman (2001) presses a related worry: since no modern plural society achieves settled agreement on a constitution's concrete content, Habermasian and Rawlsian justification ultimately depends on a "consciously shared sentiment" that the polity credibly pursues its constitutional ideal — and he interrogates what this demands of citizens' moral identity. Against this, Ingram (1996) offers a justice-based defense compatible with both Habermasian and Rawlsian premises, arguing that a shared commitment to justice can itself suffice for political unity without a nation-based idea of membership, extending the account to European integration.

Lacroix's (2009) "Habermas vs. Habermas" argument supplies the hinge to the cosmopolitan material: she finds a discrepancy between Habermas's early plea for constitutional patriotism as a critical, reflexive standard and his later European writings, which drift toward a thicker, quasi-superior European identity contrasted with the United States — a "Europatriotic" turn lacking genuine federative dimension.

At the postnational scale, Habermas (2012) reframes the Eurozone crisis as a symptom of incomplete constitutionalization of international law, arguing that "executive federalism" abandons the EU's democratizing trajectory; Lubenow (2016) and Patberg (2014) elaborate this institutionally, reconstructing the EU's technocratic democratic deficit and subjecting Habermas's "rational reconstruction" of a dual EU/member-state constituent power to methodological scrutiny. Lupel (2005) situates this "democratic regionalism" route comparatively, treating Habermas's postnational constellation as one of three structurally analogous attempts — alongside Held's cosmopolitan democracy and network-based global civil society — to broaden democratic legitimacy beyond the nation-state, and finds each only partially successful at reconciling particular contexts of legitimacy with transnational universalism. Critical responses converge from several directions. Scheuerman (2009) argues that robust democracy beyond the nation-state requires postnational statehood, not merely postnational law. Roele (2014) contends that Habermas's cosmopolitics is caught in "vicious circles" generated by his commitment to intersubjectivity, which he ultimately abandons for a prediscursive concept of human dignity. Streeck (2014) argues that a currency union without fiscal and political union structurally entrenches German economic dominance rather than transnational solidarity. Trejo-Mathys (2012) mounts an internal discourse-theoretic critique, holding that Habermas's own framework remains the most promising resource for theorizing supranational authority but has not adequately worked out its implications. Cohen (2008) constructs an alternative "political" conception of human rights and sovereignty against Habermas's constitutionalization-of-international-law approach. Fine (2003, with Smith), and independently Fine (2015) on European antisemitism, identify unexamined cracks in Habermas's cosmopolitan self-image — dilemmas reconciling cosmopolitanism with the national anchoring of constitutional patriotism, and a residual chauvinism risking the premature judgment that antisemitism has been solved in Europe.

The postcolonial cluster presses these cracks further. Bailey's (2014) edited volume anchors the "provincialism" charge across contributors in political theory, global justice, and postcolonial studies. Allen (2016) argues that Habermas, Honneth, and Forst all remain committed to a Eurocentric philosophy of progress their normative frameworks cannot justify without external validation. Rees (2017) tests this charge more sympathetically against Habermas's Axial Age thesis, asking how far his comparative-historical framework can accommodate non-Western traditions on their own terms. Kempf (2024) stages a direct Habermas-Fanon dialogue, conceding the progress-narrative critique while arguing that Habermas's concept of "systematically distorted communication" can be repurposed to analyze colonial rule as communicatively mediated domination.
