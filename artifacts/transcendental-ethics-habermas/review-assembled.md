## Introduction

The problem of ethical foundations in the post-metaphysical era has a distinctive shape. After the linguistic turn, neither appeal to a transcendent moral order nor grounding in a monological transcendental subject seemed philosophically viable. Yet the project of moral philosophy — establishing norms that are universally valid rather than merely locally endorsed — had not thereby become unnecessary. Jürgen Habermas and Karl-Otto Apel represent the most sustained attempt within the German critical theory tradition to honor both sides of this constraint: no metaphysical grounding, but genuine normative universality. This double commitment — rejecting the foundations that Kant's successors found indispensable while insisting on the universality Kant sought to secure — generates the pressure that drives the debate this review examines.

Both thinkers converge on a shared research program: discourse ethics. The core claim of this program is that moral validity is constituted by, or at minimum disclosed through, the pragmatic presuppositions of rational argumentation and communication. To engage in argumentation at all is already to presuppose certain norms — of equality, reciprocity, and truthfulness — that cannot be denied without performative self-contradiction. The universalization principle that Habermas articulates in his canonical statement of discourse ethics holds that a moral norm is valid only if all affected parties could agree to it under conditions of ideal discourse (Habermas 1990). Apel's program reaches the same destination: the ideal communication community — an unbounded, counterfactual community of rational arguers — functions as both the epistemic telos of rational inquiry and the normative criterion of discourse ethics (Apel 2023). On this foundational architecture, the two thinkers agree.

Where they sharply diverge is on the justificatory status of this shared result. Apel holds that transcendental-pragmatic reflection on the presuppositions of argumentation can achieve what he calls Letztbegründung — ultimate justification — for the norms of discourse ethics. The argument is reflexive: anyone who sincerely argues already presupposes the relevant norms; to deny them within argumentation is to commit a performative self-contradiction. Because the argument establishes its own presuppositions as inescapable, it escapes the standard philosophical regress of justifications and achieves a non-hypothetical foundational status (Apel 2023). Habermas, by contrast, holds that the very same pragmatic-transcendental dimension of language that Apel identifies — the achievement he credits as Apel's most important philosophical contribution — yields only fallible, quasi-transcendental reconstruction, not apodeictic foundations. Transcendental reflection on argumentation presuppositions is itself a form of fallible inquiry, not an a priori deduction; the norms it reconstructs carry epistemic weight but not the immunity to revision that Letztbegründung requires (Habermas 2020). As Mendieta (2002) documents in his systematic reconstruction of Apel's intellectual development, this methodological fork between "transcendental" and "universal" pragmatics is not merely a difference of degree but reflects divergent conceptions of what philosophical justification can accomplish.

The stakes of this fork are significant. Without ultimate foundations, as Apel would have it, discourse ethics risks losing the normative force that distinguishes it from mere sociological description of communicative practices. If the norms disclosed by pragmatic reflection are fallible and revisable, what prevents the moral skeptic from simply refusing to participate in the relevant kind of argumentation? With Letztbegründung, conversely, discourse ethics faces the challenge that has attended transcendental arguments since Barry Stroud's formulation of the substitution objection: any argument purporting to establish how things are on the basis of what we must believe or presuppose can, in principle, be reformulated as a weaker claim about what we must believe — not what genuinely binds (Stroud 1968). Applied to the practical domain, this challenge asks whether Apel's reflexive argument establishes that the norms of discourse are genuinely binding, or only that arguers must behave as if they are. The contest between Apel's stronger and Habermas's weaker variant is thus an instance of a structural problem in post-Kantian moral philosophy: whether any inference from the necessary conditions of rational practice to binding normative content can be cogent without collapsing into circularity or into underreach.

This review examines that problem through a systematic engagement with the relevant philosophical literature. It proceeds in six movements. The first establishes the conceptual tools by engaging the analytic literature on transcendental arguments — particularly the ambitious/modest distinction and the specific challenges facing retorsive arguments in practical philosophy. The second reconstructs Apel's transcendental-pragmatic program on its own terms, including the Letztbegründung argument and the dual Part A/Part B architecture. The third does the same for Habermas's quasi-transcendental alternative and the internal instabilities critics have identified within it. The fourth reconstructs the direct debate between them across three axes of disagreement. The fifth surveys the principal external and internal objections both programs face. The sixth assesses the contemporary state of the field, including the most productive recent deployments of quasi-transcendental justification. The review is primarily philosophical — it addresses the justificatory structure of both programs, not their sociological or political applications.

A note on terminology is warranted at the outset. The label "quasi-transcendental" is Habermas's own preferred description for his approach in certain formulations, but the secondary literature employs a range of near-synonyms: "post-transcendental," "reconstructive," "weak transcendental," and "post-metaphysical" appear in different authors as characterizations of the same methodological stance. The terminological variation is not merely cosmetic — different labels carry different philosophical emphases — and this review will track them where they matter. "Quasi-transcendental" will serve as the working term throughout, denoting an approach that retains the transcendental question (what conditions make rational communication possible?) while treating the answer as fallible reconstruction rather than a priori deduction.

## Section 1: Transcendental Arguments: Ambition, Modesty, and the Practical Turn

The programs of Karl-Otto Apel and Jürgen Habermas both make claims whose philosophical force depends on the cogency of transcendental reasoning. Before either program can be assessed on its own terms, the general conceptual framework for evaluating such reasoning must be established. The analytic literature on transcendental arguments supplies that framework, and its central conclusions impose a structural constraint on any transcendental or quasi-transcendental justification in ethics: a dilemma between ambitious arguments that face a decisive objection and modest arguments whose conclusions may be too narrow to do the required normative work. This section traces that dilemma through three steps — the foundational Stroud-Stern debate, the specific application to practical philosophy, and the direct bridging of analytic argument theory to Habermas.

### 1.1 The Stroud Challenge and the Ambitious/Modest Distinction

The standard account of transcendental arguments holds that they establish a claim X by showing that X is a necessary condition for the possibility of some undeniable Y — paradigmatically, the possibility of coherent experience, thought, or practice. Barry Stroud's 1968 paper submitted this structure to a challenge that has defined subsequent debate. Stroud argues that any transcendental argument purporting to establish a claim about how the world is can be blocked by the skeptic: all the argument actually establishes is that we must think or represent the world as containing X, not that it actually does (Stroud 1968). This "substitution objection" — replacing "X is necessary" with "belief in X is necessary" — yields an equally valid but weaker conclusion that refutes no skeptic about external reality. On this diagnosis, "ambitious" transcendental arguments, those that target the world directly and seek to establish how things are, are systematically vulnerable to substitution.

Robert Stern's work provides the systematic taxonomy that clarifies the stakes of Stroud's challenge. In his 2000 monograph and the subsequent "Plea for Modesty," Stern distinguishes ambitious arguments (targeting external-world skeptics, claiming to show how things are) from modest ones (targeting coherence skeptics who accept experience but doubt the internal consistency of their own conceptual scheme). Modest arguments, Stern argues, are not made redundant by perceptual evidence in the way ambitious arguments are, since they address a different skeptical target: the agent who accepts the existence of experience but worries whether their own conceptual commitments hang together (Stern 2000; Stern 2007). On this account, modest arguments survive Stroud's objection but only because they do not aim at what Stroud attacks. The cost is a narrower scope: modest arguments establish something about the internal coherence of conceptual schemes, not about the binding character of norms that transcend any particular scheme.

The significance of this distinction for post-metaphysical moral philosophy is sharpened by Quassim Cassam's examination of the relationship between transcendental arguments and the idealist commitments they may require. Cassam argues that Strawson's attempt to conduct transcendental arguments in a naturalistic spirit — stripped of Kantian transcendental idealism — is unsuccessful: the "objectivity argument" in *The Bounds of Sense* depends on idealist commitments about the conditions of experience that cannot simply be removed without losing the argument's force (Cassam 1987). Cassam's point raises an immediate pressure for Habermas's project: if transcendental arguments depend for their force on idealist commitments, then a genuinely post-metaphysical reconstruction that rejects idealism must either forgo transcendental argument structure or provide an account of what substitutes for the idealist underpinning. Habermas's "detranscendentalization" — situating communicative reason within historically embedded social practices — can be read as precisely such a substitute, but whether it succeeds is one of the review's central questions.

### 1.2 Retorsive Arguments and the Practical Case

The extension of transcendental argument theory to practical philosophy raises a further question: does ethics offer especially favorable terrain for the ambitious transcendental strategy? One strand of optimism here draws on the idea that the performative conditions of rational agency or moral discourse are more immediately available to reflection than the conditions of theoretical experience, and that the self-referential structure of argumentation provides a distinctive lever unavailable in theoretical philosophy. "Retorsive" arguments exploit precisely this lever: they purport to convict the moral skeptic of performative self-contradiction, showing that denying a given norm within the context of argumentation already presupposes it. This is Apel's core strategy, and it appears at least implicitly in Habermas's derivation of the universalization principle.

Stern's verdict on this strategy is largely pessimistic. In "Silencing the Sceptic?" he examines whether retorsive arguments in practical philosophy can succeed where straightforward deductive ones fail (Stern 2017). His conclusion is negative on both horns of a dilemma: either the appeal to performative self-contradiction adds nothing to what a standard deductive argument would already establish — in which case the performative element is redundant — or the argument merely hardens the skeptic's resolve by demonstrating that the skeptical position requires rejecting a presupposition the skeptic had not previously noticed they were making. In neither case does the retorsive structure achieve the ambitious goal of silencing the skeptic rather than cataloguing an inconsistency they may simply accept. Stern also tests the suggestion that anti-realism in ethics might make ambitious transcendental arguments more tractable in the practical domain than in theoretical philosophy — concluding that this route offers limited help.

James Skidmore's analysis of practical reason reinforces Stern's verdict from a different angle. Examining transcendental arguments from rational agency developed by Kant and Christine Korsgaard, Skidmore finds that each faces a version of Stroud's substitution objection adapted to the practical case: at best, the arguments show that rational agents must represent themselves as bound by certain normative principles, not that those principles genuinely bind them (Skidmore 2002). The parallel with Stroud's original objection is precise: establishing the inescapability of a self-representation leaves open the skeptic's question whether the self-representation is accurate.

The edited volume *Transcendental Arguments in Moral Theory* (Brune, Stern, and Werner 2017) represents the most recent systematic attempt to assess where this leaves practical philosophy. The volume does not overturn Stern's pessimism but registers a more nuanced picture: some contributors argue that retorsive arguments may survive in more limited form — establishing something about the coherence conditions of moral practice, even if not about the binding character of moral norms as such. Whether this limited survival is enough to support discourse ethics depends on what discourse ethics actually requires from its justificatory foundation, a question that runs through the remainder of this review.

### 1.3 Power on Transcendental Arguments and Habermas

The translation of this analytic framework into an assessment of Habermas's specific justificatory strategy was carried out most directly by Michael Power in a 1993 paper that functions as the key bridge between the Stroud-Stern debate and the discourse ethics literature. Power argues that prevailing conceptions of strong transcendental arguments — imported from analytic epistemology and applied to Habermas's early work in *Knowledge and Human Interests* — fundamentally misread Habermas's argumentative structure (Power 1993). The analytic critics who charge Habermas with attempting an ambitious transcendental argument and failing have imposed a standard that does not fit his actual procedure. Power contends that Habermas's approach is better understood as a quasi-transcendental or reconstructive strategy: it does not advance necessity claims of the kind that make traditional transcendental arguments vulnerable to substitution, but instead operates through what Power characterizes as a deeply hermeneutic reconstruction of the normative presuppositions embedded in human cognitive interests. On this reading, the "as if" character of Kantian regulative ideas is closer to what Habermas deploys than the strict transcendental necessity that Stroud targets.

Power's reappraisal anticipates the central distinction between Apel and Habermas: if Habermas is already running a modest, reconstructive strategy rather than an ambitious necessity claim, then he is less exposed to Stroud's objection than Apel, whose Letztbegründung program explicitly claims to establish the non-hypothetical standing of discourse norms. The structural asymmetry Power identifies — Habermas's reconstructive humility versus Apel's foundationalist ambition — maps onto the ambitious/modest distinction, and sets the terms for the entire Apel-Habermas debate.

Johnston (2016) reaches a complementary diagnosis from within the discourse ethics literature itself. Johnston argues that Habermas's "weak" transcendental approach is in fact unstable because it cannot coherently reconcile two conflicting philosophical inheritances: Kantian transcendental idealism and Meadian social pragmatism. The Kantian element demands a form of a priori normative grounding that the pragmatist element refuses to supply; the pragmatist element provides the social and reconstructive basis that the Kantian element cannot accommodate without idealist commitments. Johnston concludes that some form of quasi-transcendental argument remains indispensable for discourse ethics — Habermas's minimal reconstruction does not go far enough — but that Apel's Letztbegründung overreaches what the performative-contradiction strategy can establish.

Taken together, Power and Johnston locate Habermas in a position that is philosophically awkward in a precise way: a strategy modest enough to avoid Stroud's substitution objection may not be strong enough to ground the universalization principle with the normative force discourse ethics requires. This structural dilemma — ambitious arguments face Stroud; modest arguments face scope limitations; retorsive arguments face Stern's pessimism — is the framework that the remainder of this review will press against both programs. Apel's program, in aspiring to Letztbegründung, accepts the full force of the ambitious horn; Habermas's quasi-transcendentalism attempts to occupy the modest horn while retaining claims that exceed what modesty licenses. Whether either position can be stabilized is a question the existing literature has not resolved.

## Section 2: Apel's Transcendental-Pragmatic Program: Structure, Strength, and Internal Pressures

### 2.1 From Peirce and Heidegger to Transcendental Pragmatics

Karl-Otto Apel's transcendental-pragmatic program begins with a diagnosis: Kantian transcendental philosophy failed because it located the a priori conditions of knowledge in an isolated, monological consciousness. The remedy Apel proposes is a thoroughgoing transformation of this framework — not its abandonment, but its reconstruction through the resources of the "linguistic turn." As Habermas (2020) reconstructs the trajectory in his retrospective essay, Apel's path runs from the late Heidegger's existential hermeneutics, which showed that understanding is always already linguistically mediated, through Wittgenstein's dissolution of the private-language illusion, to Peirce's semiotic theory of inquiry. Each stage displaces the individual knower from the center and installs in its place a community of interpreters whose shared practices constitute the medium of meaning and validity.

The Peircean inheritance is philosophically decisive. Apel's (1980) treatment of the post-Tarskian problem of truth draws on Peirce's consensus theory while recasting it in explicitly transcendental terms. Truth, on this account, is not a property of correspondence between propositions and states of affairs but a regulative idea — the counterfactual limit of an infinite process of rational inquiry conducted within an unbounded community of communicators. This ideal unlimited communication community (*unbegrenzte Kommunikationsgemeinschaft*) is not an empirical fact about actual discourse communities; it is a condition of the possibility of meaningful assertoric practice. Mendieta's (2002) monograph, which provides the most sustained English-language reconstruction of Apel's intellectual development, situates this move at the center of the programmatic essays collected in *Towards a Transformation of Philosophy* (Apel 2023): the transcendental subject is not eliminated but socialized, its constitutive functions relocated from individual consciousness to intersubjective communication.

The consequences for ethics are immediate. If the a priori of the communication community grounds both knowledge and the validity of claims, then the norms presupposed in the practice of argumentation — norms of equality, truthfulness, and reciprocity among participants — carry transcendental status. They are not merely contingent features of how particular cultures happen to argue; they are conditions of the possibility of argumentation as such. This is Apel's departure from both empirical pragmatics and Kantian moral theory: unlike Kant, the ground of the moral law is not practical reason abstractly conceived, but the intersubjective structure of argumentative practice; unlike empirical pragmatics, these presuppositions are known through transcendental reflection, not inductive generalization. The ideal communication community thus functions simultaneously as a regulative epistemic idea — the telos toward which rational inquiry tends — and as the normative criterion against which actual discourses can be evaluated. Whether these two functions can be held together without tension is a question the program's internal critics press (Vandevelde 2010).

### 2.2 The Letztbegründung Argument: Structure and Claims

The philosophical core of Apel's program is the claim that transcendental-pragmatic reflection can achieve *Letztbegründung* — ultimate or final justification — of the fundamental norms of discourse ethics. The argument's structure, rigorously reconstructed by Kettner (2017), runs as follows. Argumentative discourse is a practice of reason-sensitive agents constituted by a normatively specific aim: fixing the true values of our reasons, that is, determining which claims and inferences are genuinely rationally warranted. This constitutive aim is not an external purpose imposed on discourse but internal to what counts as arguing rather than merely making noise or issuing commands. Performative self-contradictions — speech acts whose propositional content denies what their illocutionary performance presupposes — are therefore not merely socially awkward but pragmatically and logically incompatible with discourse's constitutive aim. Kettner concludes that competent discourse participants can know a priori that they would be performatively inconsistent if they violated a "moral kernel" intrinsic to discourse: the mutual recognition of participants as equal, symmetrically positioned, truth-seeking contributors to the collective inquiry.

The anti-skeptical thrust of this argument is essential to Apel's project. Unlike a deductive moral theory that moves from contested premises to normative conclusions, the performative contradiction argument is designed to be self-sealing: any attempt to dispute the presuppositions of argumentation within argumentation already employs those presuppositions, thereby vindicating them. This reflexive structure is what distinguishes Letztbegründung from both dogmatic axiomatics (which simply assert foundational premises) and hypothetical reasoning (which remains open to revision in light of new evidence). The norms grounding discourse ethics are, on this account, not established by finding some further, more basic justification for them, but by showing that their denial is self-undermining for anyone who engages in the practice of reason-giving at all.

The most significant internal challenge to this claim is posed by Keuth's (1983) fallibilist objection. Keuth argues that the argument, even if formally valid, establishes only the *de facto* inescapability of certain presuppositions for participants currently engaged in argumentation — not their apodeictic justificatory status. The presuppositions may be impossible to coherently deny within argumentative practice, but this shows only that arguers must behave *as if* they hold; it does not establish that the presuppositions constitute genuine normative bindingness transcending any particular practice. This is precisely the substitution that Stroud's objection predicts: where Apel claims transcendental necessity, Keuth finds only an empirically robust, but in principle revisable, pragmatic compulsion.

Kuhlmann's (1985) response insists that Keuth misidentifies the structure of reflexive justification. The self-referential character of the performative contradiction argument gives it a non-hypothetical status that marks it off from any ordinary empirical claim. To treat the presuppositions of argumentation as merely contingent features of current practice, Kuhlmann argues, one must already engage in argumentation and thereby already employ the norms in question — the very act of posing the fallibilist challenge reinstates what it purports to challenge. Whether this response succeeds depends on a contested question: does the inescapability of a presupposition within a practice constitute anything stronger than what Stroud's substitution allows? The Keuth-Kuhlmann exchange functions as the internal version of the Stroud problem, and it remains unresolved in the literature. Habermas's (2020) retrospective judgment is that the debate over Letztbegründung "unjustly overshadowed" Apel's more fundamental contribution to the philosophy of language — a formulation that implicitly concedes the intractability of the foundationalist dispute without resolving it.

### 2.3 Part B and the Application Problem

Even if the performative contradiction argument succeeds in establishing Part A — the transcendental-pragmatic grounding of discourse ethics' fundamental norms — a structural pressure remains. The norms established through a priori reflection on ideal discourse are presuppositions of an unlimited, counterfactual communication community. Actual moral agents, however, must act under conditions that depart systematically from this ideal: asymmetries of power, incomplete information, time pressure, the absence of genuine consensus, and the ever-present possibility that ideal discourse is not achievable in the historically given situation. Part B of Apel's program addresses this gap. The concept of "strategies of responsible action" (*Mitverantwortung*, co-responsibility) attempts to bridge ideal foundations and non-ideal conditions by specifying how agents ought to act when they cannot realize ideal discourse but remain committed to its presuppositions as a regulative goal.

Apel's (1993) application of this framework to the challenges of global modernity — ecological crisis, nuclear risk, economic interdependence — illustrates the ambition of Part B: the same transcendental-pragmatic foundation that yields the abstract norms of symmetry and equality in Part A is supposed to generate actionable prescriptions at the macro-ethical level. His (2007) extension to international law and democracy argues that the ideal communication community functions as a regulative idea for global institutional design, guiding the construction of international legal frameworks and deliberative institutions even when genuinely universal consensus remains out of reach.

The difficulty is that the bridge between Part A and Part B remains architecturally underspecified. As documented in Apel's *Diskurs und Verantwortung* (1988) — reconstructable from secondary accounts in Mendieta (2002) and Vandevelde (2010) — the ethics of co-responsibility requires its own normative resources for specifying which strategies count as genuinely oriented toward the ideal and which merely rationalize departures from it. This determination cannot be derived from the transcendental reflection constitutive of Part A alone; it requires substantive judgment about empirical circumstances and probable consequences. The gap between the formal content of the ideal communication community and the material content needed for concrete moral guidance therefore persists as a structural feature of the program, not merely a problem of application awaiting technical solution.

This tension is what Habermas later identifies as the untenable character of the Part A/Part B distinction: not that application is impossible in principle, but that the strict separation between transcendental foundations and empirical-strategic reasoning cannot be maintained once one acknowledges that the content of the norms themselves is shaped by the historically situated understanding of their application. Whether this is a fatal objection to Apel's architecture or a manageable tension within a broadly transcendental-pragmatic framework is a question that the debate reconstructed in Section 4 will need to assess. What the present section establishes is that the Part B underdevelopment is not an accidental limitation but a structural consequence of the program's central commitment: the higher the ambition of Part A's foundationalism, the wider the gap that Part B must bridge through resources the transcendental argument alone cannot supply.

## Section 3: Habermas's Quasi-Transcendental Alternative: Post-Metaphysical Reconstruction

Where Apel's program aspires to Letztbegründung — the non-hypothetical grounding of ethical norms through transcendental-pragmatic reflection — Habermas pursues a deliberately more modest strategy. His "quasi-transcendental" approach reconstructs the normative presuppositions embedded in communicative practice without claiming to establish those presuppositions through apodeictic reasoning. This is not simply a weakened version of Apel's program but a principled methodological reorientation: the quasi-transcendental stance rejects first philosophy as a viable ambition and relocates moral justification within what Habermas calls "reconstructive science." Understanding what this means — and why it may be internally unstable — is essential for evaluating whether the post-metaphysical move constitutes a genuine philosophical alternative or an evasion that inherits Apelian commitments without Apelian rigor.

### 3.1 Post-Metaphysical Thinking and Universal Pragmatics

Habermas's post-metaphysical philosophy rests on a negative thesis and a positive one. The negative thesis is that philosophy can no longer claim foundational certainty: "strong" accounts of ontological necessity, philosophy of the subject, and first-person certainty have all been closed off by the linguistic turn. In *Postmetaphysical Thinking* (1992), Habermas reconstructs what this closure requires: philosophy must proceed without metaphysical foundations yet retain its capacity to make universal normative claims. The positive thesis is that communicative rationality, rather than transcendental subjectivity, provides the intersubjective medium through which such claims can be articulated and redeemed. This move from consciousness philosophy to the pragmatics of language is what Habermas designates "detranscendentalization": reason is always-already embedded in social practices and historical lifeforms, not housed in an a priori subject.

The instrument through which this post-metaphysical stance generates normative content is universal pragmatics. Every speech act, Habermas argues in *Moral Consciousness and Communicative Action* (1990), raises unavoidable validity claims: claims to truth (that the propositional content corresponds to facts), rightness (that the utterance conforms to normatively recognized expectations), and sincerity (that the speaker's expressed intentions are genuine). These claims are not contingent features of particular speech communities but belong to the formal structure of communicative action as such. When participants in argumentation dispute whether a norm is valid, they unavoidably presuppose conditions — symmetry, reciprocity, openness to the force of the better argument — that encode normative constraints. Moral norms, on this account, are valid when all affected parties could agree to them under conditions of ideal discourse.

The designation "quasi-transcendental" marks a precise location between two alternatives Habermas wants to refuse. It is not transcendental in Apel's sense — no apodeictic claim is made that the presuppositions of argumentation are necessary conditions accessible through pure philosophical reflection. Nor is it merely empirical — the analysis of speech act presuppositions is not a sociological survey of what particular discourse communities happen to presuppose. In his retrospective essay "From Formal Semantics to Transcendental Pragmatics" (2020), Habermas makes this explicit: he accepts Apel's original insight that the pragmatic dimension of language use discloses a normatively relevant structure, but denies that reflection on this structure can achieve the apodictic status Apel's Letztbegründung requires. The reconstruction remains fallible, revisable in light of further inquiry, and embedded in the ongoing practice it reconstructs. This is what Habermas means by situating communicative reason in social practices rather than in a transcendental tribunal.

The philosophical stakes of this positioning are significant. A quasi-transcendental claim is not merely weaker than a transcendental one — it has a different epistemic character. Where Apel's transcendental-pragmatic reflection purports to identify conditions that cannot be coherently denied by anyone engaged in argumentation, Habermas's reconstructive approach tracks conditions that discourse participants in fact presuppose, with the status of those presuppositions remaining open to revision. This difference will become the crux of the debate with Apel examined in Section 4: Apel argues that fallibilist reconstruction abandons the only thing that could make moral norms genuinely binding; Habermas argues that the aspiration to apodictic grounding is both unachievable and unnecessary.

### 3.2 Reconstructive Science and the Grounding of (U)

The universalization principle (U) — the claim that valid norms are those to which all affected parties could agree under ideal discourse conditions — is the normative core of discourse ethics. In the 1983 programmatic essay collected in *Moral Consciousness and Communicative Action*, Habermas proposes that (U) follows by material implication from two premises: what it means to discuss whether a norm ought to be adopted, and what arguers must suppose about any consensus they reach if they are to count it as rationally motivated. This derivation is quasi-transcendental in structure: it does not proceed by first-philosophical deduction but by reconstructing the normative commitments implicit in what it means to engage in moral argumentation at all.

Rehg (1991) undertakes a systematic reconstruction of this derivation, identifying a significant gap: no satisfactory argument from the two Habermasian premises to (U) had been presented without either begging the question or smuggling in additional premises. Rehg's response is to supplement the derivation with dialogical conditions — specifically, a perspective-taking requirement that discourse participants consider the interests of all affected others. This dialogical strengthening attempts to ground (U) in the intersubjective structure of moral discourse itself rather than in an independent normative commitment. The result is a derivation that remains within the quasi-transcendental register: it appeals to conditions that arguers must presuppose, not to an independent moral standpoint, but it does so through a richer account of dialogical reciprocity than Habermas's original presentation.

Finlayson (2000) provides a more architecturally focused reconstruction of the original derivation strategy, and in doing so reveals what is at stake in its success or failure. Discourse ethics was, on Finlayson's account, originally conceived as a program of philosophical justification of morality: (U) is formally derived from non-moral principles — the pragmatic presuppositions of argumentative discourse — so that the moral theory emerges as a fallout of the general theory of communicative rationality rather than being smuggled in through prior moral commitments. The derivation's force depends on the connection between the speech act analysis and the normative conclusions: if that connection is genuine, the justification is quasi-transcendental; if it breaks down, the program either collapses into circularity or loses its claim to universal validity.

Habermas's own engagement with the Apel debate is most explicit in *Justification and Application* (1993), where he defends the sufficiency of fallibilist reconstruction against the charge that abandoning ultimate foundations leaves normativity without adequate grounding. His argument, reconstructed from Finlayson's and Rehg's analyses, is that the conditions of the possibility of reaching a rationally motivated consensus are sufficiently determinate to generate normative constraints without requiring the apodictic status of Apelian transcendental reflection. The discourse principle (D) — that only those norms are valid which could gain assent of all participants in rational discourse — is grounded not a priori but in the reconstructed presuppositions of argumentation as a practice. Whether this reconstruction has quasi-transcendental necessity or merely empirical generality is precisely what remains contested.

### 3.3 Internal Instabilities: The Heath-Finlayson Challenge

The most diagnostically acute pressures on Habermas's program come not from external critics but from those who work within its terms. Three internal challenges are particularly significant: Heath's argument that Habermas's later revisions undercut the original quasi-transcendental derivation; Finlayson's diagnosis of the "modernity/morality imbrication"; and Heller's earliest formulation of the formalism objection.

Heath (2014) identifies a structural discontinuity between the 1983 program and Habermas's subsequent revisions. The early program, organized around the desire to provide a quasi-transcendental defense of (U), grounded the universalization principle in the analysis of speech acts — specifically, in the rightness validity claim that is unavoidably raised in moral discourse. The later work, particularly the developments associated with *Between Facts and Norms* and *Justification and Application*, severs the conception of practical discourse from the analysis of speech acts. On Heath's reconstruction, this revision was motivated by legitimate philosophical pressures — Habermas wanted to distinguish sharply between moral norms, ethical-existential questions, and pragmatic questions — but its effect was to remove the basis of the original quasi-transcendental argument. Without the connection to the speech act analysis, (U) can no longer claim the quasi-transcendental necessity it was designed to have; it becomes either a contingent feature of Western modernity's moral self-understanding or a normative commitment that must be imported from elsewhere. Heath's proposed "reboot" attempts to resolve this by reverting to a more Durkheimian foundation in the pragmatics of language-dependence and cooperative interaction, but at the explicit cost of abandoning the quasi-transcendental status that the original program sought.

Finlayson's diagnosis of the "modernity/morality imbrication" identifies a second, distinct instability. Habermas's discourse ethics does not merely derive (U) from universal pragmatics; it also embeds that derivation within a theory of social modernization, according to which the emergence of post-conventional moral consciousness represents a genuine developmental achievement of Western rationalization. Finlayson argues that this embedding creates a dependency: the claim that discourse ethics expresses a post-conventional moral point of view draws on a prior ethical self-understanding rooted in the Western modernization narrative. If this is correct, then Habermas's alleged post-conventional universality is not derived purely from the formal presuppositions of argumentation but is internally dependent on a historically particular horizon. The discourse ethics program has two justificatory strategies — the formal-pragmatic derivation and the reconstructive appeal to modernity's moral self-understanding — and they may not be compatible. Finlayson contends that the tension between them reveals an unresolved problem in Habermas's attempt to found discourse ethics without committing to Apel's stronger claims, since neither strategy alone is sufficient to bear the normative weight the program requires.

Heller (1985) provides the structurally earliest version of what becomes a recurring objection. Writing at the moment of Habermas's first programmatic statement of discourse ethics, she argues that the theory faces an irresolvable dilemma: if discourse ethics is purely formal — deriving everything from the procedural conditions of argumentation — it yields empty formalism, unable to determine what morality concretely requires. If it adds substantive content, it smuggles in normative commitments that the formal derivation was supposed to generate. This formalism objection is structurally independent of the quasi-transcendental question but intersects with it: the quasi-transcendental derivation is supposed to show that the conditions of argumentation generate (U) as a non-empty principle, and if Heller's dilemma is correct, either the derivation fails or it succeeds only by importing content that is not genuinely derived.

Together, these three internal challenges suggest that the instability in Habermas's program is not merely a consequence of departing from Apel's framework but runs through the original program itself. Heath locates the problem in the internal development of Habermas's thought; Finlayson locates it in the program's original dual justificatory strategy; Heller identifies it in the formal structure of the quasi-transcendental move as such. These diagnoses collectively anticipate Apel's external objection — examined in Section 4 — that abandoning Letztbegründung leaves normativity without adequate grounding. But they also suggest that the problem may be internal to Habermas's own program rather than a straightforward consequence of rejecting Apelian foundationalism. What is at stake, across all three challenges, is whether a method situated between empirical theory and transcendental argument can do what discourse ethics requires of it.

## Section 4: The Apel-Habermas Debate: Three Axes of Disagreement

The philosophical distance between Apel and Habermas is easy to understate. They share a research program, a common intellectual inheritance from Peirce and the later Heidegger, and a central commitment to the idea that the presuppositions of rational argumentation carry normative weight that no coherent agent can refuse. Yet the disagreement that developed from the 1980s onward is not merely a difference of degree — ambitious versus modest — but a set of three intersecting disputes about what philosophical justification can achieve, what conceptual resources it requires, and whether Habermas's post-metaphysical strategy is as independent of Apelian premises as it presents itself to be. Reconstructing these three axes in order allows each dispute's logical structure to become visible, and sets the stage for Gamwell's diagnosis that neither position can be resolved without resources both parties have formally forsworn.

### 4.1 Can Letztbegründung Be Achieved?

The first axis concerns the cogency of reflexive ultimate justification. Apel holds that a transcendental-pragmatic argument can establish, with strict philosophical necessity, the normative presuppositions of argumentation. The argument is self-referential: anyone who sincerely argues already employs norms of symmetry, mutual recognition, and truth-orientation; anyone who tries to deny these norms within argumentation commits a performative self-contradiction, because the very act of denial presupposes the conditions it rejects. This self-referential structure, Apel maintains, gives the argument a non-hypothetical status that distinguishes it from empirical reconstruction. In his retrospective assessment of Apel's legacy, Habermas credits the original insight — that language use is irreducibly normative and that the presuppositions of argumentation carry moral weight no participant can coherently refuse — while maintaining his longstanding resistance to the conclusion that this insight delivers *Letztbegründung* (Habermas 2020). The key move in Habermas's dissent is the claim that transcendental reflection on the presuppositions of argumentation is itself fallible. We reconstruct what we take to be the unavoidable conditions of rational communication, but this reconstruction is an epistemic achievement subject to revision, not an apodeictic grasp of necessary structure. The difference from Apel is not about whether such presuppositions exist but about whether access to them escapes the fallibilism that governs all other inquiry.

Kettner's comparative mapping of the two programs identifies this precisely (Kettner 2006). Both Apel and Habermas accept the discourse-ethical framework in which moral norms are valid if they could command the assent of all affected parties under conditions of ideal discourse. The divergence lies upstream: for Apel, the ideal communication community functions as an a priori normative criterion accessible through transcendental reflection; for Habermas, it is a counterfactual idealization immanent to actual communicative practices that we reconstruct empirically-theoretically, not a transcendental ground we disclose through first-philosophy. Kettner documents how this upstream divergence propagates through the full architecture of each program — affecting the status of (U), the relationship between moral and ethical questions, and the treatment of the Part A / Part B distinction Apel insists upon.

The post-1992 trajectory of Habermas's work widened this gap in ways that Apel regarded as progressive abandonment of universalist ambitions. Rapic's monograph *Normativität und Geschichte* (2020) traces this widening with precision: in *Between Facts and Norms*, Habermas reconceives the normative grounding of discourse ethics not as a philosophical derivation but as an "explication of meaning" — a reconstruction of the normative self-understanding implicit in the institutions and discourse forms of modern democratic societies. From Apel's perspective, this move situates discourse ethics within a historically specific normative horizon rather than grounding it in presuppositions that hold for any possible argumentation. The Stroud-Stern framework established in Section 1 applies here directly: Habermas's post-1992 move is precisely the "substitution" Stroud's objection predicts — trading a strong transcendental conclusion for a weaker claim about what modern democratic actors must presuppose. Apel sees this as a principled failure; Habermas presents it as the proper recognition of what post-metaphysical philosophy can and cannot deliver.

### 4.2 Detranscendentalization and Its Normative Costs

The second axis concerns what Habermas calls "detranscendentalization" — the embedding of communicative reason within historically and socially conditioned lifeforms rather than deriving it from a priori conditions of possible experience or argumentation. In Habermas's post-metaphysical thinking, there is no view from nowhere: communicative rationality is always reason as it exists in social practices, linguistic traditions, and historical forms of life. The normative presuppositions of argumentation are reconstructed from the competences that actual communicators deploy, not deduced from the structure of a transcendental subject or the ideal conditions of a communication community posited independently of any historical instantiation (Habermas 1992).

Apel's objection is structural. Mendieta's careful reconstruction of the divergence identifies what is at stake in the choice between "transcendental pragmatics" and "universal pragmatics" as methodological self-descriptions (Mendieta 2002): Apel's use of "transcendental" signals the non-hypothetical, non-empirical character of his access to argumentation's presuppositions, while Habermas's "universal" signals that his pragmatics tracks communicative competence — a genuine but contingently acquired and revisable achievement of social evolution. A detranscendentalized reason is, from Apel's standpoint, a historically situated reason. And a historically situated reason cannot ground universal moral norms that transcend any particular cultural or historical context, because those norms will always be refracted through the normative horizon of the form of life from which the reconstruction proceeds. Habermas's discourse ethics, on this objection, buys its post-metaphysical plausibility at the price of genuine universalism.

Michalska (2019) advances the most analytically precise response to this exchange — one that turns the objection back against Habermas from a different angle. Her argument is that Habermas's schema of validity claims and the project of reconstructive sciences do not actually escape the need for Apelian transcendentals; rather, they presuppose the concept of the ideal communication community as a necessary underpinning that Habermas employs without explicitly thematizing. On Michalska's reconstruction, Apel's transcendental pragmatics establishes the normative role of the ideal communication community not as a "supernorm" juridically constraining all discourse, but as an action-regulative principle: a means by which agents coordinate and orient their communicative practice toward something beyond any particular historically situated consensus. If this is correct, then Habermas's validity claim schema — which requires that arguers orient themselves toward a possible consensus that transcends any local agreement — already smuggles in precisely this regulative concept, even while officially rejecting the transcendental framework that makes its normative force intelligible. Habermas has not escaped Apelian transcendentalism; he has concealed its structure by giving it different terminological clothing.

The implication is that detranscendentalization, taken seriously, would cost more than Habermas is willing to pay. If reason is genuinely embedded in social lifeforms with no residual transcendental dimension, then the validity claims raised in speech acts — truth, normative rightness, sincerity — cannot function as genuinely universal orientation points but only as standards internal to particular communicative practices. This is not Habermas's position, but Michalska's argument suggests it is the position that genuine detranscendentalization requires. The official doctrine sits uneasily between a transcendental structure it formally rejects and a historical embeddedness that cannot by itself generate the universality discourse ethics requires.

### 4.3 Gamwell's Dilemma and Irresolvability

The third axis is diagnostic rather than first-order: it concerns the question of whether the debate itself can be adjudicated within the shared philosophical commitments that both parties bring to it. Gamwell's analysis of the Habermas-Apel exchange arrives at a symmetrical verdict: each correctly criticizes the other, and for reasons that are internally compelling given their shared starting point (Gamwell 1997). Apel correctly identifies that Habermas's fallibilism leaves the universalization principle without sufficient grounding. If the presuppositions of argumentation are reconstructed fallibly, then their normative bindingness is also merely provisional — it holds as a de facto feature of our communicative practices as we currently reconstruct them, but there is no guarantee that a better reconstruction would disclose the same norms, and no non-circular way to rule out such revision. This is, in essence, Stroud's substitution objection applied to discourse ethics: the quasi-transcendental conclusion (we must presuppose these norms) does not establish what discourse ethics needs (these norms genuinely bind). Habermas, in turn, correctly identifies that Apel's ultimate justification program overreaches what the performative contradiction argument can establish. As Stern's analysis of retorsive arguments suggests, the argument from self-contradiction either proves only what a straightforward deductive argument would prove — in which case the performative element is redundant — or it merely shows that denying the norm is pragmatically awkward for someone engaged in arguing, without showing that the norm binds outside that specific context.

Gamwell's conclusion is that neither position can be resolved within the postmetaphysical commitment they share (Gamwell 1997). Both Apel and Habermas are committed to deriving universal moral principles without appeal to metaphysical facts about the good, human nature, or the structure of practical reason independent of communicative practice. But a universal moral principle, Gamwell argues, cannot be redeemed independently of metaphysics: some substantive account of what makes norms binding, not merely what makes them presupposed, appears to be required. This is not an argument for returning to pre-Kantian metaphysics, but a diagnostic claim that the shared postmetaphysical constraint forecloses the resources that would be needed to resolve the dispute.

Johnston reaches a structurally similar conclusion from within the context of educational theory (Johnston 2016). His analysis of Habermas's "weak" transcendental approach identifies a specific internal tension: the attempt to combine Kantian transcendental idealism with Meadian social pragmatism generates commitments that cannot be consistently maintained. The social-pragmatist strand implies that normative standards are immanent to communicative practices and their developmental history; the transcendental strand implies that there are conditions of possible communication that hold independently of any particular developmental stage. Johnston concludes that some form of quasi-transcendental argument may nonetheless be indispensable for discourse ethics — Apel's maximal foundationalism overreaches, but Habermas's minimal reconstruction underdelivers — without being able to specify what a stable intermediate position would look like.

Finlayson's analysis of the "modernity/morality imbrication" adds a further dimension to the irresolvability diagnosis (Finlayson 2000). Habermas's claim to post-conventional universality is internally dependent on a prior ethical self-understanding rooted in the particular Western modernization narrative. Discourse ethics presents itself as deriving universal norms from formal conditions of argumentation alone; but Finlayson's reconstruction shows that the derivation is only compelling to agents who already share a modern understanding of rationality, autonomy, and the normative significance of undistorted communication. The universality claim is thus internally compromised: it presents as universal what is in part a culturally specific achievement. This does not vindicate Apel's transcendental program, but it undermines the post-conventional status that Habermas's fallibilist reconstruction was supposed to secure without transcendental backing.

Taken together, these three axes define the space of the debate. Axis 1 establishes that the disagreement over Letztbegründung is not merely about philosophical ambition but about whether fallibilist reconstruction can sustain the normative force discourse ethics requires. Axis 2 establishes that detranscendentalization, while philosophically motivated, generates a structural tension that Michalska's dependency argument makes difficult to dissolve. Axis 3 establishes, through Gamwell's mutual-critique analysis, that neither program can resolve the debate using only the resources that both parties' shared postmetaphysical commitments make available. If the debate is internally irresolvable on these terms, the objections from outside the tradition — examined in Section 5 — take on additional urgency. They are no longer merely supplementary challenges but alternative diagnostics of the same underlying problem.

## Section 5: The Critics: External Challenges and Internal Pressures

The objections that have accumulated against discourse ethics divide, usefully if roughly, into external and internal. External critics contest the intelligibility or necessity of the universalist project itself — they doubt that philosophical argument from communication presuppositions can ground moral norms at all. Internal critics accept the project's aims while contesting specific moves, arguing that the programs of Apel and Habermas fail on their own terms. A further intermediate category — the feminist critiques — occupies a distinctive position: they work within the tradition of emancipatory theory and press for transformation rather than rejection, but in doing so they expose assumptions encoded in the allegedly neutral procedures of both programs. Understanding which objections bear specifically on Apel's stronger claims, which target Habermas's weaker version, and which cut equally against both is essential for assessing the current state of the debate.

### 5.1 Naturalist and Pragmatist Deflation

The sharpest external challenge to both programs comes not from within moral philosophy but from a pragmatist refusal of the philosophical enterprise they share. Rorty (1989) argues that liberal democratic solidarity rests not on philosophical arguments from universal communicative presuppositions but on the cultivation of sympathy through narrative and contingent historical practices. The capacity to recognize others as fellow sufferers — to extend one's "we-feeling" beyond familiar circles — is, on this account, a function of imaginative literature and sentimental education, not of transcendental reflection. The entire Apel-Habermas project is therefore not just wrong but unnecessary: its foundationalist ambition mistakes the genre of moral culture.

In his earlier mediation of the Habermas-Lyotard dispute, Rorty argues that Lyotard is right to identify the insuperable difficulty with Habermas's meta-narrative of communicative rationalization, while Habermas correctly shows that Lyotard has no resources to criticize existing injustice (Rorty 1985). Rorty's own resolution — solidarity must rest on contingent democratic commitments rather than philosophical grounding — applies with equal force to Apel. Apel's Letztbegründung purports to show that the skeptic who denies discourse norms performatively contradicts herself; Rorty's reply is that this demonstration, even if successful, adds nothing to the persuasive work that a good novel or a vivid account of suffering already does more effectively.

Williams (2006) approaches the same deflation from a different angle. The "morality system" — the framework of obligation, universalizability, and impartial justification that Kant initiated and that Apel and Habermas inherit — is, Williams argues, a culturally specific and philosophically confused construction. The "thick" ethical concepts embedded in practices (courageous, cruel, treacherous) are prior to any universalist reconstruction, and no derivation from argumentation presuppositions can recover their action-guiding force. Williams does not endorse relativism; he argues rather that the attempt to found ethics on a system of universal obligations misidentifies the structure of ethical life. The neo-Kantian aspiration shared by both Apel and Habermas — deriving binding norms from universalizable reasons — is, on this view, a philosophical detour that arrives back where it started, stripped of the richness that makes ethical concepts actually motivating.

Together, Rorty and Williams put pressure on a presupposition that Apel and Habermas share: that philosophical justification of universal norms is both possible and necessary for the legitimacy of moral claims. The challenge is particularly acute for Habermas's fallibilist program. Apel at least offers a determinate philosophical result — Letztbegründung through retorsive argument — that, if it succeeded, would distinguish the transcendental-pragmatic enterprise from mere persuasion. Habermas's quasi-transcendental reconstruction, which explicitly avoids apodeictic status, must explain why fallible reconstructive science does more than Rorty's narrative solidarity in grounding normative claims.

### 5.2 Contextualist and Postmodern Objections

MacIntyre (1981) and Lyotard (1984) mount structurally distinct but convergent challenges to the universalist-proceduralist model. MacIntyre's argument is teleological and historical: modern moral philosophy, including Kantian and neo-Kantian universalism, is incoherent because it has severed moral predicates from the teleological contexts within which they acquired their meaning. Rational universal moral principles cannot be derived from the bare conditions of discourse; they require substantive traditions with their own narrative structure and conception of human flourishing. The universalist procedures that Apel and Habermas defend are, on MacIntyre's account, parasitic on precisely the particular traditions they claim to transcend. They derive their apparent plausibility from shared moral intuitions embedded in those traditions while presenting themselves as neutral arbiters that transcend any tradition.

Lyotard's objection is formally distinct. Rather than arguing that universalism secretly depends on particularity, Lyotard contends that the very aspiration to consensus suppresses an irreducible heterogeneity of language games. The "differend" — the wrong suffered by those whose testimony cannot be articulated in any available idiom — is precisely what consensus-based discourse cannot register. Habermas's appeal to rational consensus as a regulative ideal is not merely philosophically overambitious; it actively operates as a hegemonic narrative that delegitimizes forms of expression that do not conform to the genre of argumentative discourse (Lyotard 1984). This objection cuts more directly against Habermas than against Apel: Habermas's insistence on consensus as the telos of communicative action is the specific target, while Apel's reflexive argument about argumentation presuppositions is less directly implicated.

Delanty (1997) translates these philosophical objections into a sociological critique with specific historical texture. Habermas's theory of communicative rationality, Delanty argues, remains trapped in a specifically Occidental conception of rationality and social learning. Despite its universalist aspirations, the model of communicative rationalization presupposes a particular developmental trajectory rooted in Western modernity, making it unable to engage adequately with non-Western moral traditions and the politics of cultural identity. This diagnosis reinforces what Finlayson (2000) identifies as the "modernity/morality imbrication" in Habermas's program: the universality that discourse ethics claims is internally compromised by its dependence on a historically specific self-understanding of modernity as an emancipatory project.

Habermas's own response — articulated in his 1981 essay defending modernity as an "incomplete project" rather than an exhausted or discredited one — accepts that the emancipatory potential of communicative reason has not been fully realized but insists that this potential is real and not merely occidental (Habermas 1981). Whether this response adequately answers Delanty's sociological point or Lyotard's linguistic one is contested. MacIntyre's objection is harder to answer within the post-metaphysical frame: if moral principles genuinely require teleological traditions to be intelligible, then the claim that argumentation presuppositions can ground them is not merely overambitious but confused.

### 5.3 Feminist Critiques: Transformation from Within

The feminist critiques occupy a different position from the naturalist and contextualist objections. Rather than rejecting the universalist project wholesale, Benhabib, Fraser, and Young work from within emancipatory theory to argue that discourse ethics reproduces structural exclusions in its allegedly neutral procedures. These are internal-transformative critiques: they take seriously the aspiration to universal moral validity while contesting the specific architecture through which both programs pursue it.

Benhabib (1992) identifies the foundational problem as the "generalized other" abstraction. Discourse ethics, as Habermas formulates it, constructs the moral standpoint by abstracting away from the particular histories, needs, and relationships of actual individuals, replacing them with an idealized equal participant in rational argumentation. This abstraction is not innocent: it encodes a specific model of rationality and subjectivity that marginalizes the "concrete other" — individuals whose moral claims arise from particular vulnerabilities and dependencies that the abstracted standpoint cannot register. Benhabib's response is not to abandon universalism but to transform it into "interactive universalism," a more genuinely dialogical model that takes concrete particularity into account without sacrificing the commitment to universal validity.

Fraser (1985) extends the critique by targeting the structural assumptions Habermas inherits from his wider social theory. The distinction between system and lifeworld, and the relegation of domestic caring labor to the lifeworld, naturalizes gender subordination and occludes the power relations structuring the public/private distinction. Communicative action in the lifeworld is not immune to domination simply because it takes symbolic rather than systemic form. Discourse ethics accordingly inherits these gender-blind assumptions: the "ideal speech situation" as a normative benchmark is contaminated by the social structures whose critique requires it.

Young (1990) generalizes this into a critique of impartiality as such. The "logic of identity" underlying universalist moral theories, including discourse ethics, suppresses social group differences by reducing all perspectives to a common universal standpoint. This ostensible neutrality masks the normalization of dominant perspectives: the impartial standpoint that discourse ethics adopts as its moral criterion reflects the standpoint of those whose particularity has already been institutionalized as the universal. Justice, Young argues, requires not the suppression but the affirmation of social group difference.

Against Benhabib's reading, Wright (2004) argues that Habermas's model of reciprocal perspective-taking is more compatible with feminist attention to particularity than Benhabib's critique allows. The universalization procedure in Habermas's moral theory, properly understood, requires genuine perspective-taking rather than replacement of particular perspectives with an abstract universal; this makes it potentially responsive to the demands of care and concrete recognition that the feminist critics identify. Wright's counterpoint is instructive because it shows that the feminist critiques, despite their force, are not univocal in their conclusions: they differ on whether the abstraction is internal to discourse ethics as such or is a revisable feature of particular formulations.

What the feminist literature as a whole establishes is that proceduralist universalism cannot be formally neutral: the design of the procedure encodes substantive assumptions about who speaks, what counts as a relevant consideration, and which forms of communication are legitimate. This connects directly to the Part B problem in Apel's architecture (Section 2) and to the Habermasian justification/application distinction (Section 3): if the procedure already encodes particular assumptions, the gap between universal justification and concrete application cannot be closed by more careful application of the same procedure.

### 5.4 Internal Critiques: Circularity and the Gap in Normative Force

The most technically precise challenges to discourse ethics come from critics who accept the project's aims and engage its justificatory architecture from within. These objections do not dispute the importance of finding universal grounds for moral norms; they argue that neither Apel nor Habermas succeeds in providing them, and that the specific strategies they employ face a structural gap between what they can establish and what they need to establish.

Ferrara (1985) provides the earliest sustained version of this diagnosis. The performative self-contradiction argument establishes, at most, that certain norms are de facto presupposed in the practice of argumentation — that arguers must behave as if these norms hold if their speech acts are to be coherent. What it does not establish is that these norms genuinely bind, that there is any reason beyond social convention to comply with them when the practice itself is up for question. This is the internal version of Stroud's substitution objection applied to practical philosophy: the transcendental reflection on argumentation presuppositions yields a weaker conclusion (we must represent ourselves as bound by these norms in order to argue) than the stronger conclusion discourse ethics requires (we are genuinely bound by them). Ferrara's critique targets Habermas's version of the argument, but its structure applies equally to Apel's Letztbegründung: the reflexive self-establishment of argumentation norms shows that denying them is performatively incoherent, not that they carry independent normative authority.

Heath (2014) restates this gap for Habermas's own later development. The original quasi-transcendental grounding of the universalization principle (U) derived its force from connecting (U) to the analysis of speech acts: the rightness validity claim raised in normative speech acts commits the speaker to the norm's universal acceptability under ideal discourse conditions. Habermas's later revisions severed this connection, separating (U) from the analysis of speech acts in ways that undermined the original derivation without providing an adequate replacement. The result, Heath argues, is a normative gap that neither Habermas's fallibilist reconstruction nor Apel's stronger transcendental argument successfully bridges: discourse ethics requires a principle that does the justificatory work that (U) was supposed to do, but neither program provides it in a form that survives scrutiny.

Su (2024) provides a recent comparative verdict on this gap across the full range of discourse-theoretic approaches. Examining whether Apel's, Habermas's, and Alexy's discourse theories can genuinely ground human rights as both moral and positive rights through discourse, Su concludes that the ambitious project fails across all three: while discourse theories of law address some problems of discourse ethics, no bridge exists between normative validity and social facticity. The gap that Ferrara identifies in 1985 and Heath diagnoses in the context of Habermas's revisions persists in contemporary discourse-theoretic applications. Su's negative assessment is particularly pointed because the human rights domain is precisely where the programs most need to demonstrate their practical reach: if neither Apel's ultimate justification nor Habermas's reconstructive approach can bridge validity and facticity in this paradigm case, the question of which foundation is philosophically superior becomes secondary to the question of whether any argumentation-presupposition-based foundation can deliver what discourse ethics requires.

These internal critiques close the loop with the Gamwell dilemma from Section 4. Gamwell's diagnosis — that Apel correctly identifies Habermas's fallibilism as leaving (U) without sufficient grounding, while Habermas correctly identifies Apel's ultimate justification as overreaching what the performative contradiction argument can establish — now appears not merely as a stalemate between two competing programs but as a symptom of a structural problem that affects both. If the gap between de facto presupposition and genuine normative bindingness cannot be bridged within the post-metaphysical commitment that both share, then the objections from outside the tradition (Rorty, MacIntyre, Lyotard) and from within it (Ferrara, Heath, Su) jointly identify a problem that neither program has yet resolved.

## Section 6: Contemporary Assessments: Reform, Revival, and Successors

The sustained pressure from both internal critics and external objections surveyed in Section 5 has not dissolved the discourse ethics tradition but driven it toward differentiation. Three positions now occupy the terrain: reconstructive defenses that abandon transcendental necessity while retaining proceduralist commitments; Rainer Forst's right-to-justification framework, which extends the quasi-transcendental heritage productively into substantive normative theory; and the comparative assessment of Habermasian constructivism against Rawlsian alternatives, which clarifies what is distinctive — and what is vulnerable — in grounding moral validity in communicative presuppositions rather than idealized choice procedures.

### 6.1 Rebooting and Relaunching: Reconstructive Defenses

The most direct response to the impasse diagnosed by Gamwell (1997) is reconstruction from within: accept that Apel's foundationalism and Habermas's quasi-transcendentalism have each reached their limits, and ask what can survive once both forms of transcendental scaffolding are relinquished. Heath (2014) and Kettner (2011) represent the two most developed versions of this strategy, differing principally in how far they are willing to travel from the tradition's original aims.

Heath's proposed "reboot" replaces Habermas's quasi-transcendental grounding with what he describes as a Durkheimian rather than Kantian foundation: normative validity is anchored in the pragmatics of cooperative interaction and the mutual dependencies that language-use creates among social actors, rather than in idealized procedures of rational consensus. The key move is to distinguish a weak proceduralism — compatible with naturalist constraints and fallibilist epistemology — from the stronger transcendental claims that gave the original program its characteristic ambition. On Heath's account, abandoning the quasi-transcendental claim does not forfeit the normative core; a reconstructed discourse ethics can still identify the constraints that cooperative interaction places on acceptable reasons, without asserting that those constraints have been uncovered through reflection on the necessary conditions of rational argumentation as such (Heath 2014).

Kettner's "realistic relaunch" is explicitly post-transcendental in orientation. Where Heath's revision has an immanent character — treating the quasi-transcendental dimension as an overclaim rather than an essential feature — Kettner argues that both Apel's foundationalism and Habermas's quasi-transcendentalism have reached an impasse and that neither represents a viable trajectory forward. What survives the relaunch is a commitment to the rational resolution of practical conflicts through public justification under conditions of mutual recognition, without any claim that this commitment is established by necessity rather than by considered reflection on the conditions of normative discourse (Kettner 2011). The normative orientation remains; the philosophical warrant changes character.

The reconstructive position is not without costs, and Finlayson's diagnosis remains a standing warning. His argument that Habermas's discourse ethics rests on an entanglement of ethical and moral justification — where the allegedly post-conventional, universal standpoint of discourse ethics is internally dependent on a modernity-specific interpretation of rationality and autonomy — applies with equal or greater force to any reconstructive defense that inherits the tradition's conceptual vocabulary without scrutinizing its historical embedding (Finlayson 2000). A discourse ethics rebooted on Durkheimian rather than Kantian lines is still a discourse ethics, and the question of whether its proceduralist architecture can be genuinely universalized or merely generalized from within a particular social-historical horizon does not disappear by dropping the transcendental language.

Taken together, the reconstructive defenses represent the most pragmatically viable option currently available for those who want to preserve something of the discourse ethics program. But they achieve this viability through the greatest departure from the tradition's original aims: the claim that moral norms can be validated through reflection on communicative presuppositions, with a force that is not merely persuasive or conventional, recedes from view once both forms of transcendental grounding are surrendered.

### 6.2 Forst and the Productivity of Quasi-Transcendental Justification

Against the reconstructive position, Rainer Forst's work demonstrates that quasi-transcendental justification can be philosophically productive — generating determinate normative results across multiple domains — without requiring either Apel's Letztbegründung or the concessive retreat that Heath and Kettner recommend. Forst's framework represents, within the current literature, the strongest case that the impasse Gamwell diagnosed is not irresolvable but addressable through a reformed deployment of the Habermasian heritage.

The organizing principle is the basic right to justification: every person subject to a norm that claims to bind them has a standing right to demand justificatory reasons adequate to that claim. This right is not derived from a prior moral theory or posited as a substantive value; it functions, rather, as a quasi-transcendental constraint on any normative practice, in that its denial is incompatible with the structure of normative address itself — to claim that a norm binds someone is already to invite the question of why. The constraint is quasi-transcendental rather than apodeictic: Forst does not claim to establish it through a reflexive argument immune to revision, but rather reconstructs it from within the practice of giving and demanding reasons (Forst 2011; Forst 2017).

Deployed across the justification of human rights, the framework shows that rights cannot be grounded in naturalistic accounts of universal human needs, nor in merely political accounts that make rights contingent on particular legal orders, because both approaches presuppose the very structure of justification they would explain (Forst 2011). Deployed in social theory, Normativity and Power extends the quasi-transcendental core into a critical account of how power operates through the control of justification — through the ability to present contingent norms as non-contingent — and how critique consists in exposing that contingency by appealing to the right that normative power systematically suppresses (Forst 2017). In each application, quasi-transcendental justification yields substantive results while remaining fallibilist: the right to justification is not a metaphysical absolute but a reconstructive claim about the structure of normative practice, revisable in light of further argument.

McCarthy's earlier comparative analysis is directly relevant here. Distinguishing Rawlsian "constructivism," which builds principles from idealized models of persons in the original position, from Habermasian "reconstructivism," which identifies normative presuppositions already operative in actual communicative practices, McCarthy (1994) argues that the two approaches differ on the role of idealizations, the scope of practical reason, and the relationship between moral and political philosophy. Forst's framework can be read as developing the reconstructivist option in a direction McCarthy sketches but does not execute: a quasi-transcendental constraint that is reconstructed from practice rather than constructed from a model of idealized agency, and that can therefore generate determinate normative results without the circularity charges that Rawlsian constructivism faces.

The significance of this for the Apel-Habermas debate is that Forst's approach represents the best available response to Apel's charge that Habermas's fallibilism leaves normativity without genuine grounding. Forst demonstrates that surrendering Letztbegründung need not mean surrendering the capacity to generate philosophically determinate normative theory; the quasi-transcendental constraint, operating without apodeictic status, can nonetheless organize substantive reasoning about rights, power, recognition, and redistribution.

### 6.3 The Habermas-Rawls Debate and Constructivist Rivals

The third line of contemporary assessment situates Habermasian quasi-transcendental constructivism within the broader landscape of Kantian constructivism, asking whether the discourse ethics tradition retains comparative advantages over its Rawlsian and Korsgaardian competitors. Two recent contributions address this question in ways that bear directly on the structural features of the Apel-Habermas debate.

Rees (2020) argues that Habermasian constructivism avoids the "shmagency" objection that has been pressed against Korsgaardian constitutivism. The objection, in brief, is that constitutivism — which grounds normative authority in facts about practical identity or the constitutive aims of agency — faces a circularity problem: the normative force of constitutive aims must itself be established, but any attempt to establish it either regresses or invokes the very normative authority in question. Rees contends that Habermas's strategy sidesteps this circularity because it grounds normative constraints not in the self-constitution of practical reason but in the presuppositions of communicative action — features of a non-moral practice of mutual address that can be identified without already invoking the moral authority they are supposed to generate. Whether or not one accepts this reading, Rees's argument highlights a structural feature of the quasi-transcendental approach: by grounding validity claims in communicative rather than practical presuppositions, Habermas locates the normative basis in a domain that is not itself already moral, which is precisely what Forst's right-to-justification inherits (Rees 2020).

Pedersen (2011) approaches the Rawls-Habermas divergence from a different angle, reconstructing the debate around the justification/application distinction. This distinction, central to Habermas's program and formalized in his discourse-theoretic framework, is absent from Rawlsian constructivism: Rawls does not systematically distinguish between determining which moral norms are universally valid and determining which among those norms applies in a particular situation. For Habermas, the distinction is load-bearing: universalization operates at the level of justification, while the selection of applicable norms requires a further practical judgment sensitive to context. Pedersen argues that this allows Habermas to preserve moral universalism without demanding that universal norms directly determine particular judgments — a flexibility that Rawlsian constructivism cannot achieve without importing substantive considerations at the level of principle formation itself (Pedersen 2011). The justification/application distinction also maps onto the structural asymmetry between Apel and Habermas: Apel's transcendental foundation operates at the level of justification and leaves the application problem formally unresolved; Habermas's quasi-transcendental reconstruction builds in the distinction from the start.

Habermas's direct engagement with Rawls in his 1995 article sharpens the underlying disagreement. Against Rawls's restriction of public reason to an overlapping consensus among those who already share liberal premises, Habermas argues that this cannot achieve genuine impartiality: it privileges liberal conceptions of persons and justice in ways that cannot be justified to those who do not already accept those premises. A discourse-theoretic alternative grounds moral validity not in a hypothetical consensus among idealized choosers but in the procedural requirements of actual argumentation, where the universalization principle functions as the constraint that no idealized starting point can substitute for (Habermas 1995).

Together, these contributions indicate that Habermasian constructivism retains genuine comparative advantages in contemporary debates: structural avoidance of the circularity charges facing constitutivist rivals, contextual flexibility through the justification/application distinction, and a grounding in communicative rather than idealized-agency presuppositions. These advantages, however, do not resolve the deeper instability that the Apel-Habermas debate has exposed. Michalska's argument — that Habermas's schema of validity claims implicitly relies on the ideal communication community concept he officially rejects — raises the question of whether these structural advantages are genuinely available to a program that has suppressed rather than resolved its transcendental commitments (Michalska 2019). Whether this constitutes a deeper instability in the post-metaphysical program as a whole, or a recoverable inconsistency that a reformed quasi-transcendental strategy might address, is the unresolved question to which this review's analysis has been building.

## Conclusion

The literature reviewed here converges on a single structural finding: the Apel-Habermas debate is not a disagreement between a careful philosopher and a reckless one, nor between rigor and pragmatism, but an instance of the dilemma that Stroud's substitution objection generates for any argument that attempts to derive normative conclusions from the conditions of rational practice. Apel responds by defending an ambitious transcendental argument — the performative self-contradiction argument for Letztbegründung — that purports to establish those conditions with apodeictic certainty. Habermas responds by limiting his claims to quasi-transcendental reconstruction, treating communicative presuppositions as fallible hypotheses recoverable from communicative competence rather than as necessities disclosed through transcendental reflection. Each response incurs characteristic costs. Apel's program achieves systematic unity and normative determinate force, but at the price of exposure to Stern's (2017) pessimistic verdict that retorsive arguments either collapse into standard deductive proofs or merely harden the skeptic's resolve. Habermas's program avoids this exposure but generates, as Heath (2014) and Finlayson (2000) independently argue, internal instabilities: later revisions severed the universalization principle (U) from the speech act analysis that originally gave it quasi-transcendental status, leaving discourse ethics with weaker justificatory foundations than either the early Habermasian or the Apelian version can claim.

Three questions remain unresolved in the literature. The first is whether any version of the performative self-contradiction argument can achieve what Apel (2023) claims for it, or whether Stern's pessimism is definitive. The Kuhlmann-Keuth exchange of the 1980s mapped the internal fault line precisely: Kuhlmann argued that reflexive self-grounding gives the argument a non-hypothetical status unavailable to empirical claims, while Keuth countered that inescapability of de facto presuppositions is exactly what Stroud predicts a modest argument can establish — and no more. The subsequent literature has not closed this dispute. What it has established is that Apel's program requires a richer account of the transition from "pragmatic inescapability" to "normative bindingness" than the original performative self-contradiction formulation supplies.

The second unresolved question concerns Habermas's detranscendentalization itself: whether it is a principled post-metaphysical move or an evasion that inherits Apelian commitments without Apelian rigor. Michalska's (2019) argument is the most analytically acute version of this challenge. She contends that Habermas's schema of validity claims and the project of reconstructive sciences presuppose the ideal communication community concept that Apel explicitly thematizes and Habermas officially rejects. If this is correct, Habermas has not escaped transcendentalism but obscured its structure. The implication Gamwell (1997) draws is the sharpest diagnostic available: each thinker is correct in criticizing the other, and neither position can be resolved within the post-metaphysical commitment they share. Gamwell's conclusion — that a universal moral principle may require metaphysical resources both parties have forsworn — registers as an unresolved challenge rather than a settled verdict, but it clarifies what a successful response to the dilemma would need to provide.

The third question concerns Forst's right-to-justification framework: whether it is best understood as a productive descendant of the Apel-Habermas tradition or as a sufficiently different approach that the transcendental scaffolding can be quietly dropped. Forst (2017) demonstrates that quasi-transcendental justification can yield determinate normative results — in human rights theory, in the analysis of power, in the redistribution-recognition debate — without claiming apodeictic status. Whether this demonstration resolves the Gamwell dilemma or simply defers it depends on whether the right to justification itself requires a transcendental grounding or can be sustained as a reconstructive hypothesis. The existing literature does not settle this question.

These three unresolved questions define the space into which the present research project intervenes. The project examines whether a revised quasi-transcendental strategy — one that takes seriously both Michalska's argument about implicit transcendental reliance and Forst's productive deployments — can respond to the Gamwell dilemma in a way the existing literature has not. The review has established that this question is genuine: Gamwell's irresolvability thesis has not been assessed in the light of the structural parallels between the Apel-Habermas debate and the ambitious-modest distinction developed in analytic transcendental argument theory, and the mapping of that parallel is itself an analytical contribution. Beyond clarifying this relationship between modest transcendental arguments and quasi-transcendental reconstruction, the project aims to assess which of the major objections canvassed here — Rorty's pragmatist deflation, the feminist-internal critique, the circularity objections from Ferrara and Heath — can be addressed within a reformed quasi-transcendental framework and which require more fundamental revision. The literature reviewed has shown that some objections bear specifically on Apel's stronger claims, others specifically on Habermas's weaker version, and still others cut against the shared post-metaphysical framework itself. Mapping that distribution is a precondition for any reform of the tradition that does not inadvertently replicate the instabilities it seeks to resolve.
