## Section 2: Career Development — From Levinasian Alterity to Transmodern World-System

Treating Dussel's account of Eurocentrism as a single doctrine flattens what the intellectual-historical literature consistently presents as a layered itinerary. Pimenta (2025) and Maldonado-Torres (2008) provide complementary periodizations that converge on three principal phases: an Argentine Levinasian-phenomenological liberation philosophy of the 1970s; a 1990s reformulation prompted by the dialogue with Karl-Otto Apel and Habermasian discourse ethics; and a late world-system synthesis articulated in the *Ethics of Liberation* (Dussel 2013) and the parallel *Politics of Liberation* project. The interpretive thesis these readings share — and that Dussel's own self-narrations corroborate — is cumulative rather than substitutive: each phase deepens the prior, with Levinasian alterity remaining a substrate beneath the later world-system and transmodern apparatus.

The first phase is reconstructed by Martín (2019) as a genealogical exercise rooted in the Argentine intellectual context of the late 1960s and 1970s, in which Dussel reframed Levinas's ethics of the face of the Other as a phenomenology of the periphery. Ball (2019) traces how this Levinasian register was already fused with a Marxian and liberation-theological critique, with Dussel diagnosing what he took to be the limits of each source rather than simply combining them. On this reading, the early *Para una ética de la liberación latinoamericana* already contains, in seed, the claim that Eurocentric reason occludes the Other — though without the world-historical machinery that the late work supplies.

The second phase is most economically tracked through Dussel's own account in "Principles, Mediations, and the 'Good' as Synthesis" (Dussel 1997b) and the "Architectonic of the Ethics of Liberation" (Dussel 1997a). There Dussel narrates the Apel encounter as forcing the explicit articulation of a formal-discursive moment alongside the material-ethical moment retained from the early framework, with the "good" emerging as their synthesis under a feasibility constraint. Pimenta (2025) reads this not as a concession to discourse ethics but as a structural elaboration: the Apel dialogue reshaped the form of the critique without softening its content, since the material principle — the affirmation of the life of the victim — continued to anchor the architectonic. Maldonado-Torres (2008) similarly emphasizes continuity, treating the Apel-phase reformulation as an internal expansion of the alterity ethics rather than its supersession.

The third phase, surveyed across Dussel's anthology *Beyond Philosophy* (Dussel 2003) and crystallized in the 2013 *Ethics*, integrates the prior two registers within a world-system frame drawn from Wallerstein, Frank, and Abu-Lughod. The 2007 essay "From Fraternity to Solidarity" (Dussel 2007) marks a further inflection toward politics, recasting the Eurocentrism critique as a positive normative program of global solidarity, while "Transmodernity and Interculturality" (Dussel 2012) supplies the pluriversal horizon. Pimenta (2025) argues that the critique of Eurocentrism deepens precisely because world-system analysis is added to — not substituted for — the Levinasian and discourse-ethical strata. The upshot for the rest of this review is that "Dussel's account of Eurocentrism" is a moving target whose evaluation depends on which phase, and which interlocutor (Levinas, Apel, Wallerstein/Quijano), one foregrounds.
