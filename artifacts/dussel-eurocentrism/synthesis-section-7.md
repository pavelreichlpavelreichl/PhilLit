## Conclusion

The literature on Dussel's account of Eurocentrism has settled, if not into consensus, then into three identifiable positions. A foundational-decolonial reading, exemplified by Maldonado-Torres (2008) and Grosfoguel (2012), treats Dussel's myth-of-modernity thesis and its 1492 periodization as a load-bearing commitment of the decolonial turn, to be extended (toward "coloniality of being," toward pluriversal epistemics) rather than revised at its core. An internally-tensioned-but-revisable reading, advanced by Stromback (2024), Vizcaíno (2021), and Timmenga, Van der Zande, and Zwitter (2025), accepts that Dussel's framework contains live tensions — between alterity and immanent critique, between anti-fetishism and Afro-Atlantic religiosity, between the ego conquiro thesis and explicit philosophical pluralism — but argues these are productive rather than fatal. A third position, articulated by Vargas (2005) and sharpened along the gendered axis by Lange (1998) and Schutte (1991), holds that Dussel's project remains Eurocentric at the meta-philosophical level: the very categories by which alterity is articulated belong to the European tradition the project claims to displace.

Two cross-cutting observations follow. First, the historiographical premise of the 1492 thesis — that pre-conquest Europe was peripheral within a polycentric Afro-Eurasian system — has received less Anglophone scrutiny than the ethical superstructure built atop it, a gap registered by Pachón Soto (2012). Second, transmodernity's standing as a genuinely pluriversal alternative remains contested, with the analectic and trans-ontological machinery the principal target. The relation between Dussel's Latin Americanist decoloniality and African and Caribbean decolonial thought is a productive but under-elaborated frontier. The open question for future work is whether Dussel's framework can absorb feminist and Afro-Atlantic critiques without collapsing into the postmodernism it rejects.
