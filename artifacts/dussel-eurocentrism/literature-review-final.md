## Introduction

Enrique Dussel's critique of Eurocentrism has become a reference point across at least four overlapping conversations — Latin American philosophy of liberation, the modernity/coloniality/decoloniality network, post-Levinasian ethics of alterity, and the world-system reframing of modern intellectual history — yet the secondary literature has not converged on a single reading of what that critique amounts to or how it should be assessed. Depending on the interpreter, Dussel appears as a foundational figure of the decolonial turn, as a heterodox Marxist who reroutes world-system analysis through a philosophy of the periphery, as a Levinasian ethicist for whom the alterity of the colonized Other grounds normative critique, or as a liberation-theological thinker continuing a counter-tradition internal to Christianity. These are not merely different emphases: each carries different implications for how the core thesis — that European modernity rests on a "myth" by which the violence of 1492 is recoded as civilizational mission (Dussel 1995) — should be reconstructed and tested.

This review treats Dussel's account of Eurocentrism as a layered and moving target rather than a fixed doctrine, and asks what is distinctive in his formulation and how its distinctive features have been received and contested. Three live questions organize the analysis. First, does the historiographical claim succeed on its own terms? Dussel argues that pre-1492 Europe was peripheral within an Afro-Eurasian inter-regional system and that only the colonization of the Americas allowed Europe to constitute itself as "center"; the question is whether this world-historical premise can bear the weight of the philosophical superstructure built atop it. Second, does transmodernity offer a genuine alternative both to Eurocentric universalism and to a postmodern relativism that Dussel reads as itself Eurocentric? Third, and most pressing for the project's evaluation, does Dussel's framework escape the Eurocentrism it diagnoses, or does it reproduce that Eurocentrism at the meta-philosophical level by relying on European theoretical apparatus — Levinas, Marx, Apel — to ground its anti-Eurocentric critique (Vargas 2005)?

The Anglophone reception, mediated above all by Mendieta and Alcoff's framing of Dussel for English-language philosophy (Alcoff and Mendieta 2000), has tended to foreground the ethical and political payoff while leaving the historiographical premises comparatively under-examined. Within the decolonial constellation, Maldonado-Torres (2008) reads Dussel as continuous from early phenomenology of alterity to the late world-system critique. Recent work (Timmenga, Van der Zande, and Zwitter 2025) defends the coherence of Dussel's combination of alterity and immanent critique against charges that the two strands pull apart. The sections that follow reconstruct Dussel's argument, trace its development across career phases, examine transmodernity as positive program, locate Dussel within the decolonial constellation, and stage the most pointed objections.

## Section 1: The Myth of Modernity and the 1492 Thesis

Dussel's critique of Eurocentrism turns on a structural claim about modernity itself: that it has, as it were, two birthdates and two faces. On one face, modernity is the emancipatory rationality announced in the language of autonomy, critique, and universal subjectivity; on the other, it is what Dussel calls a "myth" — the rational self-justification by which colonial violence is recoded as civilizational uplift, and by which the suffering of the colonized Other is rendered a regrettable but necessary cost of human progress (Dussel 1995). The thesis is not that modernity has emancipatory content alongside contingent historical excesses, but that the emancipatory and the sacrificial moments are constitutively entangled: Europe's self-image as the autonomous origin of reason is itself an artifact of the very occlusion it performs. Mills (2018) offers a careful reconstruction of this two-faced structure for anglophone readers, emphasizing that Dussel's "myth" operates not as ideology in the loose sense but as a quasi-transcendental logic of justification — what is hidden is not a fact about the past but the conditions under which Europe could constitute itself as "center" at all. The Mendieta–Alcoff anthology (Mendieta 2000) earlier marked the pivot of anglophone reception toward taking this genealogical claim seriously rather than treating it as rhetorical excess.

The philosophical stakes sharpen when the genealogy is pressed back behind Descartes. Dussel's contention is that the *ego cogito* has a precursor — and an underwriter — in the *ego conquiro*, the conquering "I" of 1492 whose practical certainty about its own world-constituting power furnishes the existential template for the later epistemic self-certainty of the cogito (Dussel 1995). The point is not biographical succession but conceptual dependency: the disengaged subject that Descartes inaugurates becomes available as a philosophical figure only after a historical subject has already constituted itself by reducing the Other to the merely conquerable. Maldonado-Torres (2008) reads this move as the hinge between Dussel's early phenomenology of alterity and his later world-system analysis, since the *ego conquiro* operates simultaneously at the level of subject-formation and at the level of geopolitical structure. Lange (1998) endorses the analytical productivity of dividing modernity into emancipatory and sacrificial paradigms while warning that the figure of the indigenous Other can, in Dussel's own hands, be functionalized as a foil for European self-criticism — a worry to which the review returns in Section 5.

A second pillar of the argument is historiographical rather than ethical. Dussel claims that pre-1492 Europe was peripheral within a polycentric Afro-Eurasian inter-regional system dominated by Islamic, Indian, and Chinese cultural-economic circuits, and that only the conquest and exploitation of the Americas allowed Europe to convert itself into "center" of an emerging world-system (Dussel 2013). The long historical chapter "Beyond Hellenocentrism" in the 2013 *Ethics of Liberation* draws on Abu-Lughod and Frank to dismantle the genealogy that runs Greece–Rome–Christian Europe–Modernity as a single autochthonous lineage, replacing it with a multi-centered map in which the Mediterranean is one node among several. The 2009 essay "A New Age in the History of Philosophy" extends the same move into philosophy proper, arguing that Egyptian, Chinese, Indian, and Islamic traditions are not "influences on" but co-constitutive participants in what later becomes "European" thought (Dussel 2009). Pachón Soto (2012) provides the close exegetical reconstruction of this historiographical layer, observing that Dussel's critique of Eurocentric historiography of philosophy is logically prior to, and load-bearing for, the ethical diagnosis: if Europe was never the autonomous center it took itself to be, then the "myth" is not merely a moral failure but a category mistake about world history. Pimenta (2025) frames the same point developmentally, arguing that the world-system reframing is not a late add-on but the maturation of a critique of Eurocentrism that deepens as it absorbs world-historical evidence.

The normative payoff Dussel draws from this double critique — ethical and historiographical — is transmodernity: not an anti-modern rejection nor a postmodern dissolution of universality, but a project of taking up modernity's emancipatory rationality "from the underside" while affirming the alterity of the colonized Other (Dussel 2012). The architectonic of his ethics organizes this payoff around material, formal, and feasibility moments rather than a single principle (Dussel 1997), and Deere (2017) reads the resulting "pluriversal modernity" as an attempt to hold open a non-absolute conception of the Other that does not collapse into either liberal universalism or particularist identitarianism. Whether the philosophical machinery underwriting this move — the analectic method and the trans-ontology of alterity — is internally coherent is contested (Irvine 2017), and Vargas (2005) raises the further worry that the very categories in which transmodernity is articulated remain European in provenance. Those debates belong to later sections; what the present section establishes is that the 1492 thesis, the *ego conquiro* genealogy, the polycentric historiography, and the transmodern horizon form a single integrated argument, not a collection of theses that can be detached from one another without loss.

## Section 2: Career Development: From Levinasian Alterity to Transmodern World-System

Treating Dussel's account of Eurocentrism as a single doctrine flattens what the intellectual-historical literature consistently presents as a layered itinerary. Pimenta (2025) and Maldonado-Torres (2008) provide complementary periodizations that converge on three principal phases: an Argentine Levinasian-phenomenological liberation philosophy of the 1970s; a 1990s reformulation prompted by the dialogue with Karl-Otto Apel and Habermasian discourse ethics; and a late world-system synthesis articulated in the *Ethics of Liberation* (Dussel 2013) and the parallel *Politics of Liberation* project. The interpretive thesis these readings share — and that Dussel's own self-narrations corroborate — is cumulative rather than substitutive: each phase deepens the prior, with Levinasian alterity remaining a substrate beneath the later world-system and transmodern apparatus.

The first phase is reconstructed by Martín (2019) as a genealogical exercise rooted in the Argentine intellectual context of the late 1960s and 1970s, in which Dussel reframed Levinas's ethics of the face of the Other as a phenomenology of the periphery. Ball (2019) traces how this Levinasian register was already fused with a Marxian and liberation-theological critique, with Dussel diagnosing what he took to be the limits of each source rather than simply combining them. On this reading, the early *Para una ética de la liberación latinoamericana* already contains, in seed, the claim that Eurocentric reason occludes the Other — though without the world-historical machinery that the late work supplies.

The second phase is most economically tracked through Dussel's own account in "Principles, Mediations, and the 'Good' as Synthesis" (Dussel 1997b) and the "Architectonic of the Ethics of Liberation" (Dussel 1997a). There Dussel narrates the Apel encounter as forcing the explicit articulation of a formal-discursive moment alongside the material-ethical moment retained from the early framework, with the "good" emerging as their synthesis under a feasibility constraint. Pimenta (2025) reads this not as a concession to discourse ethics but as a structural elaboration: the Apel dialogue reshaped the form of the critique without softening its content, since the material principle — the affirmation of the life of the victim — continued to anchor the architectonic. Maldonado-Torres (2008) similarly emphasizes continuity, treating the Apel-phase reformulation as an internal expansion of the alterity ethics rather than its supersession.

The third phase, surveyed across Dussel's anthology *Beyond Philosophy* (Dussel 2003) and crystallized in the 2013 *Ethics*, integrates the prior two registers within a world-system frame drawn from Wallerstein, Frank, and Abu-Lughod. The 2007 essay "From Fraternity to Solidarity" (Dussel 2007) marks a further inflection toward politics, recasting the Eurocentrism critique as a positive normative program of global solidarity, while "Transmodernity and Interculturality" (Dussel 2012) supplies the pluriversal horizon. Pimenta (2025) argues that the critique of Eurocentrism deepens precisely because world-system analysis is added to — not substituted for — the Levinasian and discourse-ethical strata. The upshot for the rest of this review is that "Dussel's account of Eurocentrism" is a moving target whose evaluation depends on which phase, and which interlocutor (Levinas, Apel, Wallerstein/Quijano), one foregrounds.

## Section 3: Transmodernity as Positive Program

If Dussel's diagnosis of the "myth of modernity" stops at unmasking, it remains a critique without consequence. The constructive payoff is transmodernity — the claim that the way past Eurocentric modernity is neither its repetition nor its rejection but its assumption from "the underside." In the programmatic statement of the project, Dussel (2012) frames transmodernity as a pluriversal undertaking that "subsumes" modernity's emancipatory rationality (formal-procedural reason, universal solidarity, the critical impulse) while affirming the alterity of the cultures that modernity occluded. The position is therefore double-edged by design: transmodernity is anti-Eurocentric without being anti-modern, and it is pluralist without surrendering normativity.

Three contrasts structure the proposal. First, against postmodernism: Dussel reads the postmodern dissolution of the grand narrative as a properly internal European event — the West deconstructing itself with the very resources it produced — and therefore as a Eurocentric self-criticism that leaves the colonial Other where it found her (Dussel 2012). Mills (2018), in his book-length reconstruction, emphasizes that this is why transmodernity is offered as a "pluriverse" rather than a particularism: the move is not to multiply local Western critiques but to begin from exteriority. Second, against anti-modernism: transmodernity does not romanticize a pre-1492 outside untouched by modernity, but engages modernity's emancipatory moments dialectically. Third, against Eurocentric universalism: the universal is reconstituted as an analogical convergence across loci of enunciation rather than projected from a single center.

The contested philosophical machinery is the analectic method and the "trans-ontology" it presupposes. Irvine (2017) argues that Dussel's analectics — the move "beyond" dialectic to a moment of revelation by the Other — relies on an ontological framework whose terms (totality, exteriority, alterity) are continuous with the metaphysics it claims to exceed, raising the question whether transmodernity escapes the categorial economy of Western thought or only renames its outside. Deere (2017) presses a related but materialist worry: the figure of "the Other" risks being treated as an absolute, when what the colonial history actually shows is concrete, geographically and economically situated subjects whose alterity is internally differentiated. A more materialist transmodernism, Deere suggests, would resist the temptation to treat alterity as a metaphysical placeholder.

How the project is taken up by allies sharpens the stakes. Grosfoguel (2012) appropriates transmodernity as the conceptual hinge for a decolonial pluriversalism running from Césaire to the Zapatistas, recasting it as an alternative to both Western universalism and postmodern relativism. Whether this reception confirms transmodernity's stability as a third position or merely relocates the underlying tension between universalism and particularism is the question Sections 4 and 5 take up.

## Section 4: Dussel and the Decolonial/Postcolonial Constellation

Situating Dussel requires resisting two tempting reductions: collapsing him into the modernity/coloniality/decoloniality (MCD) network as one voice among interchangeable others, and conflating that network with the Anglophone postcolonialism that shares some of its targets but little of its method. The secondary literature increasingly recognizes a division of labor inside the MCD network. Grosfoguel (2009) reads the network as an integrated decolonial political economy in which Quijano supplies the structural-sociological account of the "coloniality of power," Mignolo supplies the epistemic-semiotic account of border thinking and the geopolitics of knowledge, and Dussel supplies the philosophical-ethical apparatus — transmodernity, the analectic, the ethics of the Other — that articulates what a non-Eurocentric universality might be. Maldonado-Torres (2011a) positions Dussel's philosophy of liberation as a foundational current of the "decolonial turn," tracing how Dussel's analectics and ethics of alterity feed into the network's later vocabulary, while Misoczky (2019) argues that Quijano and Dussel function as complementary rather than competing frameworks, the one diagnosing the structures of racialized labor, the other supplying the philosophical ground for resistance. Pimenta (2025) makes the case that Dussel's account is irreducible to either Quijano's sociology or Mignolo's epistemic critique because it integrates economic history, world-system analysis, and metaphysics into what Pimenta describes as a "geopolitical turn in philosophy."

Where the literature is thinner is in registering the network's internal disagreements. Mignolo's (2000, 2011) program of "epistemic disobedience" and "delinking" reconfigures Dussel's geopolitical framing into a primarily semiotic project, and Mignolo (2020) restates the colonial-matrix-of-power thesis in a register that subordinates Dussel's ethical-philosophical machinery to a logic of epistemic refusal. The Mignolo–Escobar (2010) volume, which programmatically articulates the "decolonial option," exhibits these tensions without resolving them: positions are placed alongside one another more than reconciled. Vélez and Tuana (2020) name Quijano, Mignolo, Dussel, and Maldonado-Torres as the male-dominated "canon" of the decolonial turn, a framing that reveals as much about the network's self-presentation as it does about its hierarchies — and that anticipates the feminist critiques pursued in Section 5.

The sharper external boundary runs between this constellation and Anglophone postcolonialism. Grosfoguel (2011) is the canonical statement: postcolonial studies in the Said–Spivak–Bhabha lineage remained tied, on his reading, to a culturalist paradigm and an inadequate political economy, while the modernity/coloniality school anchors itself in 1492 and the colonial world-system inherited from Wallerstein. Lindner (2025) revisits the dispute, contrasting Chakrabarty's strategy of "provincializing Europe" with Mignolo's "delinking" and arguing — against the decolonial self-presentation — that the postcolonial framework better accommodates Fanon and C. L. R. James. Maldonado-Torres (2011b) frames the decolonial turn as post-continental, distinct from the linguistic and pragmatic turns, and rooted in resistance traditions reaching back five centuries rather than to nineteenth-century empire. The Moraña, Dussel, and Jáuregui (2008) volume stages the encounter most directly, with Dussel himself a co-editor; Bhambra (2014) offers a convergence position, treating postcolonial historical sociology and the modernity/coloniality program as connected sociologies that share a quarrel with disconnected historiography. What unites Dussel's distinctive position within these debates is a refusal of the linguistic turn's primacy, a 1492 rather than 1857 or 1885 periodization, and a theological-Marxian heritage absent from postcolonialism's largely Foucauldian and deconstructive toolkit.

The Latin Americanist starting point becomes visible as a starting point — rather than a neutral frame — when African and Caribbean voices enter. Grosfoguel (2012) traces a pluriversal lineage from Césaire through Dussel to the Zapatistas, presenting transmodernity as continuous with Afro-Caribbean critique of Eurocentric universalism. Ndlovu-Gatsheni (2020) extends the 1492 thesis to argue that asymmetrical North–South power and the racial relegation of African subjectivity constrain African futures, converging with Dussel on periodization while shifting the historical center of gravity toward African experience. Vellem (2017) presses harder: a Black Theology of Liberation must un-think Hellenocentric epistemology at a spiritual and not only cognitive level, raising the question whether African concepts such as *umoya* fit a framework whose theological substrate remains Catholic and Latin American. The convergence is real — anti-Eurocentrism, anti-Hellenocentrism, refusal of secular-dualist periodizations — but the divergence is real too: African historical experience does not map cleanly onto a 1492-centered narrative, and the Caribbean route through Fanon and Césaire arrives at decolonial pluriversalism by a path that Dussel's Latin Americanism does not fully retrace. The upshot is that Dussel's account of Eurocentrism is more philosophically ambitious than Quijano's structural sociology and more historically anchored than Mignolo's epistemic-turn version, but its claims to global reach depend on negotiations with African and Caribbean traditions that the secondary literature has only begun to map.

## Section 5: Reception and Criticism: Does Dussel Escape Eurocentrism?

The critical literature on Dussel converges on a single recursive question: does the framework that diagnoses Eurocentrism reproduce it at the meta-philosophical or methodological level? The objections divide into four kinds of pressure — internal-conceptual, feminist, ontological-methodological, and the sympathetic revisions that absorb these pressures without abandoning the project.

The first and most persistent worry is the self-application charge. Vargas (2005) argues that the philosophy of liberation diagnoses Eurocentrism in others while remaining heavily reliant, at the level of theoretical apparatus, on European categories — Levinasian alterity, Marxian critique, Apelian discourse ethics — such that its anti-Eurocentric program risks performative contradiction. Sharman (2011) extends the same worry into historiography: postmodern and decolonial critiques of Eurocentric modernity, Dussel's included, must articulate themselves in the very European genealogical idiom they aim to displace, and the "yet…" of his title marks the difficulty of writing one's way out of that idiom. Vizcaíno (2021) presses the worry on different ground, arguing that Dussel's anti-fetishist method retains a Eurocentric anthropology of religion that misrepresents Afro-Atlantic religious traditions — Eurocentric residues, on this reading, lodge not only in the conceptual scaffolding but in the implicit cosmology Dussel mobilizes against Eurocentric metaphysics.

The feminist critique opens a second front, targeting the figure of the Other on whom the entire ethical apparatus rests. Schutte (1991) issued the early charge that Dussel's ethics, despite its liberatory rhetoric, sustains authoritarian and patriarchal residues — a charge that has shaped subsequent feminist reception. Lange (1998) deepens the objection in a sympathetic register: Dussel's two-paradigm reading of modernity is productive, but his construction of indigenous peoples risks instrumentalizing them as a foil for European self-criticism, leaving internal indigenous gender hierarchies unexamined and bringing his framework, against his own self-positioning, closer to the postmodernism he rejects. Vélez and Tuana (2020) generalize the diagnosis at the level of the network: Dussel sits within a male-dominated decolonial canon (alongside Quijano, Mignolo, and Maldonado-Torres) whose foundational texts decolonial feminism — through Lugones in particular — has had to revise from the outside. The convergent point across Schutte, Lange, and Vélez/Tuana is that the alterity at the heart of Dussel's critique is itself a gendered construction, and that critique cannot be insulated from the meta-philosophical worry: which Other gets to figure as the ground of liberation?

The third pressure is ontological and methodological. Irvine (2011) argues that the trans-ontology underwriting the analectic method is internally inconsistent — it claims to exceed European ontology while resting on metaphysical commitments that are themselves drawn from the tradition it disavows. Sager and Spencer (2016) raise a methodological complement: even granting Dussel's corrective to Deweyan Eurocentrism, his framework would benefit from a more fallibilist, experimental procedure of social transformation, in place of the architectonic certainties that the analectic appears to license. Together, Irvine and Sager/Spencer convert the self-application worry into a structural one — the problem is not only that European categories are used, but that the deepest layer of the apparatus, the metaphysics of alterity, may be doing work the framework officially forbids.

A fourth strand, recent and sympathetic, defends the project under revision. Timmenga, Van der Zande, and Zwitter (2025) argue that the apparent tension between Dussel's Levinasian alterity and his commitment to immanent critique is not incoherence but a productive double commitment, defensible against charges of contradiction. Stromback (2024) reconstructs the ego conquiro thesis as the paradigm of modern epistemic violence and pushes Dussel's framework toward an explicit philosophical pluralism — preserving the diagnosis while resolving the residual universalist temptation. Maldonado-Torres (2008) extends Dussel through the "coloniality of being," and Alcoff (2013) treats the Anti-Cartesian Meditations as a serious genealogy worth defending while flagging its historical limits. Allen and Mendieta (2021) house the current version of this defense, situating Dussel in dialogue with Frankfurt-school critical theory.

The unresolved tension, which the critical and sympathetic literatures share, is whether grounding critique in the alterity of the Other can be done with European philosophical machinery without that machinery silently reasserting itself — a question on which the literature remains live rather than settled.

## Conclusion

The literature on Dussel's account of Eurocentrism has settled, if not into consensus, then into three identifiable positions. A foundational-decolonial reading, exemplified by Maldonado-Torres (2008) and Grosfoguel (2012), treats Dussel's myth-of-modernity thesis and its 1492 periodization as a load-bearing commitment of the decolonial turn, to be extended (toward "coloniality of being," toward pluriversal epistemics) rather than revised at its core. An internally-tensioned-but-revisable reading, advanced by Stromback (2024), Vizcaíno (2021), and Timmenga, Van der Zande, and Zwitter (2025), accepts that Dussel's framework contains live tensions — between alterity and immanent critique, between anti-fetishism and Afro-Atlantic religiosity, between the ego conquiro thesis and explicit philosophical pluralism — but argues these are productive rather than fatal. A third position, articulated by Vargas (2005) and sharpened along the gendered axis by Lange (1998) and Schutte (1991), holds that Dussel's project remains Eurocentric at the meta-philosophical level: the very categories by which alterity is articulated belong to the European tradition the project claims to displace.

Two cross-cutting observations follow. First, the historiographical premise of the 1492 thesis — that pre-conquest Europe was peripheral within a polycentric Afro-Eurasian system — has received less Anglophone scrutiny than the ethical superstructure built atop it, a gap registered by Pachón Soto (2012). Second, transmodernity's standing as a genuinely pluriversal alternative remains contested, with the analectic and trans-ontological machinery the principal target. The relation between Dussel's Latin Americanist decoloniality and African and Caribbean decolonial thought is a productive but under-elaborated frontier. The open question for future work is whether Dussel's framework can absorb feminist and Afro-Atlantic critiques without collapsing into the postmodernism it rejects.
