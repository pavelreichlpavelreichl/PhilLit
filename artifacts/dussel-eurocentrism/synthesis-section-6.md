## Section 5: Reception and Criticism — Does Dussel Escape Eurocentrism?

The critical literature on Dussel converges on a single recursive question: does the framework that diagnoses Eurocentrism reproduce it at the meta-philosophical or methodological level? The objections divide into four kinds of pressure — internal-conceptual, feminist, ontological-methodological, and the sympathetic revisions that absorb these pressures without abandoning the project.

The first and most persistent worry is the self-application charge. Vargas (2005) argues that the philosophy of liberation diagnoses Eurocentrism in others while remaining heavily reliant, at the level of theoretical apparatus, on European categories — Levinasian alterity, Marxian critique, Apelian discourse ethics — such that its anti-Eurocentric program risks performative contradiction. Sharman (2011) extends the same worry into historiography: postmodern and decolonial critiques of Eurocentric modernity, Dussel's included, must articulate themselves in the very European genealogical idiom they aim to displace, and the "yet…" of his title marks the difficulty of writing one's way out of that idiom. Vizcaíno (2021) presses the worry on different ground, arguing that Dussel's anti-fetishist method retains a Eurocentric anthropology of religion that misrepresents Afro-Atlantic religious traditions — Eurocentric residues, on this reading, lodge not only in the conceptual scaffolding but in the implicit cosmology Dussel mobilizes against Eurocentric metaphysics.

The feminist critique opens a second front, targeting the figure of the Other on whom the entire ethical apparatus rests. Schutte (1991) issued the early charge that Dussel's ethics, despite its liberatory rhetoric, sustains authoritarian and patriarchal residues — a charge that has shaped subsequent feminist reception. Lange (1998) deepens the objection in a sympathetic register: Dussel's two-paradigm reading of modernity is productive, but his construction of indigenous peoples risks instrumentalizing them as a foil for European self-criticism, leaving internal indigenous gender hierarchies unexamined and bringing his framework, against his own self-positioning, closer to the postmodernism he rejects. Vélez and Tuana (2020) generalize the diagnosis at the level of the network: Dussel sits within a male-dominated decolonial canon (alongside Quijano, Mignolo, and Maldonado-Torres) whose foundational texts decolonial feminism — through Lugones in particular — has had to revise from the outside. The convergent point across Schutte, Lange, and Vélez/Tuana is that the alterity at the heart of Dussel's critique is itself a gendered construction, and that critique cannot be insulated from the meta-philosophical worry: which Other gets to figure as the ground of liberation?

The third pressure is ontological and methodological. Irvine (2011) argues that the trans-ontology underwriting the analectic method is internally inconsistent — it claims to exceed European ontology while resting on metaphysical commitments that are themselves drawn from the tradition it disavows. Sager and Spencer (2016) raise a methodological complement: even granting Dussel's corrective to Deweyan Eurocentrism, his framework would benefit from a more fallibilist, experimental procedure of social transformation, in place of the architectonic certainties that the analectic appears to license. Together, Irvine and Sager/Spencer convert the self-application worry into a structural one — the problem is not only that European categories are used, but that the deepest layer of the apparatus, the metaphysics of alterity, may be doing work the framework officially forbids.

A fourth strand, recent and sympathetic, defends the project under revision. Timmenga, Van der Zande, and Zwitter (2025) argue that the apparent tension between Dussel's Levinasian alterity and his commitment to immanent critique is not incoherence but a productive double commitment, defensible against charges of contradiction. Stromback (2024) reconstructs the ego conquiro thesis as the paradigm of modern epistemic violence and pushes Dussel's framework toward an explicit philosophical pluralism — preserving the diagnosis while resolving the residual universalist temptation. Maldonado-Torres (2008) extends Dussel through the "coloniality of being," and Alcoff (2013) treats the Anti-Cartesian Meditations as a serious genealogy worth defending while flagging its historical limits. Allen and Mendieta (2021) house the current version of this defense, situating Dussel in dialogue with Frankfurt-school critical theory.

The unresolved tension, which the critical and sympathetic literatures share, is whether grounding critique in the alterity of the Other can be done with European philosophical machinery without that machinery silently reasserting itself — a question on which the literature remains live rather than settled.
