## Introduction

G.A. Cohen's *Karl Marx's Theory of History: A Defence* (1978) reconstructs historical materialism as a determinate, philosophically rigorous social theory rather than a loose interpretive framework. Cohen's stated aim was to subject Marx's theory of history to the same standards of definitional precision and argumentative transparency expected elsewhere in analytic philosophy — a "no-bullshit" method that broke self-consciously with both orthodox Marxist exegesis and the Hegelian-inflected continental traditions that had dominated prior discussion of historical materialism (Cohen 1978). This analytical reconstruction did not emerge without precedent: in an earlier symposium exchange with Acton, Cohen had already defended historical materialism against standard objections concerning falsifiability and the causal role of ideas (Cohen and Acton 1970), and in "Being, Consciousness and Roles" he had begun developing an account of production relations in terms of effective material control rather than legal ownership — the germ of what the 1978 book would elaborate as the "rechtsfrei" characterization of the economic structure (Cohen 1974).

The book's argument rests on two interlocking claims. The Development Thesis holds that the productive forces possess a built-in historical tendency to develop; the Primacy Thesis holds that the level of development of the productive forces explains the character of the relations of production, which in turn explain the legal-political superstructure. Cohen defends this explanatory priority as a form of functional explanation, modeled loosely on evolutionary biology: economic structures obtain because, and only for as long as, they are suited to developing the productive forces they contain, and are superseded once they come to "fetter" further development. Making this claim non-circular requires Cohen's distinction between the purely material and the socially/legally described properties of production — a distinction whose adequacy becomes the focus of sustained later contestation (Section 1 below provides the full exposition).

The book's reception combined rapid acclaim with methodological controversy from the outset. Ruben (1981), reviewing the book's contribution to Marxist philosophy, quotes Jon Elster's judgment that it "sets a new standard for Marxist philosophy," while himself pressing the Primacy Thesis on whether its textual and functional-explanatory support is as secure as Cohen claims. Miller (1981) situates the book as the culmination of a decade of analytic efforts to rehabilitate Marx against mid-century dismissals, while questioning whether the pivotal notion of "fettering" is defined precisely enough to bear the causal weight the Primacy Thesis assigns it.

This review proceeds from exposition of the book's core theses, through its contemporary reception, the functional-explanation controversy it provoked, the Analytical Marxism movement it founded, Cohen's own subsequent qualifications, the class-struggle-primacy critique mounted from within Marxist theory, and finally external challenges from Hegelian/historicist and feminist perspectives. One limitation should be flagged at the outset: a substantial portion of the historical debate — including Elster's and Roemer's own founding methodological statements and nearly all documentation of Cohen's later self-revisions — could not be independently verified through recoverable abstracts within this project's sourcing constraints. Where these positions bear on the argument, they are reconstructed here through secondary sources with complete metadata, a limitation of the available evidentiary record rather than any indication that the debates themselves did not occur.

## Section 1: The Book's Core Theses: Development, Primacy, and Functional Explanation

Before surveying the critical literature, it is necessary to state Cohen's own theses precisely, since virtually every debate treated in later sections turns on a specific reading of, or objection to, one of the claims set out here.

### 1.1 The Development and Primacy Theses

Cohen (1978) reconstructs historical materialism as two interlocking claims. The Development Thesis holds that the productive forces of a society tend, across history, to develop: human beings, facing conditions of scarcity, are disposed to seize opportunities to improve their productive power. The Primacy Thesis holds that the level of development of the productive forces explains the character of a society's economic structure (its relations of production), which in turn explains the character of its legal-political and ideological superstructure. On this reconstruction, an economic structure obtains, and persists, because it is suited to developing the productive forces it contains, and is superseded once it comes to "fetter" further development. Central to this account is a distinction between the purely material properties of productive forces and relations of production and their social (legally or normatively described) properties — a distinction that allows Cohen to characterize the economic structure independently, or "rechtsfrei," of the legal categories that structure is supposed to explain.

This apparatus did not emerge fully formed in 1978. In the 1970 symposium with Acton, Cohen answered standard mid-century objections to historical materialism — that it is unfalsifiable, that it neglects the causal role of ideas, that base and superstructure cannot be non-circularly distinguished — while Acton pressed the classical skeptical case against the theory's determinism (Cohen and Acton 1970). Cohen (1974) then developed, in "Being, Consciousness and Roles," an account of production relations in terms of effective material control rather than legal ownership, explicitly setting out three requirements any adequate clarification of historical materialism must meet: it must specify what would confirm or disconfirm it, remain recognizably Marx's thesis, and avoid triviality. This essay supplies the germ of the material/social distinction later central to the 1978 book. Ruben (1981), reviewing the finished book, judged that it "sets a new standard for Marxist philosophy" in definitional and argumentative rigor, while pressing the further question of whether the textual and philosophical case for the Primacy Thesis's explanatory priority is as strong as Cohen claims — a question that anticipates the internal-Marxist critique addressed in Section 6.

### 1.2 Functional Explanation as the Logical Form of Historical Materialism

Cohen's fullest statement of the explanatory strategy underlying the Primacy Thesis appears in "Functional Explanation, Consequence Explanation, and Marxism" (Cohen 1982). There Cohen distinguishes functional (or "consequence") explanation from both ordinary causal explanation and teleological explanation: a structure is explained functionally when it is explained by its propensity to have a certain beneficial effect, on the model of explanations in evolutionary biology, rather than by an antecedent efficient cause. Cohen uses this paper to revise the relationship between "functional" and "consequence" explanation from his 1978 formulation, to defend consequence explanation against metaphysical and epistemological objections, and — most consequentially for the debate reconstructed in Section 3 — to reject Elster's proposal that game theory, rather than functional explanation, should occupy the central explanatory role in Marxist theory. This paper thus establishes Cohen's own terms for the functional-explanation controversy before the opposing side of that dispute is reconstructed, in Section 4, largely through secondary sources.

### 1.3 The Material/Social Property Distinction and Its Contemporary Critics

The material/social distinction was contested from the outset. Suchting (1982), in the Analyse & Kritik symposium, argued that Cohen's reconstruction misdescribes Marx's own conception of productive forces and relations of production, and that class struggle rather than forces-driven development is the primary explanatory factor for the historical periods Marx analyzed. Cohen's "Reply to Four Critics" (Cohen 1983) responded to Suchting alongside Van Parijs, Fisk, and Lukes, clarifying an ambiguity in the argument for historical materialism, insisting that the theory is consistent only if relations of production are explained functionally by their propensity to develop the productive forces, and rebutting the claim that class struggle should carry greater explanatory weight than he assigns it. Mills (1989) later pressed a textual argument that Cohen's "material" is an idiosyncratic, socio-neutral category — asocial facts about human rationality — that does not match Marx's own usage, where the "material" already includes the economic and is therefore inescapably social. These three exchanges directly anticipate the class-struggle-primacy critique and the technological-determinism charge examined in Section 7, marking the material/social distinction, alongside the game-theoretic rejection of Section 1.2, as contested from the theory's earliest reception.

## Section 2: Contemporary Reception and the Early Exegetical Contest, 1978-1985

Within the citable record, the book's immediate reception combined admiration for its argumentative technique with targeted questioning of specific evidential and definitional claims. Ruben (1981) opens his review by citing Elster's verdict that Cohen's *Karl Marx's Theory of History* "sets a new standard for Marxist philosophy," and argues that this praise, properly understood, credits Cohen not with inventing new standards particular to Marxism but with holding Marxist philosophy to the standards of precision and sustained argument already expected of philosophy generally. Ruben's own contribution is a close scrutiny of the Primacy Thesis itself, probing whether the textual and philosophical case Cohen assembles for the explanatory priority of the productive forces is as secure as the book's confident tone suggests, and whether Cohen's functional-explanation strategy secures a genuinely explanatory — rather than merely correlational — form of priority. This combination of admiration for method and skepticism about a specific substantive claim is characteristic of the period's citable reception more broadly.

Miller (1981) situates the book differently, as the culmination of a decade in which Cohen's own earlier articles had already begun rehabilitating Marx against the mid-century Anglo-American verdict, associated with Acton, Popper, and Plamenatz, that Marx's theories were too obscure or confused to merit serious philosophical engagement. On Miller's reading, the 1978 book consolidates that rehabilitative project into a single systematic argument. Yet Miller does not treat the book as settling the matter: he questions specifically whether the textual evidence Cohen assembles for the Primacy Thesis bears the explanatory weight assigned to it, and whether "fettering" — the mechanism by which economic structures are said to be superseded once they impede further development of the productive forces — is defined precisely enough to do the causal work the account requires. Fettering and the strength of textual support for the Primacy Thesis thus emerge, already in this early window, as the two evidential pressure points that recur throughout the functional-explanation debate (Section 3) and the class-struggle-primacy critique (Section 6).

A parallel development complicates any picture in which the technological-determinist reading of Marx belongs to Cohen alone. Shaw (1979), published the year after Cohen's book but argued independently of its functional-explanation apparatus, defends the same broad reading of the 1859 Preface — that productive forces form the material basis determining a society's economic, political, and religious structures — through close textual reconstruction of Marx's correspondence and later writings rather than through consequence-explanation. Shaw's article shows that the interpretation Cohen would go on to defend functionally already had an independent, exegetically driven champion, and that its textual plausibility did not depend on accepting Cohen's methodological apparatus.

The wider reception window is documented in this bibliography chiefly by titles rather than recoverable arguments, so this section's claims should be read as anchored in a thin but substantive citable core. It is worth noting, without further elaboration, that early objections to the book were not confined to philosophical questions about evidence and definition: political critiques from within revolutionary-socialist Marxism also charged that Cohen's functionalism evacuated class struggle as an independent political force — a concern that resurfaces in more academic register in Section 7.

## Section 3: The Functional Explanation Debate

The most sustained methodological controversy provoked by *Karl Marx's Theory of History* concerns the explanatory form Cohen assigns to the Primacy Thesis. Cohen holds that economic structures obtain, and persist, because they are suited to developing the productive forces they contain — a functional, or "consequence," explanation modeled loosely on the logic of natural selection in evolutionary biology (Cohen 1982). This claim that historical materialism's central explanatory relation is functional rather than ordinary-causal became the target of a methodological-individualist challenge associated above all with Jon Elster, which demanded that any functional claim be underwritten by a specified mechanism linking a structure's beneficial consequences to its persistence. Because Elster's and Roemer's own founding statements of this challenge lack recoverable abstracts and are excluded from citation in this review, the debate is reconstructed here chiefly through how later, complete-metadata commentators — Wood, Schwartz, Moggach, and Wolff — characterize and respond to it. What emerges is not a settled verdict but a proliferation of rehabilitation strategies, such that the dispute evolves from a binary opposition between functionalism and individualism into a more pluralistic methodological landscape.

### 3.1 The Methodological-Individualist Critique, Reconstructed

Writing in an *Inquiry* symposium occasioned by Elster's *Making Sense of Marx*, Wood (1986) characterizes Elster's position as combining two claims: a concession that Marx's own particular deployments of functional explanation are often defective, together with a stronger, principled philosophical objection to functional and teleological explanation as such in social science. Wood (1986) argues that this stronger objection rests on a caricature of what functional and teleological explanation actually assert, so that even if Wood grants Elster's point about specific applications, the general case against the explanatory form Cohen employs is considerably weaker than Elster presents it. On Wood's characterization, then, Elster's critique conflates the soundness of particular functional explanations with the legitimacy of the explanatory schema itself.

Schwartz (1993) reconstructs the dispute in different terms, attributing to Elster a "mechanical" conception of explanation that requires an intelligible causal process linking cause and effect, which Elster deploys against Cohen's own "nomological" conception of functional explanation. Schwartz's (1993) argument is that Elster's mechanical standard, properly applied, actually vindicates rather than undermines functional explanation in social science, while Cohen's nomological account is what cannot adequately underwrite the functional claims historical materialism requires. Schwartz (1993) further characterizes Elster as objecting that functional explanation commits theorists to metaphysically bizarre collective subjects, and argues that this objection rests on an implausible, eliminativist reading of methodological individualism — one that need not be accepted even by philosophers otherwise sympathetic to individualist constraints on social explanation. The effect of Schwartz's intervention is to reframe the dispute as turning on which of Cohen's or Elster's own explanatory standards is more defensible, rather than on whether functional explanation is legitimate in the abstract.

Moggach (1991) approaches the critique from a different angle, arguing that Elster's methodological individualism rests on what Moggach calls a "monadic" conception of individual agents — abstracted from the relational and social framework of production within which, on Moggach's reading, Marx's theory situates them. On this characterization, Elster's rational-choice reconstruction cannot adequately capture the interactive, teleological character of social reproduction that Cohen's functionalism, however imperfectly, was attempting to model; the individualist reconstruction, Moggach (1991) contends, sacrifices explanatory resources rather than simply replacing them. Wolff (1990) offers a complementary assessment from outside the analytical Marxist circle, subjecting Elster's game-theoretic reconstruction of Marx to scrutiny and arguing that the formal apparatus of game theory and rational choice does not deliver the explanatory gains Elster claims over functionalist or structural readings, and that methodological individualism as a general constraint on social explanation is less self-evidently correct than Elster and Roemer assume. Taken together, these four complete-metadata sources converge on a common diagnostic: whatever the merits of Elster's and Roemer's demand for microfoundations, the demand as reconstructed by Wood, Schwartz, Moggach, and Wolff does not straightforwardly defeat Cohen's functionalism on its own terms.

### 3.2 Rehabilitating Functional Explanation

A second cluster of responses does not merely characterize the individualist critique but attempts to answer it on its own philosophical footing. Pettit (2000) examines what he calls the "empty black box" objection — the charge, central to the case against Cohen, that functional explanations are vacuous unless they specify the selecting mechanism — and argues that rational-choice explanation itself is frequently open to the identical objection, since appeals to agents' rationality often function as an unexplicated filtering assumption rather than a specified causal mechanism. Pettit's (2000) symmetry thesis turns the charge back onto its source: if functional explanation is suspect for lacking a mechanism, then much rational-choice explanation, including the individualist alternative proposed to supplant Cohen's functionalism, is vulnerable to the same worry.

Carling (2002) pursues a different rehabilitation strategy, reconstructing the debate as fundamentally about social evolution and arguing that Cohen's consequence explanations can be given a defensible selectionist reading, modeled on competitive selection among social forms, that would answer the mechanism objection without requiring full individualist microfoundations. This evolutionary-selection model supplies, on Carling's (2002) account, the kind of "elaboration" the individualist critique demands, while remaining continuous with Cohen's own biological analogy rather than replacing it with game-theoretic microfoundations. Kincaid (2002) situates the Cohen dispute within a broader taxonomy of functionalist explanation across the social sciences, distinguishing cases where functionalist reasoning succeeds from cases where it overreaches by positing self-correcting social equilibria without adequate evidence of the requisite feedback process; on this framework, whether Cohen's version of historical materialism counts as a successful or excessive use of functionalism becomes a case-by-case evidential question rather than one settled by a general philosophical verdict against the explanatory form. Together, Pettit, Carling, and Kincaid show that the "mechanism" demand central to the individualist critique is not uncontested even on its own philosophy-of-science terms.

### 3.3 Structuralist and Later Assessments

A further line of assessment approaches the dispute from outside the analytical Marxist camp altogether, or from a temporal distance that allows for retrospective stock-taking. Callinicos (2004) develops a structurationist account of the relation between agency and social structure and, in the course of doing so, criticizes Cohen's functional explanation of production relations for failing to specify how individual agency mediates between the developmental tendency of the productive forces and the class struggles that are supposed to bring compatible relations of production about; on Callinicos's (2004) reading, Elster's individualist alternative fares no better, since it substitutes reductive microfoundations for a genuine theory of structural constraint and enablement. Van Bouwel (2019) extends the mechanism question into contemporary philosophy of social science, examining the more recent "mechanism-based" turn in analytical sociology and arguing that the availability of social mechanisms does not by itself vindicate methodological individualism, since mechanisms can operate at, or link, multiple levels rather than bottoming out uniquely in individual-level facts — a conclusion that unsettles any simple equation of "mechanism" with "microfoundations" of the sort Elster's critique presupposed. Kumar (2008) offers a more deflationary assessment, arguing from a broadly pragmatist standpoint that the metaphysical stakes Elster and Cohen both attached to the functionalism/individualism dispute may be less important than pragmatic questions about which explanatory strategy proves fruitful for particular explanatory projects within Marxist social theory.

Carling (2006), writing roughly a quarter-century after the book's publication, provides an explicit retrospective judgment: the research program Cohen's functional-explanation apparatus inaugurated retains vitality despite the individualist critiques of Elster and Roemer, provided its contemporary defenders confront the historical fate of Marxism rather than treating the theory of history as a purely abstract methodological exercise. This retrospective assessment does not amount to a claim that the individualist challenge was refuted; rather, read alongside Pettit's symmetry thesis, Carling's selectionist rehabilitation, Kincaid's case-by-case taxonomy, and Van Bouwel's mechanism pluralism, it indicates that the functional-explanation debate settled into methodological plurality rather than resolution in either direction. This trajectory — from a binary confrontation between functionalism and individualism toward a landscape of selection-based, symmetry, and pragmatist positions — sets the stage for the fragmentation of Analytical Marxism as a movement, and for Carling's own typology of "primacy," both taken up in later sections.

## Section 4: Analytical Marxism and the September Group

Cohen's book did not remain an isolated philosophical intervention; it became the founding document of a broader intellectual movement, later named Analytical Marxism, whose members shared a commitment to reconstructing Marxian categories using the tools of analytic philosophy, game theory, and rational-choice social science. Veneziani (2012) supplies a taxonomy of this movement useful for locating Cohen's own contribution within it: he distinguishes Analytical Marxism (AM) as a broad, anti-reductionist approach to reconstructing Marx's core propositions from the narrower Rational Choice Marxism (RCM) associated with Jon Elster and, in places, John Roemer, which insists that social explanation be grounded in individual-level microfoundations. On Veneziani's account, this distinction matters because it separates the functionalist project Cohen defended — compatible with structural and anti-reductionist explanation — from the methodological-individualist current that, within the same broader school, increasingly diverged from it. This taxonomy helps explain why a movement founded on shared rejection of "bullshit" Marxism (Elster's phrase, quoted approvingly across this literature) nonetheless fragmented into distinct and at times incompatible research programs.

That fragmentation is documented by several of the movement's own participants, writing in retrospect. Elster's later self-assessment is the most explicit repudiation of the project's original explanatory ambitions: reflecting on his own involvement, Elster (2011) describes it as a case of "intellectual autophagy," concluding that "non-bullshit Marxism" turned out to be "largely an empty set," and that what remains of value in Marx concerns not the substantive social-scientific theses Cohen had reconstructed but Marx's normative vision of human self-realization through active, joint activity. This verdict, from one of the figures most closely associated with the methodological-individualist critique of Cohen's functionalism (Section 3), constitutes direct evidence — rather than external inference — that at least one wing of the movement judged the explanatory program largely abandoned.

Roemer's retrospective assessment documents a related but distinct divergence. Roemer (2015), responding to Cohen's late writings, traces how the two September Group co-founders' intellectual paths separated after the KMTH-era debates: Cohen moved toward normative egalitarian political philosophy and the assessment of "Cohenesque socialism," while Roemer pursued a political-economy program oriented toward the feasibility of market socialism, introducing his own concept of "Kantian equilibrium" as an alternative account of cooperation. Roemer's essay thus shows the two founders remaining in dialogue about Cohen's legacy even as their substantive projects diverged.

A parallel trajectory is traced within the class-analytic wing of the movement. Burawoy (2020), reflecting on the career of Erik Olin Wright, distinguishes Wright's "Scientific Marxism" — the class-structural, analytically rigorous program continuous with Cohen's and Roemer's functionalist and rational-choice commitments — from his later "Critical Marxism," the normatively oriented Real Utopias Project. Burawoy argues that these two frameworks, joined in Wright's early work, increasingly came apart over the course of his career, with the normative Critical Marxism eventually predominating. This account corroborates, from within the sociological rather than philosophical wing of the movement, the same pattern of divergence between structural-explanatory and normative-political concerns visible in Elster's and Roemer's retrospectives.

Heath (2009) situates this trajectory within a wider comparative frame, arguing that analytical Marxists and Jürgen Habermas, despite starting from unrelated theoretical premises, both ended up "talking themselves out of being Marxists in any recognizable sense of the term" in the course of updating Marxist theory with more defensible social-scientific and normative foundations. Tarrit (2015) narrows this comparative diagnosis back onto Cohen specifically, tracing his shift from KMTH's functionalism through engagement with self-ownership and Rawlsian justice, "while his Marxism was withering away" — a trajectory examined further in Section 5.

Because the primary documentary sources for the September Group's formation and internal history are not independently citable in this corpus, the movement's institutional details — its founding, membership, and meeting practices — cannot be reconstructed here; what the six papers above jointly establish is that a functionalist project, launched by Cohen's book, diversified into rational-choice Marxism, class analysis, and normative political philosophy, with several of its own founders explicitly judging the original explanatory ambitions largely unrealized.

## Section 5: Cohen's Self-Revisions and the Turn to Political Philosophy

The debates reconstructed in Sections 3 and 4 concern how Cohen's 1978 theses were received and contested by others. A separate question is what Cohen himself came to think of them. Secondary literature converges on the claim that Cohen did not hold the Development and Primacy Theses as a fixed position across his career, but instead moved, over roughly two decades, from defending historical materialism as an explanatory theory toward normative political philosophy centered on equality, self-ownership, and a sustained critique of Rawlsian justice. This trajectory is well attested in the sources available to this review, even though none of Cohen's own revisionary texts — where he directly renegotiated the scope of his 1978 claims — can be verified and cited independently here (see the gap note below). The account that follows therefore rests entirely on how other scholars characterize that shift, not on direct engagement with Cohen's own later arguments.

Tarrit (2015) offers the most direct treatment of this trajectory available in the corpus, describing Cohen's path from the functionalist historical materialism of *Karl Marx's Theory of History* through an engagement with the libertarian concept of self-ownership and into the normative debate surrounding Rawls's theory of justice — a period Tarrit characterizes as one in which Cohen's "Marxism was withering away" even as his philosophical output continued. Tarrit frames this not as an abrupt break but as a gradual departure, and asks whether Cohen's intellectual journey remains consistent given his continuing, if attenuated, relationship to Marx's own categories.

Roemer (2015), writing in the same symposium issue as Tarrit, approaches the same trajectory from within the September Group's rational-choice wing. Roemer's essay on Cohen's "final testament" addresses Cohen's theory of distributive justice and the relationship Roemer takes Cohen to have posited between human nature and justice, introducing the idea of a "Kantian equilibrium" as a way of assessing the feasibility of what Roemer calls "Cohenesque socialism." Read alongside Section 4's account of Roemer's own divergence toward market-socialist political economy, this piece documents two September Group co-founders arriving at markedly different destinations from a shared explanatory starting point.

Casal's (2020) feminist critique, treated at length in Section 7, opens by noting that Cohen's 1978 reconstruction is "still widely, and justifiably, considered the best of its kind" some forty years on — a framing that implicitly registers the distance between the book's continued standing as an exegetical achievement and Cohen's own subsequent movement away from defending it as a live explanatory theory.

**Gap note**: The primary texts documenting this shift in Cohen's own words — including his qualified restatements of historical materialism and his explicit turn to egalitarian theory in the late 1980s through 2000 — lack recoverable abstracts in this corpus and are therefore excluded from citation, as are the secondary monographs devoted specifically to this trajectory. This section accordingly reports the fact and rough shape of Cohen's self-revision, corroborated independently by three scholars, without asserting particular claims about the content of those excluded primary texts.

## Section 6: The Primacy of Class Struggle: The Levine-Wright Critique and Its Aftermath

Where Section 3 examined whether Cohen's functional explanations require independent mechanisms, a parallel and more substantively Marxist line of criticism accepted much of Cohen's analytical apparatus but attacked the Primacy Thesis's specific assignment of explanatory priority. This critique holds that Cohen inverts the actual direction of dependency between forces and relations: class struggle, conducted by agents whose interests and capacities are already structured by existing relations of production, is what drives both the development of the productive forces and the transformation of the relations themselves.

### 6.1 The Founding Critique and Its Contemporaries

Levine and Wright's "Rationality and Class Struggle" (1980) states this reversal in its founding form. Against Cohen's claim that the character of the relations of production is functionally explained by the level of development of the productive forces, Levine and Wright argue that relations of production should instead be assigned explanatory priority, with class struggle supplying the actual mechanism connecting the two variables (Levine and Wright 1980). Ruben (1981), writing shortly after both Cohen's book and Levine and Wright's article, examines the Primacy Thesis specifically rather than the book as a whole, probing whether Cohen's functional-explanation strategy secures genuine explanatory priority for the forces over the relations rather than a merely correlational one (Ruben 1981).

Two contributions to the 1982 *Analyse & Kritik* symposium on Cohen's book pursue this reversal on more directly textual and conceptual grounds. Suchting (1982) argues that Marx's own conceptions of "productive forces" and "relations of production" do not support the claim that the growth of the forces basically determines historical development, and identifies class struggle as the primary factor in the epochal transitions Marx actually analyzed. Fisk (1982) distinguishes different senses "primacy" might bear in historical explanation, arguing that on the sense that actually matters for Cohen's project — being the underlying framework governing interconnections between causes and effects, rather than being their direct stimulus — only the relations of production, not technology, can be primary; he further contends that technological development is not autonomous but depends on the cooperation of producers, reintroducing class struggle as a condition of technological change and undercutting a technological reading of class rule (Fisk 1982).

Duquette (1992) extends this line by attacking the material/social property distinction on which the Primacy Thesis depends, arguing that Cohen's way of distinguishing the material "content" from the social "form" of a system of production is defective and therefore fails to support the Primacy Thesis — and that the resulting position, whatever its merits, is not in any case Marx's own (Duquette 1992). Duquette's charge that Cohen's distinction collapses into a "vulgar" technological determinism prompted a direct published reply from Shaw in the same year; that reply is not independently verifiable in this corpus and is not cited here. The underlying question it raised — whether any reconstruction of productive-forces primacy can avoid becoming, in substance, a class-struggle theory — is instead resolved below through Carling's (1993) typology and Chibber's (2011) retrospective assessment.

### 6.2 Typologizing and Testing Primacy

A second wave of literature reframes the dispute from a binary opposition (forces versus relations) into a more differentiated taxonomy of what "primacy" could mean. Mills (1989) approaches the question terminologically rather than through the concept of primacy itself, arguing that Cohen's "technological determinist" reading rests on an idiosyncratic interpretation of Marx's own term "material" as socio-neutral fact about human nature and rationality. Reviewing Marx's writings, Mills argues that the textual evidence favors the traditional interpretation on which the "material" already includes the economic and is therefore inescapably social — so that critics who want the relations of production folded into historical materialism's very explanans have Marx's own terminology and concepts on their side (Mills 1989). This undercuts the material/social distinction at its definitional root, rather than merely disputing the explanatory weight it is asked to bear.

Carling (1993) supplies the taxonomy that structures the remainder of the debate, distinguishing three doctrines that could each be labeled "primacy of the productive forces": Natural Primacy, which he judges probably true but of limited relevance to historical process; Intentional Primacy, Cohen's own doctrine, which Carling judges untenable; and Competitive Primacy, an evolutionary, selection-based doctrine on which class struggle explains the origin of new production regimes while competitive pressures explain their subsequent survival, which Carling argues may be defensible (Carling 1993). Writing nearly three decades after Cohen's book, Laibman (2006) offers a programmatic stock-taking of the resulting research program, surveying the proposals for substantiating productive-forces primacy and the accumulated historical work on the transition to capitalism, and arguing that directedness and necessity in historical transformation do not, in principle, preclude agency or cultural variety (Laibman 2006). Laibman's assessment thus keeps open the possibility that some reconstructed version of primacy remains defensible, in contrast to the more skeptical trajectory running from Levine and Wright through Duquette.

### 6.3 Chibber's Retrospective Verdict

Chibber (2011) evaluates this cumulative literature directly, examining the two most developed attempts to rescue Cohen's thesis: the reconstruction associated with Wright and colleagues, and Carling's evolutionary Competitive Primacy. Chibber argues that both attempts are largely unsuccessful, and that insofar as they do render the primacy thesis more plausible, they do so only by converting it, in substance, into a class-struggle theory of history (Chibber 2011). He concludes that this spells the demise of the classical, technologically determinist version of historical materialism, while explicitly resisting the inference — feared by some defenders of Cohen's project — that abandoning that classical version leaves only a voluntaristic understanding of history. Chibber's verdict functions as this section's capstone: it draws together the founding reversal proposed by Levine and Wright, the textual and conceptual critiques of Suchting, Fisk, and Mills, and the rescue attempts catalogued by Carling and Laibman into a single retrospective judgment. It also pairs with Section 5's account of Cohen's own retreat from strong historical materialism, suggesting that internal-Marxist criticism and Cohen's self-revision converge on a common conclusion about the unsustainability of unqualified productive-forces primacy.

## Section 7: Critiques from Outside Analytical Marxism: Hegelian/Historicist and Feminist Challenges

The debates surveyed so far — over microfoundations (Section 3), the fate of the September Group (Section 4), and the primacy of class struggle (Section 6) — are internal to a broadly analytical style of Marx scholarship that largely accepted Cohen's own terms of argument even while disputing his conclusions. Two other lines of criticism reject those terms more fundamentally: a Hegelian and historicist tradition that objects to rational reconstruction as a method, and a feminist tradition that argues the forces/relations schema, however defended, cannot without substantial revision accommodate gender relations, reproduction, and domestic labor.

### 7.1 Hegelian and Historicist Objections to Rational Reconstruction

The clearest instance of a Hegelian-Marxist critique aimed directly at Cohen's book — Sayers's 1984 argument that KMTH's "entirely non-dialectical" apparatus strips out the categories of contradiction, totality, and internal relation that Marx's own theory of history requires — is documented in the secondary literature but could not be independently verified for this review and is therefore not cited here. A direct Hegelian rebuttal of Cohen is thus known to exist, but the corpus available for this synthesis cannot substantiate its content beyond this acknowledgment.

What the corpus does support is evidence that the underlying methodological contrast — dialectical, agency-centered Marxism versus analytic rational reconstruction — has remained a live alternative independently of any specific engagement with Cohen. Evans (2020) reads Marx's *Capital* through "analytic neo-Hegelian" resources drawn from the Pittsburgh School, treating capital as a contradictory "space of reasons" that simultaneously enables and forecloses human freedom, and explicitly positions this reading as preferable because it "avoids the flaws of analytical Marxism, existentialism and structuralism." This shows the Hegelian/analytical contrast persisting decades after Cohen's book, but Evans's argument differs from Sayers's in period, target, and content: it addresses Marx's account of capital rather than his theory of history, and it is not framed as a response to Cohen specifically. It should not be read as a substitute for the direct critique Sayers is reported to have made.

A related, though distinct, historicist current is E. P. Thompson's agency-centered historical materialism, developed against the "objectivism" Thompson attributed to both orthodox base-superstructure Marxism and Althusserian structuralism. Del Valle Alcalá (2013) reconstructs this tradition, tracing affinities between Thompson's conception of working-class subjectivity and Antonio Negri's theory of proletarian self-constitution, and situates Thompson's target as structuralism rather than analytical Marxism as such. Independent confirmation that Thompson's *The Poverty of Theory* was in fact aimed at Althusser and Popper, and not at Cohen, could not be verified for this review; on the evidence available, del Valle Alcalá's reconstruction should be read as documenting a parallel historicist quarrel with objectivism rather than as establishing a Thompson-Cohen exchange. Subsection 7.1 is accordingly the most thinly evidenced portion of this review: the existence of Hegelian objections to rational reconstruction is well attested, but their direct application to Cohen's book rests on sources this synthesis cannot independently cite.

### 7.2 Feminist Critiques of Historical Materialism and of Cohen Specifically

Casal (2020) provides the corpus's only source directed exclusively at Cohen's book rather than at historical materialism generally, and anchors this subsection. Opening by noting that KMTH is "still widely, and justifiably, considered the best of its kind" forty years after publication, Casal argues that once the theory is extended to the circumstances of women, its component parts fail: predicted developmental patterns do not materialize, Cohen's periodization of historical stages breaks down, and the functional explanation of ideology by its effects on productive development cannot account for the persistence and pervasiveness of sexist ideologies across historical epochs. Casal concludes that these failures reveal Cohen's "orthodox" version of Marxism to function, in this respect, as a conservative theory — one that egalitarians, including Cohen himself in his later normative turn (Section 5), have reason to view with suspicion.

Casal's critique extends a longer-standing feminist engagement with historical materialism's basic categories. Hartsock (2005) develops a feminist standpoint epistemology modeled on the Marxist theory of proletarian standpoint, arguing that women's structural position within the sexual division of labor — centrally, reproductive and domestic labor — generates a standpoint from which the limits of existing, masculinist historical materialism become visible; on this view, historical materialism's basic categories must be reconstructed around gender relations rather than merely extended to include women within an unchanged framework. This is the earliest citable statement of the social-reproduction lineage in this corpus; Lise Vogel's contemporaneous 1983 work is part of the same tradition but could not be independently verified here.

Kain (1993) tests Marx's own conceptual apparatus directly, asking whether his categories of alienated and unalienated labor can make sense of housework and childcare. Kain argues that Marx's distinctions allow housework to be understood simultaneously as a paradigm of unalienated labor and as a site of profound oppression — a tension read in sharply divergent ways by different feminist theorists, and one that bears on whether the forces/relations schema's silence about domestic labor is a contingent omission or a structural blind spot in Cohen's reconstruction. Hensman (2011) revisits the 1970s domestic-labor debate from an Indian perspective, arguing that the classical debate's frameworks require revision once informal-sector and Global South patterns of household and market labor are considered, extending the question of whether value-producing "production" can or should include domestic labor beyond its original Western-industrial context.

More recent contributions integrate these threads with broader feminist theory. Ferguson (2016) argues that social-reproduction feminism and intersectionality, often treated as competing paradigms, are complementary: social-reproduction theory supplies a structural account of how capitalism organizes the reproduction of labor-power, while intersectionality corrects that tradition's historical tendency to underplay race. Portella and Busk (2024) apply Marx's concepts of formal and real subsumption to the problem of patriarchy's transhistorical status, arguing that this framework can explain how patriarchy is reorganized and intensified by capitalist relations of production without treating it as either fully autonomous from capitalism (as dual/triple-systems theories hold) or as foundational to capitalism's origins.

Together, these feminist critiques converge on a claim that parallels Section 6's internal verdict: a schema built on historically specific, production-centered categories struggles to accommodate social relations — gendered, or class-conflictual — that are not reducible to material productive capacity, whether or not that schema is defended functionally, and whether the challenge comes from within the analytical tradition or from outside it.

## Conclusion

Taken together, the methodological, internal-Marxist, external, and autobiographical lines of evidence surveyed above converge on a qualified conclusion: the Primacy Thesis and its functionalist defense have been subjected to sustained pressure from nearly every direction, without any one critique proving decisive on its own. The functional-explanation debate (Section 3) shows this most clearly as an unresolved methodological contest — Wood, Schwartz, and Moggach's defenses of functional explanation against the individualist demand for microfoundations sit alongside Pettit's, Carling's, and Kincaid's more equivocal rehabilitations, so that the dispute has fragmented into a plurality of positions rather than settling in favor of functionalism or individualism. The internal-Marxist critique (Section 6) is more convergent: Levine and Wright's reversal of Cohen's explanatory direction, refined through Fisk's, Mills's, and Carling's typologies of what "primacy" could mean, is judged by Chibber's (2011) retrospective to have forced even the most sophisticated rescue attempts — Carling's evolutionary reading and Wright, Levine, and Sober's selection-based reconstruction — to concede so much to class struggle that they become, in substance, theories of class conflict rather than of technological determinism. Casal's (2020) feminist critique arrives independently at a similar verdict: extended to women's circumstances, Cohen's forces/relations schema fails to generate the predictions and periodizations it promises, suggesting that the schema's abstraction from social relations not reducible to material productive capacity is a recurring point of failure across otherwise unrelated critical traditions (Section 7).

Cohen's own trajectory corroborates this picture, though the corpus documents it only thinly. Tarrit (2015) and Roemer (2015) both describe Cohen's movement away from KMTH's functionalist ambitions and toward normative political philosophy — self-ownership, equality, Rawlsian justice — a shift that, on the available secondary evidence, looks less like an updated defense of the 1978 theses and more like their gradual displacement (Section 5).

Yet the theory's conceptual vocabulary has outlasted doubts about its substantive claims. Siermiński's (2025a; 2025b) recent readings of Marx's 1840s and 1850s writings continue to organize their analysis around Cohen's Development and Primacy Theses — one treating them as accurate to the young Marx's internally strained position, the other arguing the mature Marx reversed Cohen's forces-over-relations priority — demonstrating that Cohen's categories remain the standard idiom for this debate even among scholars who reject or complicate his conclusions. Carling's (2006) quarter-century retrospective had already anticipated this durability, judging the research program Cohen inaugurated still viable after the Elster-Roemer critiques, provided its defenders reckon with Marxism's own historical record rather than treating the theory of history as a purely abstract exercise.

Two questions remain genuinely open: whether any class-struggle-inflected reconstruction preserves enough of the original Primacy Thesis to count as its defense rather than its supersession (Chibber's skepticism notwithstanding), and whether Cohen's normative turn should be read as continuous with or a repudiation of the 1978 project — a question this review cannot resolve given how thinly Cohen's own self-revision texts are represented in the citable corpus. This review's contribution is accordingly a source-verified map of these debates, explicit about its own limits: the reception literature (Section 2) and the record of Cohen's self-revisions (Section 5) are documented only sparsely in citable form, while the functional-explanation debate (Section 3) and the class-struggle-primacy critique (Section 6) are documented densely enough to support confident synthesis.
