## Section 3: The Functional Explanation Debate

The most sustained methodological controversy provoked by *Karl Marx's Theory of History* concerns the explanatory form Cohen assigns to the Primacy Thesis. Cohen holds that economic structures obtain, and persist, because they are suited to developing the productive forces they contain — a functional, or "consequence," explanation modeled loosely on the logic of natural selection in evolutionary biology (Cohen 1982). This claim that historical materialism's central explanatory relation is functional rather than ordinary-causal became the target of a methodological-individualist challenge associated above all with Jon Elster, which demanded that any functional claim be underwritten by a specified mechanism linking a structure's beneficial consequences to its persistence. Because Elster's and Roemer's own founding statements of this challenge lack recoverable abstracts and are excluded from citation in this review, the debate is reconstructed here chiefly through how later, complete-metadata commentators — Wood, Schwartz, Moggach, and Wolff — characterize and respond to it. What emerges is not a settled verdict but a proliferation of rehabilitation strategies, such that the dispute evolves from a binary opposition between functionalism and individualism into a more pluralistic methodological landscape.

### 3.1 The Methodological-Individualist Critique, Reconstructed

Writing in an *Inquiry* symposium occasioned by Elster's *Making Sense of Marx*, Wood (1986) characterizes Elster's position as combining two claims: a concession that Marx's own particular deployments of functional explanation are often defective, together with a stronger, principled philosophical objection to functional and teleological explanation as such in social science. Wood (1986) argues that this stronger objection rests on a caricature of what functional and teleological explanation actually assert, so that even if Wood grants Elster's point about specific applications, the general case against the explanatory form Cohen employs is considerably weaker than Elster presents it. On Wood's characterization, then, Elster's critique conflates the soundness of particular functional explanations with the legitimacy of the explanatory schema itself.

Schwartz (1993) reconstructs the dispute in different terms, attributing to Elster a "mechanical" conception of explanation that requires an intelligible causal process linking cause and effect, which Elster deploys against Cohen's own "nomological" conception of functional explanation. Schwartz's (1993) argument is that Elster's mechanical standard, properly applied, actually vindicates rather than undermines functional explanation in social science, while Cohen's nomological account is what cannot adequately underwrite the functional claims historical materialism requires. Schwartz (1993) further characterizes Elster as objecting that functional explanation commits theorists to metaphysically bizarre collective subjects, and argues that this objection rests on an implausible, eliminativist reading of methodological individualism — one that need not be accepted even by philosophers otherwise sympathetic to individualist constraints on social explanation. The effect of Schwartz's intervention is to reframe the dispute as turning on which of Cohen's or Elster's own explanatory standards is more defensible, rather than on whether functional explanation is legitimate in the abstract.

Moggach (1991) approaches the critique from a different angle, arguing that Elster's methodological individualism rests on what Moggach calls a "monadic" conception of individual agents — abstracted from the relational and social framework of production within which, on Moggach's reading, Marx's theory situates them. On this characterization, Elster's rational-choice reconstruction cannot adequately capture the interactive, teleological character of social reproduction that Cohen's functionalism, however imperfectly, was attempting to model; the individualist reconstruction, Moggach (1991) contends, sacrifices explanatory resources rather than simply replacing them. Wolff (1990) offers a complementary assessment from outside the analytical Marxist circle, subjecting Elster's game-theoretic reconstruction of Marx to scrutiny and arguing that the formal apparatus of game theory and rational choice does not deliver the explanatory gains Elster claims over functionalist or structural readings, and that methodological individualism as a general constraint on social explanation is less self-evidently correct than Elster and Roemer assume. Taken together, these four complete-metadata sources converge on a common diagnostic: whatever the merits of Elster's and Roemer's demand for microfoundations, the demand as reconstructed by Wood, Schwartz, Moggach, and Wolff does not straightforwardly defeat Cohen's functionalism on its own terms.

### 3.2 Rehabilitating Functional Explanation

A second cluster of responses does not merely characterize the individualist critique but attempts to answer it on its own philosophical footing. Pettit (2000) examines what he calls the "empty black box" objection — the charge, central to the case against Cohen, that functional explanations are vacuous unless they specify the selecting mechanism — and argues that rational-choice explanation itself is frequently open to the identical objection, since appeals to agents' rationality often function as an unexplicated filtering assumption rather than a specified causal mechanism. Pettit's (2000) symmetry thesis turns the charge back onto its source: if functional explanation is suspect for lacking a mechanism, then much rational-choice explanation, including the individualist alternative proposed to supplant Cohen's functionalism, is vulnerable to the same worry.

Carling (2002) pursues a different rehabilitation strategy, reconstructing the debate as fundamentally about social evolution and arguing that Cohen's consequence explanations can be given a defensible selectionist reading, modeled on competitive selection among social forms, that would answer the mechanism objection without requiring full individualist microfoundations. This evolutionary-selection model supplies, on Carling's (2002) account, the kind of "elaboration" the individualist critique demands, while remaining continuous with Cohen's own biological analogy rather than replacing it with game-theoretic microfoundations. Kincaid (2002) situates the Cohen dispute within a broader taxonomy of functionalist explanation across the social sciences, distinguishing cases where functionalist reasoning succeeds from cases where it overreaches by positing self-correcting social equilibria without adequate evidence of the requisite feedback process; on this framework, whether Cohen's version of historical materialism counts as a successful or excessive use of functionalism becomes a case-by-case evidential question rather than one settled by a general philosophical verdict against the explanatory form. Together, Pettit, Carling, and Kincaid show that the "mechanism" demand central to the individualist critique is not uncontested even on its own philosophy-of-science terms.

### 3.3 Structuralist and Later Assessments

A further line of assessment approaches the dispute from outside the analytical Marxist camp altogether, or from a temporal distance that allows for retrospective stock-taking. Callinicos (2004) develops a structurationist account of the relation between agency and social structure and, in the course of doing so, criticizes Cohen's functional explanation of production relations for failing to specify how individual agency mediates between the developmental tendency of the productive forces and the class struggles that are supposed to bring compatible relations of production about; on Callinicos's (2004) reading, Elster's individualist alternative fares no better, since it substitutes reductive microfoundations for a genuine theory of structural constraint and enablement. Van Bouwel (2019) extends the mechanism question into contemporary philosophy of social science, examining the more recent "mechanism-based" turn in analytical sociology and arguing that the availability of social mechanisms does not by itself vindicate methodological individualism, since mechanisms can operate at, or link, multiple levels rather than bottoming out uniquely in individual-level facts — a conclusion that unsettles any simple equation of "mechanism" with "microfoundations" of the sort Elster's critique presupposed. Kumar (2008) offers a more deflationary assessment, arguing from a broadly pragmatist standpoint that the metaphysical stakes Elster and Cohen both attached to the functionalism/individualism dispute may be less important than pragmatic questions about which explanatory strategy proves fruitful for particular explanatory projects within Marxist social theory.

Carling (2006), writing roughly a quarter-century after the book's publication, provides an explicit retrospective judgment: the research program Cohen's functional-explanation apparatus inaugurated retains vitality despite the individualist critiques of Elster and Roemer, provided its contemporary defenders confront the historical fate of Marxism rather than treating the theory of history as a purely abstract methodological exercise. This retrospective assessment does not amount to a claim that the individualist challenge was refuted; rather, read alongside Pettit's symmetry thesis, Carling's selectionist rehabilitation, Kincaid's case-by-case taxonomy, and Van Bouwel's mechanism pluralism, it indicates that the functional-explanation debate settled into methodological plurality rather than resolution in either direction. This trajectory — from a binary confrontation between functionalism and individualism toward a landscape of selection-based, symmetry, and pragmatist positions — sets the stage for the fragmentation of Analytical Marxism as a movement, and for Carling's own typology of "primacy," both taken up in later sections.
