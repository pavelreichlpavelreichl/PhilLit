## Section 2: The Divine Law/Human Law Debate — Ethical Substance, Guilt, and Agency

The most heavily populated node in the secondary literature concerns the logical structure of the Antigone/Creon conflict and Hegel's claim, central to the *Phenomenology*'s Ethical Order chapter, that ethical action is inherently guilty. Two broad interpretive families organize this debate. "Reconciliation" or "systemic" readings treat the mutual destruction of Antigone and Creon as productive and necessary — a structural condition for the emergence of higher, more differentiated forms of recognition and self-conscious normativity, not a moral failure. "Retrospectivity" or "agency" readings instead foreground the action-theoretic puzzle the guilt-thesis poses: why should responsibility attach to agents independently of what they subjectively know or intend? These families are not strictly opposed — most retrospectivity readings presuppose some version of the reconciliation thesis — but they ask different questions of the same text, and a third strand asks whether either family's confidence in Hegel's resolution is warranted by Sophocles' play at all.

### 2.1 Reconciliation and Recognition Readings

Williams (1998) supplies the recognition-theoretic vocabulary much of this literature draws on or reacts against, reading the divine law/human law conflict as exposing a form of mutual recognition — between family and state, and between the particular individual and ethical substance — too immediate and undifferentiated to accommodate the individual, Antigone, whom it destroys. Greek ethical substance, on this account, cannot yet recognize its members as more than bearers of an unreflectively inherited role, which makes the collapse of Sittlichkeit necessary rather than contingent. Wenning (2019) restates this as "tragic recognition": Antigone and Creon achieve genuine, if fatal, recognition of each other's law only by first refusing, in action, to recognize its legitimacy, so that mutual destruction is the mechanism, not merely the byproduct, of recognition. Bisticas-Cocoves (2005) extends the reading temporally, tracing "ethical action" from the tragic one-sidedness of Antigone and Creon through the *Phenomenology*'s subsequent turn to comedy, where unreflective identification with a single ethical power is superseded by a consciousness no longer bound to it — tragedy alone, on this account, does not complete the movement the chapter stages. Tralau (2005) approaches the same mutual-guilt thesis from political theory rather than moral psychology, reading the "self-destruction" of Antigone's and Creon's respective laws as illustrating the structural instability of any political order unable to accommodate both principles at once. Pippin (2008) generalizes the reconciliation reading furthest, situating the guilt-thesis within a non-metaphysical theory of rational agency in which self-knowledge is always mediated by social recognition rather than transparent to the individual will in advance; Antigone's case becomes one instance of a general Hegelian claim that agents cannot fully anticipate or control the social meaning their deeds will acquire.

### 2.2 Guilt, Action, and Retrospectivity

A second cluster treats the guilt-thesis as a problem in the philosophy of action, asking how Hegel can hold agents responsible for consequences they neither intended nor foresaw. Speight (2001) supplies what has become the dominant Anglophone framework: ethical agency in the *Phenomenology* is fundamentally "retrospective," since agents discover the full meaning and guilt of their deeds only through the deed's reception and consequences, not in advance. Antigone's confrontation with Creon is paradigmatic of this structure because her action is at once fully hers — an exercise of self-certain will — and only fully determined, in its ethical significance, after the fact. Congdon (2008) traces how Hegel's concept of guilt (*Schuld*) develops across the "Spirit" chapter, from Antigone through the French Revolution to confession and forgiveness, distinguishing "abstract guilt," which produces only shame and punishment, from "determinate guilt," which is productive, indebting the guilty party to others and driving further ethical development; Antigone's guilt in violating one law while fulfilling another becomes the paradigm case of this second, structurally necessary form. Sandis (2010) supplies the technical vocabulary underlying both accounts, examining Hegel's distinction between *Tat* — the deed as an objective, publicly ascribable event — and *Handlung* — the deed as intentionally owned and known by the agent. Applied primarily to Oedipus but bearing directly on Antigone's more deliberate transgression, this distinction explains why ethical action is guilty regardless of an agent's subjective knowledge, a point Sandis argues commentators frequently mishandle by importing contemporary action-theoretic assumptions. Mudde (2009) reframes the structure around "risky subjectivity": Antigone's action is a self-certain deed undertaken in full awareness that it necessarily "trespasses" against one universal law even as it fulfills another, so that the universal character of trespass, not any individual failing, makes her guilt inescapable. Burke (2010) emphasizes a related feature, first-person authority — Antigone's self-certainty about what she is called to do is not undermined by, but constitutive of, the collision with Creon's equally self-certain human law. Orozco Garibay (2024) poses the sharpest version of the underlying question, arguing that Hegel's opposition of two duties, each compelling action and each transgressed by fulfilling the other, makes fate rather than culpable choice responsible for breaking ethical substance — raising directly whether such guilt is avoidable in principle or a "fatal," structurally unavoidable feature of finite ethical agency.

### 2.3 Appropriation, Distortion, or Rearticulation?

A third strand asks directly whether Hegel's reconciliatory schema is earned by Sophocles' text or imposed upon it. Van den Berge (2017) accepts Hegel's diagnosis that Antigone and Creon occupy positions equally incomplete and equally "divine," rejecting the standard view that the play stages a simple clash between divine and human law; but he departs from Hegel in denying that the play promises any higher synthesis reconciling the two positions, arguing instead that Sophocles teaches the unattainability of such harmonization — deploying Hegel's own analytic apparatus to reach an anti-reconciliatory conclusion Hegel himself resisted. Schmidt (2015) reaches a structurally similar skepticism by comparing Hegel's reading to Heidegger's: what Hegel treats as a necessary and ultimately productive catastrophe, the self-destruction of an ethical substance too immediate to sustain itself, is on Heidegger's alternative emphasis better understood as genuinely monstrous (*ungeheuer*) and without guaranteed recuperation into a higher configuration. Finlayson (1997) defends reconciliation directly against a skeptical challenge, responding to J. M. Bernstein's psychoanalytically inflected charge that Hegel's reading suffers from an "Antigone complex" papering over a properly tragic, non-recuperable remainder in the Sophoclean material — a reply that anticipates, within Hegel scholarship proper, the psychoanalytic and deconstructive critiques treated more fully later in this review. Taken together, these positions show the appropriation-versus-distortion question does not divide neatly along the reconciliation/retrospectivity line of 2.1–2.2: van den Berge and Schmidt reach anti-reconciliatory conclusions while working substantially within Hegel's own conceptual vocabulary, while Finlayson defends reconciliation against an explicitly external, psychoanalytic challenge.

### 2.4 Gender as Constitutive of Ethical Substance

A final question, bridging this section to the feminist reception literature treated next, is whether the gendering of divine and human law is incidental to, or constitutive of, the chapter's philosophical argument. Hoff (2006) argues against a simple assignment of women to a natural, ahistorical family sphere, contending that the chapter's dialectic exposes how Greek Sittlichkeit itself naturalizes gender difference in a way that the subsequent development of Spirit, and Hegel's own systematic account, ultimately criticizes rather than endorses — Antigone's exclusion is diagnosed by the text rather than affirmed by it. Farneth (2013) reaches a convergent conclusion from a religious-ethics standpoint: Hegel criticizes the ancient Greeks' understanding of both human law and divine law, and this critique, on her reading, supplies resources against essentialist approaches to sex and gender, since the tragedy of the Antigone/Creon conflict lies precisely in the fact that Greek Sittlichkeit's gendered identities and obligations are naturalized and withheld from scrutiny or revision — a condition Hegel's own dialectic exposes as untenable. Both readings locate an internal, gender-critical dimension within an otherwise reconciliation-oriented interpretation, treating gender as constitutive of the divine law/human law structure while resisting the conclusion that Hegel's own verdict on that structure is straightforwardly essentialist. Whether this internal-critique reading survives contact with the fuller feminist literature — beginning with Irigaray's sharper charge that Hegel's own text, not merely ancient Sittlichkeit, forecloses women's entry into the universal — is the question Section 3 takes up.

**Section Summary**: The literature converges on reading Hegel's guilt-thesis as structurally motivated — grounded in the *Tat*/*Handlung* distinction and the developmental logic of *Schuld* — rather than as a moralizing verdict on Antigone or Creon individually. It remains divided on whether the reconciliatory payoff Hegel claims for their collision is actually earned by his own text (Williams, Wenning, Pippin, Finlayson) or whether, even granting Hegel's diagnosis of the conflict's structure, the promised synthesis is unattainable on Sophocles' own terms (van den Berge, Schmidt) — a division that recurs, in different registers, throughout the sections that follow.
