## Section 1: Textual Foundations — Antigone Across Hegel's Corpus

Antigone does not appear in a single, self-contained argument in Hegel's work; she recurs across texts written in different registers, for different purposes, over roughly two decades. Establishing where and how she recurs — and where commentators disagree about the continuity of that recurrence — supplies the textual apparatus on which the rest of this review depends.

### 1.1 Primary Loci and Canonical Commentaries

The best-known statement occurs in the *Phenomenology of Spirit*'s "True Spirit: Ethical Life" section (Hegel 1977), where the unreflective ethical substance (Sittlichkeit) of the Greek polis is shown to divide necessarily into two equally justified "laws" — the divine law of kinship, embodied by Antigone, and the human law of the state, embodied by Creon — whose collision destroys both and propels Spirit toward "Legal Status," the Roman world of abstract personhood. The *Philosophy of Right* restates this division systematically rather than narratively: §166's Remark, invoking Sophocles' play by name, assigns masculinity to civil and political self-subsistence and femininity to the family's "substantial" ethical disposition (Hegel 1991). The *Aesthetics* recasts the same structure in explicitly aesthetic-theoretical terms, treating Sophoclean "collision" (Kollision) — two substantively justified ethical powers embodied in agents who identify wholly with one and thereby violate the other — as the paradigm of ancient, as opposed to modern, tragedy (Hegel 1975a). These three statements are complementary rather than redundant: the *Phenomenology* narrates a dialectical collapse, the *Philosophy of Right* fixes the resulting gender division within a systematic architecture, and the *Aesthetics* generalizes the collision-structure into a theory of the tragic genre as such.

Terminological and translational choices complicate comparison across these texts and their commentaries. Miller's *Phenomenology* translation and Knox's or Wood's *Philosophy of Right* do not render Hegel's vocabulary identically, and "ethical life" and "ethical order" are used somewhat interchangeably in the secondary literature to translate *Sittlichkeit*, a variability synthesis and commentary alike must track rather than treat as a substantive disagreement. The three standard English-language book-length commentaries on the *Phenomenology* — Pinkard's account of Sittlichkeit's collapse as a crisis in "the sociality of reason" that cannot yet accommodate self-conscious, reflective norm-giving (Pinkard 1994), Harris's paragraph-by-paragraph philological reading of the Ethical Order section as the first fully "actual" shape of Spirit (Harris 1997), and Hyppolite's mid-century French reading of the same section as staging a self-contradiction that must be superseded rather than preserved (Hyppolite 1974) — converge on treating the chapter's outcome as a necessary, non-contingent transition rather than a moral verdict on the participants. The Blackwell Guide's multi-author chapters largely confirm this convergence as representative of mainstream Anglophone scholarship (Westphal 2009). Kojève's recognition-centered lectures, by contrast, recast the same material around the struggle for recognition and the master-slave dialectic, reading Greek ethical life's collapse as an early, unequal stage en route to fully reciprocal recognition in the modern state (Kojève 1969); this recognition/desire-centered emphasis is a documented outlier relative to the Pinkard-Harris-Hyppolite convergence and should not be treated as representative of "the" standard reading.

### 1.2 Continuity, Genealogy, and the Limits of the Chapter

Close textual scholarship complicates any assumption that the *Phenomenology*'s tragedy discussion exhausts Hegel's verdict on Greek ethical life or that "Sittlichkeit" originates with Antigone at all. De Boer argues that the Ethical Order chapter's argument does not conclude with the tragic destruction dramatized through Antigone and Creon but continues through a comparatively neglected discussion of Greek comedy, echoing Aristophanes, which for Hegel marks a more thorough dissolution of immediate ethical substance than tragedy alone achieves (de Boer 2010). Hoff traces a parallel continuity forward rather than sideways: the unresolved tension Antigone embodies does not simply disappear once the *Phenomenology* moves to "Legal Status," but persists as a structuring remainder in the subsequent account of law, right, and ultimately mutual forgiveness among conscientious agents (Hoff 2006). Both arguments caution against treating the Antigone passage as a bounded set piece with a clean endpoint.

A second continuity runs backward. Molloy's reading of the 1802 Natural Law essay shows "ethical life" emerging as a technical category — opposed to both empiricist and Kantian-Fichtean formalist natural law — prior to any explicit engagement with Antigone, already containing conceptual resources the *Phenomenology* and *Philosophy of Right* later deploy (Molloy 2024; Hegel 1975b). The roughly contemporaneous *System of Ethical Life* manuscript develops stages of "absolute" and "relative" ethical life independently of any Antigone reference, forming a direct conceptual precursor to the *Phenomenology*'s chapter (Hegel 1979). On the systematic side, Katsman argues that the *Philosophy of Right*'s ethical-life architecture is structured as a syllogism, with family, civil society, and the state mediating universal, particular, and individual moments, against which Greek Sittlichkeit's immediate, non-syllogistic unity stands as historical antecedent (Katsman 2023); Moyar traces how individual right, developed through Abstract Right and Morality, is integrated into this "living" ethical order in a way that reworks, without simply repeating, the unreflective identification found in Greek Sittlichkeit (Moyar 2021). Together, these genealogical and forward-tracing arguments establish that the Antigone material is not a self-contained set piece but a node threaded through Hegel's system — a premise later sections, particularly those on the polis-to-empire narrative and on gender's constitutive status, must test rather than assume.

**Section Summary**: The primary-source spine — *Phenomenology*, *Philosophy of Right* §166, and *Aesthetics* — is well mapped and its commentaries largely convergent, with Kojève a documented exception. But scholars disagree about where the Antigone material's argumentative work actually ends (tragedy or comedy; the Ethical Order chapter or the later chapters on right and forgiveness) and about how far back its conceptual apparatus reaches, since "Sittlichkeit" has its own pre-Antigone genealogy in the Jena writings.
