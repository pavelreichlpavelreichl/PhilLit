## Section 7: Comparative Afterlives — Kierkegaard, Lacan, Žižek, Derrida, Steiner

Beyond Hegel scholarship proper, Sophocles' *Antigone* generates a distinct comparative-philosophical literature in which later thinkers take Hegel's dialectical resolution of the Antigone-Creon conflict as the target against which a rival logic is counterposed. Kierkegaard displaces Hegel's ethical substance with reflective, hereditary guilt; Lacan and Žižek relocate Antigone from recognition to desire and the death drive; Derrida deconstructs the family bond Hegel's reading depends on; and Steiner supplies the reception-historical frame within which all of these confrontations are situated. Read together, this literature treats Hegel's *Antigone* less as a settled interpretation than as a recurring occasion for post-Hegelian philosophy to define itself against dialectical reconciliation.

### 7.1 Kierkegaard's Modern Antigone

Kierkegaard's essay "The Tragic in Ancient Drama Reflected in the Tragic in Modern Drama" (in *Either/Or*) imagines a "modern Antigone" who, unlike Sophocles' heroine, possesses reflective knowledge of Oedipus's crime — a hereditary, subjective guilt that Jerr (2014) argues isolates her from the communal ethical substance in which Hegel locates Antigone's action. Where Hegel's Antigone acts from unreflective ethical certainty grounded in Sittlichkeit, Kierkegaard's figure is paralyzed by inherited knowledge she cannot share or resolve through recognition, and Jerr reads this modern Antigone's fate as tragic rather than redemptive: she sacrifices her own particularity in her very effort to embody the tragic ideal. Sestigiani (2006) extends this reading by treating Kierkegaard's "Danish" refiguration as evidence of a historical fracturing, not merely an individual psychological condition: modernity itself, on this view, has fragmented the conditions for the kind of ethical totality Hegel finds in Sophocles' play, so that Kierkegaard's isolated modern subject marks a genuinely post-Sittlichkeit condition rather than an idiosyncratic variation on it. Stevanović (2013) approaches the same pairing from a classicist angle, rereading both Hegel's and Kierkegaard's interpretations against research on Sophocles' text and its performance context in fifth-century Athenian theater, with particular attention to the political dimension of Antigone's mourning voice — a dimension Stevanović argues both philosophers, in their different ways, underplay in favor of treating Antigone's act as primarily ethical or psychological rather than political. Together these readings position Kierkegaard's counter-figure as a deliberate departure from Hegelian reconciliation whose own adequacy to Sophocles' text remains, in turn, contestable.

### 7.2 Lacan — Desire Beyond Ethical Substance

Lacan's *Seminar VII* relocates Antigone from the register of ethical substance and mutual recognition to that of desire and the death drive, and Cha (2016) offers a systematic side-by-side comparison of the two frameworks, contrasting Hegel's "ethics of goodness" — in which Creon's defense of the state's law carries legitimacy because individual conscience cannot stand apart from communal ethical life — with Lacan's "ethics of desire," in which Antigone becomes the tragedy's only true hero precisely because she moves beyond the good/evil distinction to realize a pure desire that "does not cede." Moyaert (2014) sharpens the textual stakes of this relocation by examining Antigone's disputed "extravagant" speech on the irreplaceability of the brother — the passage that famously perplexed Goethe — arguing that Lacan's use of this crux clarifies how his ethics diverges from both Kantian universalizability and Hegelian mediated recognition: genuine ethical sensibility, on Lacan's reading, requires a resistance to rational justification that Antigone's speech exemplifies rather than undermines. Sjöholm (2004) situates this Lacanian departure within a longer philosophical genealogy running from Hegel through Heidegger, Irigaray, and Butler, arguing that each of these thinkers' constructions of "feminine desire" via Antigone shares an underlying view of women as prone to excess or deficiency relative to ethical norms; Sjöholm's study thereby connects the psychoanalytic reworking of Hegel's Antigone to the gender-theoretic debates addressed elsewhere in this review, while keeping its own focus trained on desire rather than kinship or performativity as such. ### 7.3 Žižek and the Contested Hegelian-Lacanian Synthesis

Žižek's fusion of Hegel and Lacan is itself a live site of dispute rather than a settled extension of either thinker. In *The Most Sublime Hysteric* — his foundational statement of the Hegelian-Lacanian project, originally published in French in 1988 — Žižek (2014) argues against the standard image of Hegel as a theorist of totalizing abstraction, proposing instead a Hegel of unresolved negativity and revolutionary openness whose dialectic is structured by the same unanswerable questioning Lacan finds in hysteria. Dews (1995) mounts an early critique of this synthesis, questioning why a Lacanian framework should be considered the most promising route to unlocking Hegel's "secret" and probing the interpretive costs of insisting on convergence between the two thinkers even where Lacan's own pronouncements resist it. Applied specifically to the Antigone material, this dispute persists: Robertson (2012) contests Žižek's characterization of Antigone's desire as "diabolical," proposing instead that her act is better understood as "demonic" — a distinction Robertson argues better captures how her action problematizes the moral categories at work within rival strands of psychoanalytic thought, and which demonstrates that the Hegelian-Lacanian-Žižekian line is not monolithic. Swiffen (2010) extends the contestation into political theory by examining the dispute between Žižek and Butler over Lacan's concept of the real, arguing that their disagreement — played out partly through competing readings of Antigone's relation to law and ethics — reflects differing political commitments (Žižek's more revolutionary, universalist reading against Butler's more particularist, kinship-focused one) rather than a straightforward difference in interpretive correctness, a finding that connects this Lacanian dispute back to the feminist-reception debates over Butler's *Antigone's Claim*. ### 7.4 Derrida's Glas — Deconstructing the Family

Derrida's *Glas* reads Hegel's treatment of the family, and specifically the sister-brother bond through which Antigone achieves what Hegel calls "peaceful" recognition, as depending on exclusions that dialectical Aufhebung cannot fully absorb. Charen (2011) works through the textual mechanics of this claim, arguing that Hegel's *Phenomenology* first grants Antigone the achievement of full ethical recognition through her grasp of two contradictory laws, divine and communal, but then represses this speculative accomplishment through her subsequent disappearance from the text — a structure of elevation followed by erasure that Derrida identifies and interrogates in *Glas*. Kramer (2014) offers a technically precise account of how Derrida's general deconstructive apparatus, the quasi-transcendental, is worked out through this specific case, coining the term "Antigonanette" (drawing on Derrida's 1971–72 lecture course "La famille de Hegel") to describe how Antigone functions in Derrida's reading as neither fully inside nor outside Hegel's system. Chanter (2016) offers a critical assessment that goes beyond exposition of Derrida's reading to identify a gap within it: while Derrida interrogates the peaceful recognition Hegel attributes to Polynices and Antigone as brother and sister, he largely refrains from interrogating the relation between the master-slave dialectic and sexual difference directly, remaining silent on Hegel's twofold elision of the master-slave dialectic with the husband-wife relation and of Antigone with the figure of the wife — Chanter argues that by symbolically "marrying her off," Hegel subordinates Antigone to a symbolic husband in a move Derrida's own reading passes over. Baptist (2021) situates *Glas*'s treatment of Hegel against Derrida's earlier and parallel confrontation with Levinas, arguing that the "remainder" Derrida locates in Hegel's system is not a Levinasian ethical alterity but a more contingency-oriented category — a transgressive desire for contingency rather than eschatological infinity — clarifying how Derrida's deconstructive stance differs from adjacent Levinasian critiques of Hegel. ### 7.5 Steiner's Reception-Historical Frame

George Steiner's *Antigones* (1984) supplies the reception-historical frame within which the preceding four lines of reception can be situated, arguing that Sophocles' play — and above all its uptake by the German idealists, Hegel foremost among them — became the "pivot of consciousness" through which European thought reflected on itself. Steiner's claim that Antigone functions as philosophy's privileged self-reflective test case supplies the framing thesis invoked throughout this domain to explain why Kierkegaard, Lacan, Žižek, and Derrida each felt obliged to produce their own confrontation with Hegel's reading. Ponton (1991) tests one specific piece of this genealogy in a review-essay assessing Steiner's placement of Hegel relative to Aristotle, arguing against Steiner that Aristotle's discussion of Antigone in the *Rhetoric* already registers the conflict between an atemporal familial mode of being and the category of the historical, and that Hegel's *Lectures on the History of Philosophy* does not elevate Antigone above Socrates but rather uses Socrates to describe the hero who possesses "for himself the absolute right of deciding consciousness." Ponton's rejoinder thus qualifies, without abandoning, Steiner's broader claim about Hegel's centrality to the modern philosophical afterlife of the play. **Section summary.** Across all five lines of reception, post-Hegelian philosophy returns to Hegel's *Antigone* not primarily to extend it but to test whether its dialectical resolution is achieved, repressed, or merely displaced. Kierkegaard's reflective guilt, Lacan's desire beyond the good, and Derrida's undialectizable remainder each identify something Hegel's reconciliation is said to exclude or fail to absorb, while the internal disputes within the Žižekian synthesis (Dews, Robertson, Swiffen) show that even the most self-consciously Hegelian-Lacanian appropriations of Antigone remain contested on their own terms. Steiner's reception history frames this pattern as constitutive of Antigone's modern philosophical significance rather than incidental to it, making the comparative afterlife itself a continuous argument about the limits of Hegelian reconciliation — one that bears directly on how the earlier sections' internal Hegel-scholarship debates (Section 2's guilt and recognition readings, Section 3's feminist critiques) should be assessed against readings that reject the terms of Hegelian dialectic altogether.
