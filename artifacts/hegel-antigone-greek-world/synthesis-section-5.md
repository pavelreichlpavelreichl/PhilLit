## Section 4: Tragedy and the Tragic — Hegel's Aesthetic Theory and Its Rivals

Hegel's discussion of Sophocles in the *Aesthetics* is frequently summarized as a single thesis: tragedy stages a collision between one-sided but equally justified ethical powers, and reconciliation (*Versöhnung*) is achieved when the eternal justice underlying both is vindicated through the protagonists' downfall. The literature surveyed here complicates this summary along four axes: whether the theory can accommodate subjective suffering as well as objective collision; whether Antigone or another Sophoclean or Aeschylean play best exemplifies the theory; how the theory is positioned relative to Kant and to the broader post-Kantian discourse on "the tragic"; and how it fares against Nietzsche's rival account and against systematic critique from within Hegel scholarship itself.

### 4.1 The Technical Apparatus — Collision, Reconciliation, Experience

The standard reading of Hegel's tragic theory, as Peters (2011) reconstructs it, treats tragedy primarily as a conflict of objective ethical substances—paradigmatically the competing claims embodied in Antigone and Creon—resolved through a rationality that "underlies and drives" the conflict itself. Peters (2011) argues that this reading, however textually well-grounded in the *Lectures on Aesthetics*, leaves unanswered the question of whether Hegel's theory can account for tragic suffering as experienced by the protagonist. Rather than treating this as a fatal objection, Peters (2011) derives a complementary theory of tragic experience from the *Phenomenology of Spirit*, arguing that Hegel's account of ethical consciousness's self-relation supplies the missing subjective dimension without displacing the objective-collision model. This move is significant for the review's purposes because it directly anticipates the charge, developed later by Nietzschean and psychoanalytic critics, that Hegel's rationalism cannot accommodate suffering as such.

Finlayson (1999) undertakes a related but distinct technical reconstruction, focused on clarifying what "reconciliation" means in Hegel's theory and distinguishing it explicitly from Aristotelian catharsis. Having established this apparatus, Finlayson (1999) advances a claim with direct bearing on the status of Antigone within Hegel's own theory: he argues that Aeschylus' *Eumenides*, not Sophocles' *Antigone*, "better exemplifies" Hegel's model of collision resolved through reconciliation. On this reading, the *Eumenides*' explicit adjudication of the conflict between the Furies and Apollo by Athena provides a cleaner instance of higher reconciling authority than Antigone's death achieves. This claim reframes a question presupposed throughout earlier sections of this review—whether Antigone is paradigmatic or merely illustrative for Hegel's aesthetics—as itself contested within the tragedy-theory literature, independent of any external critique.

Cooper (2016) situates the collision-and-reconciliation apparatus within Hegel's break from Kant, arguing that existing scholarship on Hegel's tragic theory divides between those who see it as abandoning Kant's critical achievements for pre-critical metaphysics and those who see Hegel as allowing philosophy to be transformed by tragic experience. Cooper (2016) credits the latter camp's diagnosis of Hegel's critique of Kant's theoretical assumptions but argues it underestimates the practical significance of Kant's separation of knowledge and aesthetics, proposing instead that tragedy represents an action that is internally unified yet irreducible to theoretical explanation. Donougho (2006) extends the collision framework beyond the *Aesthetics* into the *Phenomenology*'s "art-religion" chapter, arguing that Hegel's tragic theory is misrepresented if confined to the ethical content addressed in chapter VI ("the tragic") without attention to the dramaturgical enactment addressed in chapter VII ("tragedy" as cultic performance). Falkenstern (2018) tests the general collision model against Oedipus rather than Antigone, finding that the conflict in *Oedipus the King* is not between two ethical spheres embodied in different individuals but between Oedipus's deeds and his later knowledge of them—a divergence that helps isolate what in Hegel's reading of Antigone is specific to that play against what belongs to his general theory of ancient tragic heroism.

### 4.2 German Idealist Context and the Genealogy of "the Tragic"

Billings (2014) situates Hegel's theory within the emergence of "the tragic" as a distinct philosophical category around 1800, arguing that post-Kantian writers—Schiller, the young Schelling, Hölderlin, and Hegel—turned to Greek tragedy, and to *Oedipus Tyrannus* and *Antigone* specifically, because it offered a historicized model of freedom responsive to debates opened by Kant's third *Critique*. Billings (2014) treats Hegel's *Phenomenology* and Hölderlin's "Notes" on Sophocles as the two most complex post-Kantian treatments of tragedy as a "figure" for historicity, positioning Hegel as one participant within a shared discourse rather than its culmination. Beaney (2009) extends this reception-historical context, examining Hegel and Hölderlin together as the era's most influential readers of Antigone and asking whether the period's fascination with the play reflected a broader cultural preoccupation with the "paradox" of aesthetic pleasure taken in tragic suffering, specifically female death. This gendered inflection of the paradox-of-tragic-pleasure question connects the aesthetic-theoretical literature to the feminist reception traced in Section 3.

### 4.3 Hegel versus Nietzsche, and Systematic Critique

Houlgate (1986) undertakes a sustained comparison of Hegel and Nietzsche centered on tragedy, arguing that Nietzsche's critique of metaphysics itself relies on a Life/Thought distinction that constitutes a metaphysical dualism of the kind Nietzsche claims to reject, whereas Hegel's dialectical treatment of alleged opposites as "defining components of a dynamic" offers what Houlgate (1986) presents as a more thoroughgoing critique of metaphysical dualism. On this account, Hegel's reconciliatory model is defended not merely as an alternative to Nietzsche's Dionysian affirmation of unresolved conflict but as philosophically more radical on Nietzsche's own terms. Galle (1993) frames the same comparison as a structural "disjunction" internal to the philosophy of the tragic, invoking Hegel's figure of Minerva's owl to characterize the relationship between the historical decline of tragic drama and the rise of philosophical theorizing about it, and positioning Hegel's dialectical reconciliation against Nietzsche's affirmation of unresolved Dionysian conflict as two genuinely incommensurable options rather than a simple succession.

Roche (1998) undertakes what he presents as the first sustained evaluation of Hegel's theory of tragedy and comedy together, working through collision, ethical substance, and reconciliation before developing an independent systematic theory of both genres partly through critique of Hegel's account (Roche 1998; entry drawn from publisher description, without independently verified abstract). The Alznauer collection (2021) extends this systematic engagement across Hegel's corpus, addressing not only literary and dramatic-form questions but the role tragedy and comedy play in Hegel's account of the history of society and religion, and represents, according to its own self-description, the current plural state of Anglophone scholarship on Hegel and tragedy rather than a single interpretive thesis (Alznauer 2021).

### 4.4 Toward Reception History

Leonard (2005) traces how post-war French thinkers—Lacan, Derrida, Foucault, Irigaray, and Vernant among them—turned to ancient Greek material, including tragedy and the polis, to formulate new accounts of political subjectivity, extending the German-idealist habit of reading Greek tragedy as a resource for thinking the political into its twentieth-century French, structuralist and post-structuralist afterlife. Van den Berge (2017) returns the discussion to Antigone specifically, accepting Hegel's diagnosis that Antigone's and Creon's positions are each incomplete—denying elements of law and justice no less essential to the other—while explicitly rejecting Hegel's further claim that the play promises resolution into Sittlichkeit; on Van den Berge's reading, the play instead teaches that such harmonization is unattainable. This qualified acceptance-and-rejection of Hegel's schema anticipates the more systematic classicist and philosophical objections examined in Section 6, and the psychoanalytic and deconstructive displacements of Hegel's reconciliation thesis examined in Section 7.

**Section Summary**: Hegel's theory of tragedy is more internally contested than a monolithic "reconciliation thesis" suggests. Commentators disagree on whether Antigone or the *Eumenides* best exemplifies the theory (Finlayson), whether it can accommodate subjective suffering without supplementation (Peters), how it should be positioned relative to Kant's separation of knowledge and aesthetics (Cooper), and how it fares against Nietzsche's rival account of tragic affirmation (Houlgate, Galle)—debates that set the terms for the classicist and comparative-reception literatures addressed in the sections that follow.
