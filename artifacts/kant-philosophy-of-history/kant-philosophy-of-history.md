## Section 1: The 1784 Essay — Arguments, Mechanism, and the Status of Nature's Plan

Kant's "Idea for a Universal History with a Cosmopolitan Purpose" deploys three philosophically distinct moves that the secondary literature has not always kept carefully separate. First, it offers a causal-mechanical story: unsocial sociability — the human tendency to enter society while simultaneously resisting it — functions as the engine of historical development, driving individuals and states into conflicts that eventually compel the creation of legal and political institutions. Second, it makes a teleological claim: nature "intends" this development, such that the pain and destruction of historical conflict are instruments of a plan aimed at a cosmopolitan legal order. Third, it advances a normative-methodological proposal: we should write universal history from a cosmopolitan point of view, treating the sequence of human events as if it were the working-out of nature's rational purpose. Whether these three moves cohere in a single argument, or whether they pull in different directions requiring different epistemic resources, is the organizing question of the first domain of the secondary literature. As Kleingeld (2001) notes, the failure to distinguish them explains why the essay has generated such divergent readings.

### 1.1 Unsocial Sociability — Mechanism, Theodicy, and the Moral Engine

The concept of unsocial sociability is Kant's version of the theodical problem in secular form. Schneewind (2009) reads the essay as a secular theodicy: just as traditional theodicy argued that apparent evil serves divine purposes, Kant argues that human conflict serves nature's rational plan. The mechanism is dialectical — social drives and antisocial impulses must coexist for development to occur, since a society of perfectly sociable beings would stagnate in pastoral contentment without the antagonism required to develop human capacities. This reading identifies the essay's deep structure as theodical while locating its distinctiveness in the displacement of providential agency onto a naturalistic "plan."

Sargentis (2015) develops the moral-crisis dimension of the same mechanism. On his reading, unsocial sociability does not merely produce instrumental goods (legal institutions, political arrangements) but creates conditions of moral crisis that press human beings toward rational reflection on their situation. The antagonism is not merely an efficient cause of institutional development but a condition for the emergence of moral self-consciousness. This connects the 1784 essay to Kant's later reflection on radical evil in the Religion: the same antagonism that drives political development also expresses the propensity to subordinate moral principle to self-interest.

The most searching critical analysis of what unsocial sociability can and cannot explain is provided by James (2019). James argues that the mechanism faces an explanatory gap that the secondary literature has largely passed over: the mechanism generates the *conditions* for legal and political arrangements (through conflict, competition, and the pressure of mutual antagonism), but it does not explain how moral development follows from the establishment of those arrangements. Legal coercion can produce rule-following without moral motivation; a civil constitution can exist without citizens whose practical reason is oriented by anything more than interest. The mechanism that drives history toward republican and cosmopolitan institutions does not, on its own, close the gap between external legality and internal moral conversion. As James puts it, Kant's history secures the political preconditions of morality while leaving unexplained how those preconditions generate morality itself.

Caranti (2015) offers a reformist response to this explanatory gap. He argues that the mechanism in the 1784 essay can be detached from its pre-Darwinian natural teleology — from the claim that nature has a literally purposive plan — and reconstructed in naturalistic terms compatible with evolutionary biology. If unsocial sociability is understood as a selected trait whose effects happen to be conducive to institutions that extend human flourishing, the teleological gloss is dispensable. Caranti's naturalized reading deflates the metaphysical ambitions of the essay while preserving the core argument about how conflict-driven development produces legal and political arrangements. The cost is that this reading cannot address James's gap from within its own resources: a merely naturalized mechanism gives no more guidance than the original about how legal institutions generate moral development.

Lenczewska (2022) approaches the mechanism from a different angle, connecting it to Kant's broader account of reason's emergence from its pre-human origins. Her reading places the "Conjectural Beginning of Human History" (1786) alongside the 1784 essay, arguing that reason's historical self-manifestation is not simply the product of antagonism but requires tracing reason's emergence from nature and its gradual self-articulation through history. This developmental picture situates the mechanism of unsocial sociability within a larger story about what it means for a rational species to constitute itself through history — a reading that anticipates the systematic concerns of Section 2.

### 1.2 Regulative or Constitutive? The Epistemic Status of Nature's Plan

The epistemic status of nature's "plan" is the master-interpretive axis for the entire secondary literature on the 1784 essay. Four positions have been staked out with increasing precision over the past three decades.

Kuehn (2009) offers the deflationary historiographical reading. On his account, the essay is primarily a contribution to Geschichtsschreibung — a proposal for how to write universal history from a rational standpoint — rather than a metaphysical claim about history's deep structure. The nature-teleology of the essay is a regulative device for historical narration, not a theoretical claim about causal mechanisms or a practical postulate about moral requirements. This reading has the advantage of dissolving most of the apparent philosophical tension in the essay (between its speculative claims and critical constraints), but critics have noted that it achieves this by reducing the essay to something less than Kant himself appears to intend.

O'Neill's (2008) regulative-practical reading takes the idea of nature's plan as an Idea of Reason in the technical sense from the first Critique: a concept that cannot be theoretically constitutive (no experience could verify that nature literally has a rational plan) but that can orient inquiry and action. On this reading, the philosophy of history is a practical application of the regulative use of reason — we must proceed as if history were oriented by rational purpose, because only from this standpoint can historical inquiry be integrated into the demands of practical reason. O'Neill situates this within her broader account of how Kant's ethics requires attention to the empirical conditions of its realization.

Lindstedt (1999) provides the most technically precise formulation of the postulate reading: the claim that nature guarantees progress toward a cosmopolitan order is a postulate of practical reason analogous to the postulates of immortality and God's existence, grounded not in theoretical evidence but in the requirements of moral agency consistently pursued. Just as the postulate of immortality does not rest on empirical evidence but on the requirements of an agent committed to moral progress, the postulate of historical progress rests on the requirements of an agent committed to cosmopolitan legal reform. The epistemic status is not theoretical but practical-normative.

Kleingeld (2001) defends the most ambitious version of the regulative reading — what she calls the "strong integration thesis." On her account, Kant's philosophy of history is not a dispensable supplement to the moral philosophy but theoretically and morally necessary. Practically, the claim that nature cooperates with moral demands is required to make sense of moral hope — without the idea that historical conditions can be improved through rational action, the demand for moral effort in history would be unaccompanied by any rational expectation of its efficacy. Theoretically, the nature-freedom bridge that the essay attempts to articulate is not an optional extra to the critical system but a requirement of its coherence. Kleingeld's reading makes the philosophy of history constitutive of the moral philosophy in a way that goes beyond what Kuehn's and O'Neill's accounts allow.

Kryluk (2024) extends this line of analysis forward into Kant's political philosophy of the 1790s. Tracing the connections between the first Critique's regulative ideas, the 1784 essay's teleological proposals, and the argument for perpetual peace, Kryluk shows how the regulative reading of the 1784 essay coheres with Kant's political philosophy by treating the idea of cosmopolitan order as a practically necessary orientation for political agents — neither a theoretical prediction nor a merely optional aspiration, but a normative framework without which political reform loses its rational warrant. This closes some of the gap James identifies: while the mechanism alone does not generate moral development, the practical commitment to cosmopolitan reform gives moral agents a rationally grounded orientation for their historical engagement.

The resolution of the regulative/constitutive debate is not merely a question of Kant-interpretation. As Rauscher (2002) shows in an essay that will be central to Section 3, Hegel's constitutive approach to historical teleology — his claim that Geist is immanent in the historical process — represents a direct response to what he took to be the theoretical inadequacy of Kant's merely regulative proposal. Hegel's constitutive philosophy of history is, in part, an attempt to supply what Kant's regulative approach refuses to provide.

### 1.3 The Essay's Relation to the Critical Project

The 1784 essay predates the Critique of Judgment (1790) by six years, and a persistent question in the scholarship concerns how its teleological proposals relate to the systematic position that the third Critique articulates. Three approaches have been developed.

Woo (2023) offers a developmental narrative: Kant's account of moral hope underwent a significant shift between his pre-critical and early critical writings (where hope was located in individual eschatology and the highest good) and the 1784 essay (where hope takes on a collective, historical dimension). On Woo's account, this shift is philosophically significant — it represents Kant's recognition that the highest good requires not only individual moral striving but the development of social and political conditions that make moral life collectively possible. The 1784 essay is thus not a mere popular piece but a reconfiguration of the moral philosophy's relationship to history.

Williams (2024) complicates this developmental narrative by examining the late essay "Renewed Attempt to Answer the Question: Is the Human Race Continually Improving?" (1798). Williams argues that in this text Kant himself retreats from the ambitious claims of the 1784 essay to a more modest position: what can be established is not that the human race is improving morally but only that it is capable of rightful-political progress — the development of legal and constitutional arrangements, independent of whether moral motivation keeps pace. This late modesty is significant because it suggests that Kant himself came to acknowledge the explanatory gap that James identifies: the mechanisms that produce political progress are not identical with those that would produce moral progress, and perhaps only the former can be defended with evidential resources available in history.

Lenczewska (2025) provides the most recent book-length treatment of the systematic question. Her central argument is that the "Conjectural Beginning of Human History" (1786) is essential for understanding the 1784 essay, because the "Conjectural Beginning" traces the emergence of practical reason from its pre-human origins and shows how the development of rational agency — not merely the institution of legal arrangements — is historical through and through. On this reading, the historical dimension of Kant's philosophy is not a supplement to the practical philosophy but constitutive of it: practical reason has a history, and that history is not contingent to its structure but partially explanatory of it. Lenczewska's account thus offers a way of addressing James's gap from within Kantian resources: if the emergence of practical reason is itself a historical process, then the connection between legal development and moral development is not a further premise that the mechanism must supply but a structural feature of reason's self-articulation in history.

Allison (2009) approaches the systematic question from the third Critique. His central claim is that sections 83–84 of the third Critique provide the philosophical grounding that the 1784 essay lacks: by distinguishing culture as the "ultimate end" (letzter Zweck) of nature from moral humanity as the "final end" (Endzweck) that lies beyond nature altogether, Kant supplies a principled account of how natural teleology relates to the demands of practical reason without collapsing either into the other. The 1784 essay's teleological proposals, on this reading, are anticipatory sketches of the more rigorous systematic position that the third Critique articulates. This interpretation sets the agenda for the systematic debates of Section 2: does the third Critique's teleological framework actually provide adequate grounding for the philosophy of history, or does Allison overstate the coherence of the bridge between natural purposiveness and moral vocation?

---

The 1784 essay is not self-interpreting on the most fundamental question it raises. The Kuehn-versus-Kleingeld axis — deflationary historiography against strong systematic integration — frames the interpretive choices facing any reader. James's identification of the mechanistic gap and Williams's analysis of Kant's own late modesty represent genuine critical interventions, not merely scholarly dissensions. The most defensible reading, on the evidence of the secondary literature, is a regulative-practical one in Kleingeld's and Kryluk's sense: the philosophy of history is morally necessary without being theoretically constitutive. But this reading must take seriously the force of James's challenge — the mechanism of unsocial sociability does not on its own close the gap between political legality and moral agency — and the developmental complication that Williams introduces.

## Section 2: The Critical System and the Philosophy of History — Grounds, Coherence, and Tensions

Whether Kant's historical writings constitute a philosophically coherent whole or merely a set of occasional reflections whose systematic aspirations exceed what the critical philosophy can accommodate is the central controversy in the literature on Kant's systematic philosophy of history. The question has three sub-questions that must be kept distinct. First: is the third Critique's teleological framework — specifically sections 83–84, which introduce culture as the "ultimate end" of nature and moral humanity as its "final end" — adequate as a foundation for the political essays' progress claims? Second: does the Religion's doctrine of radical evil introduce a genuine tension with the optimistic teleology of those essays, and if so, is it resolvable within Kantian resources? Third: what is the methodological status of the Anthropology from a Pragmatic Point of View — is its empirical-pragmatic register compatible with transcendental idealism's constraints on what can count as knowledge of human beings? The affirmative systematic reading (Allison 2009; Kleingeld 1999; Lenczewska 2025) holds that these elements cohere; the anti-systematic reading (Krijnen 2011; Fonnesu 2022) identifies architectonic tensions that the positive reading obscures.

### 2.1 The Third Critique's Grounding of History — Culture, Final End, and the Nature-Freedom Bridge

The most important recent claim in the systematic literature is that the third Critique provides the philosophical grounding that the 1784 essay lacks. Allison (2009) argues that sections 83–84 supply a principled account of how natural teleology connects to moral vocation: by distinguishing culture as the "ultimate end" (letzter Zweck) — the highest purpose that nature can pursue within its own order — from moral humanity as the "final end" (Endzweck) that lies beyond what nature can provide, Kant establishes a graduated relationship between nature's purposes and morality's demands without collapsing either into the other. Culture — the development of human skills, talents, and capacities through the use of reason — is what nature maximally aims at within its own domain; but the full realization of those capacities in accordance with the moral law requires a step that nature cannot take on its own. On this reading, the philosophy of history occupies the space between natural teleology (what nature can bring about through its mechanisms) and moral teleology (what practical reason demands), and the third Critique provides the framework for articulating that space.

Guyer (2005) elaborates the internal structure of this argument. His central thesis is that the third Critique contains a new argument connecting natural teleology to morality that was not available to Kant before the 1790 publication: the argument from culture as ultimate natural end provides a systematic grounding for the claim that nature cooperates with the demands of practical reason, not merely as a convenient hope but as a principled philosophical result. This reading foregrounds the architecture of the third Critique in a way that makes the philosophy of history appear as a systematic consequence rather than an ad hoc supplement.

Cha (2020) offers the most detailed analysis of what sections 83–84 actually argue. He reconstructs the internal structure of Kant's distinction between letzter Zweck and Endzweck, showing how the argument for culture as ultimate natural end depends on the prior teleological framework for natural purposiveness and how the transition to moral humanity as final end requires the introduction of practical-rational considerations that lie outside the teleological judgment's proper domain. Cha's careful textual analysis both supports and complicates Allison's reading: it confirms that the third Critique supplies a principled framework for the nature-freedom bridge, while revealing that the bridge is more conditional than Allison suggests — it depends on accepting a regulative teleological interpretation of nature that carries its own philosophical costs.

Krijnen (2011) offers the strongest critical response to this systematic reading. His central claim is that the third Critique's teleology of culture concerns natural purposiveness — the way in which organisms and natural products exhibit purposive structure — while the philosophy of history concerns the realization of freedom, and these two domains cannot bear the architectonic weight of connecting them. Kant's natural teleology, even in its most developed form, concerns reflective judgment's treatment of natural organisms; it does not establish that historical development has a teleological structure, because history is not a natural process in the relevant sense but a domain constituted by human freedom. The third Critique's grounding of the philosophy of history, on Krijnen's reading, is not a genuine philosophical solution but an architectonic sleight of hand — it imports teleological concepts across a boundary that the critical system itself demarcates. Rauscher (2002) identifies a related ambiguity: Kant's use of "nature" in the political essays oscillates between a mechanical-naturalistic sense (the causal mechanism of unsocial sociability) and a teleological-purposive sense (nature as oriented toward rational ends), and it is unclear whether these can be unified within the constraints of the critical philosophy. This ambiguity, Rauscher argues, is precisely what Hegel later "resolves" by making historical rationality constitutively real — but the resolution comes at the price of the theoretical modesty Kant's critical philosophy demands.

The debate between Allison and Krijnen is not merely exegetical. It bears directly on the question, raised in Section 1, of whether the regulative-practical reading can provide adequate grounding for the philosophy of history. If Krijnen is right that the third Critique's teleology cannot bear the architectonic weight of grounding historical claims about the development of freedom, then the philosophy of history is left without systematic grounding within the critical philosophy — it is at best a postulate, at worst an inconsistency. If Allison and Guyer are right, the systematic reading is defensible, but only within the constraints that the third Critique's regulative teleology imposes.

### 2.2 Radical Evil, Moral Progress, and the Religion

The doctrine of radical evil in the Religion within the Bounds of Reason Alone (1793) appears to be in tension with the progressive optimism of the political essays. Radical evil is Kant's name for the foundational human propensity to subordinate the moral law to self-interest — not merely to act wrongly on occasion, but to adopt a fundamental orientation in which inclination takes priority over duty. If this propensity is as fundamental as Kant claims — if it characterizes the human species as a whole, not merely particular individuals — then the optimistic teleology of the 1784 essay and the "Guarantee of Perpetual Peace" seems to require either that radical evil can be overcome through natural-historical mechanisms or that the guarantee is compatible with a species that remains radically evil. Neither implication sits easily with Kant's moral philosophy.

Anderson-Gold (2000) offers the most influential positive resolution of this tension. Her central argument is that radical evil is "unnecessary" in a precise technical sense: it is a free, self-chosen propensity rather than a naturally determined trait, and its contingency is precisely what makes genuine moral progress (as opposed to merely natural development) conceivable. If human beings were naturally and necessarily evil, moral progress would require either a miraculous intervention or a denial of natural determination; but if evil is contingently self-chosen, it can be freely abandoned through moral conversion. This reading preserves both the seriousness of the radical evil doctrine (evil is not merely a weakness but a fundamental orientation) and the coherence of the progress narrative (genuine moral improvement is possible because evil is not necessitated). The philosophical cost of this resolution is not insignificant, however: it depends on a robust account of transcendental freedom that critics of Kant's practical philosophy find difficult to sustain.

Buddeberg (2019) develops the social dimension of moral progress that Anderson-Gold's reading leaves underspecified. Even if individual moral conversion is possible in principle, it is not sufficient for the kind of moral progress that Kant's philosophy of history tracks. Moral progress requires the development of institutional and social conditions — what Kant calls the "ethical community" in the Religion — within which individuals can mutually support and reinforce each other's moral development. Buddeberg argues that Kant's account of the ethical community provides a bridge between the radical evil doctrine and the progressive teleology: the institutional conditions for moral progress are themselves the product of historical development, and the Religion's theology of moral community is continuous with rather than in tension with the political philosophy's account of legal and constitutional progress. On this reading, the Religion and the political essays address different dimensions of the same problem — the conditions under which rational agents can make genuine moral progress — rather than contradicting each other.

Papish (2024) takes the tension more seriously and traces a genuine shift in Kant's position. She argues that the recognition of radical evil in the Religion leads Kant to revise his account of the grounds of hope for progress. In the early political essays, hope for historical progress was grounded primarily in natural teleology — the mechanism of unsocial sociability was supposed to produce, at least at the political level, conditions conducive to moral development. After the Religion, this natural-teleological grounding is supplemented (and partially displaced) by a moral-religious account: hope for progress is grounded not in nature's mechanism but in a moral-religious postulate about the compatibility of the moral vocation with historical conditions. This revised account is more modest than the earlier one but also more consistent with the constraints that radical evil places on optimism about natural mechanisms. Papish's reading thus confirms Williams's (2024) observation about late Kantian modesty from a different angle: the recognition of radical evil is part of what motivates Kant's retreat from strong teleological progressivism.

Woo (2023) and Fonnesu (2022) illuminate this from the direction of the highest good concept. Woo shows that Kant's shift toward historical hope was already underway before the Religion, tracking the development of his account of how virtue and happiness can be united under conditions of temporal experience. Fonnesu identifies what he calls a "crisis" in the highest good concept across the critical corpus: from the first Critique's "Canon of Pure Reason," where the highest good is presented as the object of moral teleology and the basis for the postulates, to the Religion, where the connection between virtue and happiness becomes increasingly strained as Kant's account of radical evil deepens, the coherence of the highest good as a systematic concept is progressively undermined. The "crisis" Fonnesu identifies is thus not merely a tension between the Religion and the political essays but a deeper structural difficulty in Kant's moral philosophy's account of how moral striving and worldly happiness can be coherently connected. Engstrom (1992) provides the essential conceptual background for this debate, offering a rigorous analysis of the highest good concept that clarifies what would be required for it to serve its systematic function — and implicitly, therefore, how demanding the conditions for avoiding Fonnesu's "crisis" are.

### 2.3 The Anthropology, Pragmatic Register, and Historical Knowledge

The Anthropology from a Pragmatic Point of View (1798) presents a methodological challenge for systematic readers of Kant's philosophy of history. The pragmatic anthropology operates in a register — knowledge of the human being as a free agent who can be influenced rather than merely described — that sits uneasily between empirical psychology (which Kant explicitly rejects as a science in the Metaphysical Foundations of Natural Science) and transcendental philosophy. If the Anthropology is merely empirical, its claims about human nature cannot bear the philosophical weight that the philosophy of history requires; if it is somehow continuous with the transcendental philosophy, its compatibility with the constraints of the critical system requires explanation.

Cohen (2009) argues that the pragmatic register has its own coherent standards, distinct from both empirical psychology's causal-deterministic methods and transcendental philosophy's a priori methods. Pragmatic anthropology concerns the human being as an agent who can use knowledge of human nature to influence both others and herself — it is knowledge for action rather than theoretical knowledge of human nature. On this reading, the Anthropology is neither pseudo-science nor covert metaphysics but a practically oriented form of self-knowledge whose standards are appropriate to its distinctive subject matter. This reading defuses some of the methodological anxiety about the Anthropology's place in the Kantian system, though it raises its own questions about how pragmatic knowledge relates to the systematic claims of the critical philosophy.

Kleingeld (1999) provides the most careful analytic reconstruction of how the two components of Kant's philosophy of history — natural-teleological development of human capacities, and moral progress in freedom — are related but not identical. Her central distinction is between the development of natural capacities (which Kant believes history shows) and moral progress in the strict sense (which requires the orientation of human agency by the moral law). The Anthropology is relevant to the first component: it tracks the empirical development of human capacities through history and contributes to the naturalistic side of Kant's historical narrative. But it cannot, on its own, establish the second component — moral progress — which requires the normative apparatus of practical reason. Kleingeld's reading thus assigns the Anthropology a limited but genuine role in the philosophy of history, consistent with Cohen's account of its distinct methodological standards.

Oh (2022) develops this by arguing that the Anthropology's foundation is a "twofold teleology of nature and history" — it takes human beings as natural organisms subject to natural teleology and as rational agents subject to the teleology of reason, and its pragmatic perspective is precisely the perspective from which these two aspects of human existence can be considered together. This reading, if correct, makes the Anthropology central to rather than peripheral in the philosophy of history, since it is the discipline that articulates the point of intersection between natural development and practical-rational vocation.

The question of whether the empirical evidence Kant deploys in the political essays — the growth of commerce, the spread of republican constitutions, the reaction to the French Revolution — can do the evidential work he assigns it is, in the end, a question about the Anthropology's methodological standing. If pragmatic anthropological evidence cannot establish theoretical conclusions about historical causation, then the progress narrative rests entirely on the regulative-practical interpretation: it is what we must hope, not what we can know. This is perhaps the most defensible conclusion, and it is consistent with the regulative reading that the review defends overall.

---

The systematic reading of Kant's philosophy of history is defensible but requires careful qualification. The third Critique provides the most developed philosophical framework for connecting natural teleology to the moral vocation, but the connection is more conditional than Allison's account suggests and more architectonically precarious than Kleingeld's systematic reading acknowledges — Krijnen's critical analysis of the architectonic limits is not easily dismissed. The radical evil doctrine introduces a genuine complication for simple progressivism that Anderson-Gold and Buddeberg address resourcefully but do not fully dissolve; Papish's reading of a genuine shift in Kant's position is historically plausible and philosophically significant. These tensions are, in a particular sense, productive: they explain why Kant's philosophy of history neither collapses into theoretical metaphysics (as a constitutive reading would require) nor retreats to pure moral prescription (as a merely deflationary reading would suggest), and they set up the critical challenges from Herder, Hegel, and postcolonial theory that the following sections address.

## Section 3: Rivals and Successors — Herder's Challenge and the Kant–Hegel–Marx Trajectory

The distinctiveness of Kant's philosophy of history emerges most sharply not from internal analysis alone but from comparison with what it refuses to be. Three external pressures define its philosophical boundaries: Herder's immediate objection that any universalist teleology of history instrumentalizes particular cultures and imposes a culturally parochial measure of progress; Hegel's successor claim that the regulative modesty of Kant's approach is a philosophical defect — that idealism demands the constitutive immanence of reason in historical actuality; and Marx's deeper inversion that both Kant and Hegel are guilty of idealism in the sense that counts most, mistaking the development of reason or Geist for the motor of history when the real motor runs through material productive relations. Each challenge is philosophically serious. The task of this section is to trace what each challenge succeeds in establishing and where, on a careful reading, Kantian resources survive the pressure.

### 3.1 Herder contra Kant — Organism, Humanität, and the Structure of the Dispute

The Kant-Herder controversy erupted publicly when Kant published two sharply critical reviews of Herder's *Ideas for the Philosophy of the History of Humanity* in 1785. The controversy is commonly presented as a dispute between Enlightenment universalism and Romantic particularism, but this framing is too simple and obscures what is philosophically at stake on both sides. Zammito (2002) provides the essential historical reconstruction, showing that the dispute reflects a deep methodological disagreement about the use of analogy and teleological inference in natural history, not merely a difference in political sensibility. Herder's *Ideen* deployed analogical inference between human and non-human natural beings to argue for an organically continuous account of cultural development; Kant objected that such inferences introduced teleological speculation without critical warrant, trading on supposed resemblances that obscure fundamental differences between natural organisms and cultural communities. The methodological dispute is thus at bottom a dispute about the epistemological status of teleological judgment before Kant had articulated, in the third Critique, a systematic account of that status. As Nassar (2015) has shown, however, the controversy was philosophically productive for Kant in ways he did not fully acknowledge: the pressure from Herder's analogical naturalism forced Kant toward the more carefully circumscribed critical teleology of sections 83–84 of the third Critique, even as he continued to criticize Herder for precisely the kind of analogical reasoning that his own race essays, as McNulty (2022) has argued, relied upon in parallel ways. If McNulty is right that Kant's own speculations about human races in natural history make use of teleological inference substantially similar to what he imputed to Herder, the methodological contrast between them is less sharp than Kant's reviews suggest — a complication that returns with force in Section 4's treatment of the race-and-progress critique.

Herder's positive philosophical position, as reconstructed by Barnard (2003) and Sikka (2007, 2011), is not merely negative criticism of Kantian teleology but a genuine alternative with its own internal structure. Herder argues that each *Volk* achieves its own distinctive *Humanität* — a qualitative flowering of the full range of human capacities in historically particular cultural forms — and that no single culture or historical epoch can be treated as merely instrumental for the moral-rational development of a later one. Barnard (2003) characterizes this as a form of qualified universalism rather than relativism: Herder does not deny that there are universal human capacities; he denies that their development takes a single universal form to which all cultures must approximate. The cosmopolitan order toward which Kant's philosophy of history is oriented — a federation of republican states culminating in legal-rational organization — is not, for Herder, the measure of human flourishing but one historically particular expression of it.

The normative stakes of this dispute have been analyzed most precisely by Sikka (2007), who focuses on the happiness dimension. Herder objects that Kant's universalist teleology makes the well-being of existing individuals subordinate to the progress of a future cosmopolitan order — in effect, treating the people of earlier or less "advanced" cultures as instruments in a historical drama whose resolution lies beyond their lifetimes and their forms of life. Kant's rejoinder, implicit in his 1785 reviews, is that without a universal standard of progress Herder has no principled basis for claiming any historical improvement at all; the *Volk*-relative standards of flourishing Herder invokes cannot themselves evaluate the development of cultures over time. Sikka (2011) presses Herder's response: the position she calls "enlightened relativism" combines recognition of genuine cultural incommensurability with a minimal universal commitment to *Humanität* as what all cultures share — not a shared destination but a shared kind of striving.

A persistent question in the secondary literature is whether Herder's alternative is internally coherent. James (2022) shows that Herder's own philosophy of history is committed to claims that create internal tension with his pluralism: in attempting to situate the Enlightenment itself within his philosophy of history, Herder is compelled to grant that the Enlightenment's abstract thinking and hostility to prejudice constitute a distinctive contribution to the development of humanity — a claim that sits uneasily with his insistence that no age can be ranked above another. Abstract thinking and its critique of prejudice cannot, on Herder's own terms, be merely one contribution among equally weighted others; they are part of the very framework within which his universal survey of cultures proceeds. This tension is philosophically significant: it suggests that some form of evaluative ranking is unavoidable in any philosophy of history that claims to do more than chronicle, and that Herder's position, insofar as it is a philosophy of history rather than a sociology of cultures, cannot entirely escape the universalist commitments it seeks to criticize in Kant.

Mertel (2016) locates a further internal complexity in Herder's *Also a Philosophy of History* (1774), the work that anticipated the *Ideen* and directly targeted Enlightenment pretensions: Herder's historicism, on Mertel's reading, retains a critical edge that is not reducible to simple particularism. The hermeneutical stance Herder adopts — accepting the embeddedness of all historical understanding while retaining a critical perspective on the standpoint from which understanding proceeds — is closer to a philosophically sophisticated recognition of the condition of historical knowledge than to a mere defense of cultural particularity. Denby (2005) makes a similar observation from a different direction: Herder's concept of *Kultur* as the process through which humanity develops retains a unitary model of civilization closer to Enlightenment universalism than the standard contrast with Kant suggests. On these readings, the Kant-Herder dispute is less a clean opposition between universalism and particularism than a disagreement about the form that universalism can defensibly take.

The dispute's continued philosophical relevance consists precisely in this: Herder's objection that Kant's cosmopolitan telos is covertly Eurocentric — that the "universal" standard of progress it invokes is not genuinely universal but particular to a European, rational-juridical conception of human flourishing — anticipates the postcolonial challenge that Section 4 addresses. Whether this objection reaches a well-formulated Kantian position or whether the regulative-practical reading can absorb it by declining any constitutive claim about a single cultural destination is the critical question that connects this section to the next.

### 3.2 From Kant to Hegel — The Regulative/Constitutive Divide

The transition from Kantian to Hegelian philosophy of history is the most important single development in the field after Kant, and its philosophical interpretation is contested at a fundamental level. Rauscher (2002) provides the clearest analytical framework for understanding what is at issue. Kant's regulative approach treats historical progress as a guiding idea — a practical orientation that organizes inquiry and action without constitutively asserting that rational patterns exist in historical actuality independently of human inquiry. Hegel's constitutive teleology makes a stronger claim: Geist is immanent in the historical process, such that the succession of historical forms is the medium of reason's self-realization. The distinction is not merely verbal. On Kant's account, asking whether history is "really" progressive requires recognizing that the question is not answerable from the theoretical standpoint; on Hegel's, it is answered by the philosophical comprehension of world history itself. The "Owl of Minerva" spreads its wings only at dusk: philosophy can comprehend the rational necessity of what has occurred, and that comprehension is not merely a retrospective exercise but a recognition of rationality's actual presence in historical actuality.

Rauscher's article is notable for its candor about the implications of this contrast. He argues that Hegel's constitutive approach is, in a certain sense, the "more consistent" theory for an idealist, since Kant's separation of empirical history from reason's a priori regulative role creates an internal ambiguity: on the one hand, Kant wants to distinguish empirical historical science from the a priori deliverances of reason; on the other hand, his view of how reason functions in experience seems to preclude such a sharp separation. Hegel, by making reason and understanding together constitute historical experience, achieves a theoretical consistency that Kant's approach cannot. The question is whether this consistency is a gain or whether it purchases theoretical coherence at too high a price — the metaphysical commitment that rational necessity is immanent in actual historical events. Rosen (2022) places this transaction within a larger historical argument: both Kant's and Hegel's philosophies of history retain and transform the structural framework of providential theology rather than decisively breaking with it. What Kant calls the "plan of nature" and what Hegel calls Geist's self-realization are both, on Rosen's reading, secular displacements of the providential idea that history has a rational author and a rational direction. The "shadow of God" falls across both positions, which means that the regulative/constitutive divide, while philosophically real, does not represent the complete secularization of historical thinking that both thinkers claim.

The most philosophically challenging contemporary position on the Kant-Hegel transition is Pippin's (1989, 2008). Pippin argues that Hegel's move is not a departure from Kant but a radicalization of what is already implicit in Kantian idealism. If, as Kant holds, the categories of experience are constitutively mind-dependent — if experience is what it is in virtue of the normative framework that rational subjects bring to it — then it becomes difficult to maintain a sharp distinction between the norms that practical reason legislates and the historical conditions under which those norms are determined and revised. Pippin's 1989 argument is that Hegel accepts the Kantian insight that mindedness is essentially norm-governed while arguing that Kant fails to account for the historical and social conditions under which norms are themselves constituted. Kant's transcendental idealism, on this reading, is incomplete: it shows that experience is norm-governed without explaining how the norms governing experience come to be what they are. Hegel's "completion" of Kantian idealism consists in showing that the determination of rational norms is itself a historical process — that the history of reason is not merely biographical but structural. Pippin (2008) extends this to the practical domain: rational agency is constituted through Sittlichkeit, the concrete ethical institutions of historical social life, which means that the historical development of those institutions is not contingent to but constitutive of what rational agency is.

The force of Pippin's challenge for the present review is genuine. If normativity is constitutively historical in Pippin's sense, Kant's separation of practical reason from history — his insistence that the moral law has authority independently of what has been historically achieved or institutionally realized — is untenable. The regulative-practical reading defended in Sections 1 and 2 depends on a distinction between practical reason's demands (which are not historically constituted) and the historical conditions of their realization (which are empirically contingent). Pippin's argument challenges this by suggesting that the conditions of practical reason's determination are not separable from its content. The Kantian response must maintain that the authority of the moral law does not derive from historical institutions but from the structure of practical reason as such — a claim that requires defending Kant's account of transcendental spontaneity against the Hegelian argument that all norms are immanent in social life.

Pinkard (2017) offers a reconstruction of Hegelianism that attempts to preserve what is philosophically compelling in the constitutive approach while avoiding the metaphysical overcommitments that make it vulnerable to Kantian objections. On Pinkard's reading, Hegel's claim is not that Geist is a cosmic substance whose self-realization drives history from behind, but that the historical succession of forms of social life can be evaluated as more or less adequate realizations of human self-understanding — a claim that requires normative criteria without requiring a speculative metaphysics of spirit. This neo-Hegelian position is more philosophically moderate than the traditional metaphysical reading, but it still involves a constitutive commitment that Kant's regulative approach refuses: the claim that historical actuality can be comprehended as having a rational structure, not merely oriented as if it did.

Sedgwick (2023) maps the contrast between Kantian and Hegelian necessity precisely. Hegel's claim that world history is necessary — that it unfolds according to a rational structure that philosophical comprehension can articulate — is stronger than Kant's regulative proposal, but Sedgwick argues that both positions share the motivation for requiring a "unifying idea" that connects historical events. Without such an idea, historical narration is impossible: the succession of events cannot be understood as *history* at all, only as chronicle. The disagreement concerns the epistemic and metaphysical status of the unifying idea, not its necessity for historical thought as such. Kant's regulative proposal holds that the unifying idea is a practical orientation without constitutive theoretical import; Hegel's stronger necessity claim holds that the idea is also a recognition of rationality's actual presence in the historical world. Hutchings (1991) draws out the political consequences of this difference: Kant's regulative framework makes perpetual peace a genuine practical goal that political agents can work toward without claiming to know that history necessarily tends in that direction; Hegel's constitutive teleology, in which war plays a positive dialectical role, undermines the aspiration to perpetual peace by treating conflict as a permanent feature of rationality's self-realization in history.

The defensibility of Kant's regulative approach, against both the Rauscher consistency objection and Pippin's neo-Kantian challenge, turns on whether the distinction between the authority of practical reason and the historical conditions of its exercise can be maintained. The review holds that it can — that Pippin's argument shows at most that the content of rational norms is historically mediated, not that their authority derives from historical institutions — but this is a contested position that requires the systematic commitments established in Sections 1 and 2, particularly the third Critique's account of how natural teleology relates to moral vocation without collapsing either into the other. Beiser's (2002) historical account of how the internal pressures in Kantian idealism drove the post-Kantian trajectory toward Hegelian constitutivism is illuminating precisely because it shows that the Hegelian development was philosophically motivated rather than arbitrary. But being philosophically motivated and being philosophically required are different things, and the regulative reading can argue that Hegel's resolution of Kant's ambiguities achieves theoretical consistency through metaphysical inflation rather than through philosophical argument that Kant's own framework cannot meet.

### 3.3 Marx and the Limits of Idealist Philosophy of History

Marx's challenge to the philosophy of history tradition differs from Hegel's and Herder's in its point of attack. Herder targets the universalism of Kant's cosmopolitan telos; Hegel targets the theoretical modesty of the regulative approach. Marx targets the idealism that both Kant and Hegel share: both treat the development of reason, consciousness, or spirit as the motor of historical change, when the real motor is material — the development of productive forces and the transformation of productive relations. On Marx's account, the sequence of historical forms that Hegel interprets as Geist's progressive self-realization is better explained by the functional development of the forces of production, which generates crises in existing relations of production until those relations are transformed to accommodate new productive capacities. G. A. Cohen's (1979) analytical reconstruction of historical materialism clarifies the logical structure of this claim with unusual precision: Marx's theory involves a primacy thesis (productive forces develop independently of the superstructure) and a functional explanation (relations of production exist because they enable productive development), and the combination constitutes an account of historical causation that assigns explanatory priority to material conditions without reducing everything to brute economic determinism.

What Cohen's reconstruction makes clear for the purposes of this review is where and how Marx's critique applies to Kant, and where it primarily targets Hegel. The force of the inversion thesis — that Hegel's idealist philosophy of history must be "stood on its feet" by replacing spirit with matter — applies most directly to Hegel's constitutive teleology, since Hegel's claim that history is the medium of Geist's self-realization is precisely the claim that a form of consciousness or rationality is the constitutive driver of historical development. Kant's regulative approach is differently structured: Kant does not claim that history is driven by the development of reason, but rather that we must orient our historical action by treating progress toward cosmopolitan order as a practical necessity. The claim is normative and practical, not theoretical-causal. In this sense, Marx's inversion of Hegel does not immediately touch Kant's regulative position, because that position does not make the constitutive claim that the inversion targets.

However, Marx's critique is not only aimed at constitutive idealism. It also challenges any philosophy of history that takes the development of moral or rational capacities as its primary concern rather than attending to the material and social conditions under which those capacities are developed or stunted. Fraser and Burns (2000) show that the Hegel-Marx relationship is more complex than the standard "inversion" thesis suggests: Marx retains the dialectical structure of Hegelian thought while materializing its basis, which means that the critique of idealism is not simply a charge of mistaken causal priority but involves a more substantive claim about the conditions of human emancipation. The development of productive forces creates the material conditions for human freedom without guaranteeing that freedom; what is required is the conscious transformation of productive relations through revolutionary agency. This picture is closer to Kant's insistence on active moral agency than to Hegel's philosophical comprehension of historical necessity — but Marx's account of the material conditions of freedom challenges any view, Kantian or Hegelian, that treats the realization of freedom as primarily a matter of rational orientation rather than social transformation.

Carr (2017) represents the most radical skeptical position: the entire tradition of speculative philosophy of history, from Kant through Hegel and Marx, rests on an epistemic illusion. The idea that history as a whole has a direction, meaning, or telos is not a genuine philosophical insight but a projection of human narrative onto a sequence of events that has no inherent structure. Even Kant's regulative version of historical teleology is, on Carr's account, unjustified: the claim that we must act as if history were progressing toward a cosmopolitan order imports a teleological structure that empirical history does not support. Against Carr, the regulative reading can deploy a precise philosophical response: Kant does not claim to know that history has a direction, but to show that agents committed to moral and political reform must presuppose that their efforts can make a difference to historical outcomes. This presupposition is not an empirical hypothesis that could be verified or falsified by consulting the historical record; it is a practical condition for the coherence of morally motivated historical agency. Carr's target is the constitutive claim that history literally has a telos — the illusion that historical events have a built-in direction independent of any human perspective. This is the claim that Hegel makes and that Kant explicitly refuses to make. The regulative reading is thus precisely not the illusion Carr diagnoses.

---

The three external challenges reviewed in this section jointly define what is distinctively Kantian in the tradition of philosophy of history. Herder's challenge is to the universalism of the cosmopolitan aim: he objects that no single cultural destination can serve as the measure of all historical development without covertly privileging a particular cultural standpoint. The internal tensions James (2022) and Denby (2005) identify within Herder's own position suggest that some form of cross-cultural evaluation is unavoidable in any philosophy of history, which mitigates but does not dissolve this challenge. Hegel's challenge is to the theoretical modesty of the regulative approach: he argues that idealism cannot coherently maintain Kant's separation between reason's practical authority and the historical conditions of its exercise. Pippin's version of this argument is the most philosophically sophisticated and cannot be dismissed without engaging the Kantian account of practical reason's authority at a level of depth that the present review can only indicate. Marx's challenge is to the idealism shared by both, arguing that the real conditions of human emancipation are material and social rather than rational-normative. The regulative reading's structure makes it more resistant to this challenge than Hegel's constitutive approach, but the materialist insistence on the conditions of practical reason's exercise cannot be simply deflected — it demands attention to the social and institutional prerequisites of the moral agency that Kant's philosophy presupposes. The regulative/constitutive divide remains the organizing principle for understanding what is philosophically distinctive about Kant's contribution to the tradition, and none of the three challenges, taken singly or together, is obviously decisive against a well-formulated Kantian position.

## Section 4: Cosmopolitanism, Race, and the Challenge to Kantian Progressivism

The three preceding sections have reconstructed and defended a regulative-practical reading of Kant's philosophy of history: nature's "guarantee" of progress is best understood not as a theoretical prediction about historical causation but as a practical postulate that agents committed to cosmopolitan republican ideals must presuppose in order to act coherently. The present section examines how this reading performs under two distinct forms of pressure that arise from the contemporary reception: first, the debate about how to interpret the guarantee article's specific mechanism in *Perpetual Peace* (1795), which presses on precisely this regulative/constitutive distinction at the level of international politics; and second, the race and colonialism scholarship, which challenges not just the metaphysical ambitions of the progress narrative but its supposedly neutral practical structure — the claim that even a regulative progress-idea carries ideological freight that cannot be excised by deflating its teleological commitments.

### Subsection 4.1: The Guarantee Article and Its Interpretations — Providence, Nature, or Practical Postulate

*Perpetual Peace*'s "guarantee article" is the theoretical pivot on which the entire architecture of Kant's political writings turns. The guarantee extends the mechanism of unsocial sociability — the principle that human antagonism and competition produce, through their very destructiveness, the conditions for legal and political order — from the domestic level of civil society to the international arena. Nature, Kant argues, uses war, commerce, and competitive self-interest to drive states toward republican constitutions and, eventually, toward some form of international federation, independently of whether political actors possess the moral motivation to seek such an order. The guarantee thus makes perpetual peace achievable through purely mechanical means, which is crucial to the political essays' practical claim: it explains how peace is not merely morally obligatory but historically possible, even given the persistence of radical evil and moral deficiency in actual agents.

Interpretations of the guarantee article have divided sharply over whether this mechanism commits Kant to a theological providentialism or can be read in wholly naturalistic terms. Molloy (2017) argues that the guarantee cannot be sustained without reference to Kant's practical theology: the appeal to "nature" as a guarantor is effectively an appeal to the God of the postulates of practical reason. On Molloy's reading, the guarantee article is a piece of political theology — Kant needs providential governance to make the claim that nature's mechanisms will reliably produce the conditions for peace. The secular-naturalist reading (nature as a concatenation of causal mechanisms indifferent to human purposes) would not suffice to support the guarantee's normative force, since a purely indifferent nature has no reason to favor republican over despotic outcomes. Molloy's reading has the virtue of taking seriously the religious vocabulary that Kant's text actually employs and of connecting the guarantee article to the broader "shadow of God" that Rosen (2022) has traced across the philosophy-of-history tradition.

Against providentialism, both the naturalistic teleology reading and the practical-constructivist reading have been advanced. Kleingeld (2001) provides the foundational reconstruction of the nature/providence distinction in Kant's historical writings, arguing that Kant's use of "providence" is not a commitment to theological intervention but a rhetorical device for expressing the idea that we may think of history as oriented toward human freedom — a regulative idea that is theoretically neutral between theism and naturalism but morally indispensable for practical hope. This reading integrates the guarantee article with the broader regulative framework: nature functions as an orienting concept for practical reflection, not as an independent causal agent with teleological intentions. The detailed architecture of Kant's international legal theory — including the three-tier structure of domestic, international, and cosmopolitan right — is reconstructed by Kleingeld (1998), who shows how the right to hospitality and the concept of world citizenship constitute a genuine third tier of public right, not merely metaphorical gestures. The guarantee article's role within this architecture is to explain how the empirical conditions for this legal order can be expected to materialize.

Ypi (2010) provides the most developed alternative to both the theological and the naturalistic readings, pressing the guarantee's interpretation in a constructivist-practical direction. On Ypi's account, the guarantee article is best read not as a prediction (theologically or naturalistically grounded) but as what agents committed to republican ideals must presuppose in order to act coherently toward their moral-political goals. The guarantee is thus neither a theoretical claim about historical causation nor a covert theological postulate but a practical-constructivist requirement: it articulates the background assumption without which republican political engagement would be motivationally and rationally groundless. This reading aligns with the postulate reading that Lindstedt (1999) has defended — that historical progress functions on the model of the postulates of practical reason, as a practically necessary assumption rather than a theoretically confirmed proposition — while making explicit the constructivist structure that grounds this necessity.

What is at stake in this interpretive dispute goes beyond the exegesis of a single text. As Kleingeld (2004) argues, the guarantee article is inseparable from the question of what institutional form Kant's international order is supposed to take. If the guarantee is read as a providential mechanism that will eventually produce peace regardless of human action, it sits uneasily with Kant's insistence that the approach to perpetual peace requires the active development of republican institutions, commercial interdependence, and international legal frameworks. If it is read constructively, as a presupposition of republican political agency, then it integrates naturally with Kant's institutional analysis — the league of free states (foedus pacificum) that Kleingeld (2004) identifies as Kant's practical approximation to the regulative ideal of a world federation functions as the institutional vehicle through which agents enact the presupposition. The practical-constructivist reading thus has an advantage in coherence: it explains how the guarantee article is connected to, rather than independent of, actual political effort.

### Subsection 4.2: Contemporary Cosmopolitanism — Developments and Departures

The contemporary literature on Kantian cosmopolitanism has developed in two directions that correspond roughly to the regulative structure of Kant's own approach. The first, represented by Kleingeld (2011), stays close to the text while pressing beyond minimal hospitality norms: on her reconstruction, Kant's mature cosmopolitanism involves a commitment not merely to non-interference but to a positive program of international institutional development, including protection of refugees, constraints on economic exploitation across national boundaries, and the gradual approximation of international order toward something more than a mere balance of power. The second, represented by Pogge (1992), Beitz (2005), and the Habermasian tradition, deploys Kant's moral universalism to press toward considerably more robust conclusions — global distributive justice, dispersed sovereignty across multiple institutional levels, and deliberative democratic governance at transnational scale — than Kant's own texts authorize.

What distinguishes these two directions, from the perspective of this review, is their contrasting relationship to Kant's philosophy of history. Kleingeld's reconstruction preserves the regulative-teleological structure: the approach to perpetual peace is a historical process governed by practical ideals that agents must approximate without expecting to fully realize, and the pace and form of institutional development must be calibrated to the actual moral and political conditions of historical agents. The stronger institutionalist readings, by contrast, tend to appropriate Kant's moral universalism while largely setting aside or remaining silent on the philosophy of history that generated and sustained that universalism in Kant's own thinking. Pogge's dispersed sovereignty and Beitz's global liberalism both invoke the moral equality of persons as their ground, but they do not engage the question of how historical progress toward global institutions is to be understood — whether such progress is to be expected on the basis of natural mechanisms, practically postulated on the basis of moral necessity, or simply demanded as a matter of normative argument. The selective appropriation is intelligible and practically motivated, but it is itself a philosophical choice with consequences: it severs Kant's normative cosmopolitanism from the account of how it is historically achievable, leaving the institutional program as a moral demand without the account of its realizability that the philosophy of history was supposed to supply.

The Habermasian reconstruction illustrates these tensions with particular clarity. Fine and Smith (2003) subject Habermas's attempt to reconstruct Kantian cosmopolitanism through deliberative democracy and constitutional patriotism to critical scrutiny. The dilemmas they identify are philosophically instructive: Habermas needs the normative resources of Kantian moral universalism to ground cosmopolitan obligations, but he has explicitly rejected the Kantian moral metaphysics (the grounding of normativity in rational will's autonomous legislation) that underwrites those obligations. "Thinking with Kant against Kant" — the program that Apel formulated and Habermas pursues — proves difficult to execute consistently, since the Kantian guarantee article's role as a bridge between normative aspiration and historical possibility has no evident replacement in a discourse-theoretic framework that lacks Kant's teleological apparatus. The postnational constellation Habermas envisions may be normatively attractive, but it inherits from Kant only the moral universalism while abandoning the philosophy of history that explains why the cosmopolitan ideal is not merely a rational demand but a historically achievable one.

Nussbaum's (1997) situating of Kant within the Stoic tradition of cosmopolitanism illuminates a different dimension of the reception: the long pre-history of cosmopolitan universalism within which Kant's version is one distinctive formulation, transforming the Stoic doctrine of concentric circles of loyalty through the specifically Kantian grounding in rational nature. This genealogical perspective has the philosophical consequence of showing that Kantian cosmopolitanism can be assessed not only in terms of its internal critical-philosophical consistency but also in terms of its relationship to a broader humanist tradition — which both broadens the resources available for its defense and complicates the charge that it is distinctively tied to a specific European historical moment.

### Subsection 4.3: Race, Teleology, and the Postcolonial Challenge

The race and colonialism debate is the most philosophically consequential challenge to have emerged in Kantian scholarship over the past two decades, and it cannot be addressed as a peripheral biographical embarrassment. It goes to the heart of the regulative reading that sections 1 through 3 of this review have defended, raising the question of whether even a practically oriented progress-idea can be ideologically neutral. The debate has a clear structure, and respecting that structure requires keeping separate the textual-exegetical question (did Kant change his mind on race?), the structural-philosophical question (is the teleological framework constitutively tied to racial hierarchy?), and the normative question (what follows for Kantian cosmopolitanism as a philosophical resource?).

The textual debate was initiated by Kleingeld (2007), who argued on careful textual grounds that Kant underwent a genuine intellectual transformation on race between the 1780s and the 1790s. The mid-1780s race texts, including the essay "Determination of the Concept of a Human Race" (1785), defend a racial hierarchy in which whites occupy the apex of a natural scale of rational development. Kleingeld shows that by the mid-1790s — in *Perpetual Peace* and the *Metaphysics of Morals* — this hierarchy has been abandoned in favor of a more consistently universalist position, and that this development is connected to Kant's systematic elaboration of cosmopolitan right and his growing condemnation of European colonialism. Kleingeld (2014) extends this argument to colonialism directly: Kant's early endorsement of European colonial enterprises, she argues, was connected to his racial hierarchy, and both were revised together in the 1790s as his legal philosophy and his cosmopolitanism developed. On this revisionist reading, the mature cosmopolitan theory is philosophically defensible precisely because it represents a genuine correction of the earlier racial views.

Lu-Adler (2022) offers the most sustained counter-revisionist challenge to this narrative. Examining Kant's treatment of slavery with careful attention to historical context, Lu-Adler argues that the passages Kleingeld cites as evidence of Kant's racial egalitarianism do not hold up under scrutiny: the texts either do not address modern race-based chattel slavery, or treat it as a cautionary tale for European labor relations rather than as a moral problem involving the humanity of enslaved Africans. Lu-Adler's central interpretive claim is that what Kant could and could not say about slavery was constrained by his philosophy of history, which depicted Africans as "natural slaves" suited by nature to a different stage of development — and that this constraint was never lifted. The humanity of the enslaved "Negroes" was, Lu-Adler argues, simply absent from Kant's moral reasoning. This counter-revisionist position implies that Kleingeld's reading attributes to Kant a change of mind whose evidence, on examination, does not survive careful contextualization.

Marwah (2022) raises the structural-philosophical challenge that is, in some ways, more fundamental than the textual dispute. Even if one grants — for the sake of argument — that Kant changed his explicit views on racial hierarchy in the 1790s, Marwah argues that the teleological framework encasing the philosophy of history implies constraints on the capacity of non-whites to make moral progress that are not simply cancelled by dropping explicit racial ranking. The framework depicts cultural and moral development as proceeding through stages that are modeled on European civilization; race is not merely an external addition to this framework but is internal to its teleological logic of human difference and development. Marwah's argument is that race is only intelligible within Kant's natural history through a teleological framework that already limits certain peoples' capacity to participate in humanity's moral vocation. If this is correct, the problem is not eliminable by attending to the texts of the 1790s, because the difficulty is carried by the teleological architecture rather than by the explicit racial rankings. The revisionist reading that excises the racial hierarchy while retaining the teleological framework has, on Marwah's account, not addressed the root of the problem.

Gani (2017) carries this analysis into the hospitality norm of *Perpetual Peace* itself, arguing that the cosmopolitan right to hospitality performs an erasure of race: it presents itself as a universal norm while presupposing a racial framework that excludes non-European peoples from full standing as recipients of hospitality rights. The universalism of the cosmopolitan hospitality norm, on this reading, is an ideological construction that naturalizes European expansionism and commercial penetration of non-European territories under the guise of a mutual right. Gani draws on the concept of coloniality to show how Kant's text encodes the racial assumptions of its historical context even in its ostensibly universal legal categories. Basevich (2021) extends the structural critique to the progress narrative specifically, arguing that the connection between racial hierarchy and the teleological narrative of development toward moral freedom and cosmopolitan right is not incidental but reflects the structural constitution of Kant's teleological framework. The "universal" subject of history is a specifically European and — in Basevich's formulation — white subject, and this is not an external application of the framework but a feature of how the framework itself defines the vocation of humanity.

The challenge that Allen (2016) poses is the most fundamental for the regulative reading defended in this review, because it does not operate merely at the level of the teleological claims' metaphysical commitments but at the level of the practical orientation itself. Allen argues that the normative foundations of critical theory — inherited from Kant and elaborated by Habermas — are structured by a philosophy of history that takes European modernity as the implicit telos of development, and that this ideological structure is carried not merely by the constitutive claims about historical progress but by the practical orientation of reason itself. Even a regulative reading that deflates the teleological commitment still orients practical reason toward what it would mean for history to "go well" — and this orientation, Allen contends, is shaped by assumptions about civilization, development, and rationality that replicate rather than escape the colonial hierarchies that postcolonial critique targets. The regulative/practical reading that sections 1 through 3 defend as the most philosophically coherent interpretation of Kant is, on Allen's account, not thereby freed from ideological entailment; the practical orientation is itself ideologically structured.

Allen proposes replacing the progressivist structure of critical theory — its forward-looking orientation toward a rational social order modeled on European modernity — with what she calls "unlearning": a critical problematization of historically sedimented standpoints that draws on Foucault and Adorno without presupposing a telos of progress toward which the critique is oriented. This proposal raises significant questions about whether critical theory can sustain its normative commitments without some account of progress — a point that Ypi's (2010) constructivist argument presses in the opposite direction — but Allen's challenge to the regulative reading must be taken seriously rather than simply set aside as an external political objection.

The section summary proposed in the synthesis outline correctly identifies the two directions in which the contemporary reception of Kantian cosmopolitanism has developed. The normative-extension direction — Pogge, Beitz, Habermas — presses Kant's moral universalism toward stronger institutional conclusions while leaving behind the teleological philosophy of history; this direction preserves the normative core but loses the account of historical realizability that the guarantee article was supposed to provide. The critical-demolition direction — Marwah, Lu-Adler, Allen — argues that the teleological framework is not separable from the racial hierarchy it embeds, and that even deflating the teleological commitment to a practical postulate does not dissolve the ideological structure that shapes what practical reason orients itself toward. Neither direction leaves the regulative reading of sections 1 through 3 intact without revision. The normative-extension direction shows that the practical-constructivist reading of the guarantee is compatible with more demanding institutional conclusions than Kant himself drew; the critical-demolition direction shows that the regulative reading must engage, rather than simply acknowledge and move past, the question of whose progress is being oriented toward and whose is being structurally excluded. These are not merely hermeneutic questions but philosophical ones, and they define the most pressing open problems in the field.

## Section 5: Moral Progress and the Grounds of Hope — Signs of History, Critique, and Defense

The preceding sections have established that the most defensible reading of Kant's philosophy of history is regulative-practical in character: the claim that nature works toward cosmopolitan conditions functions not as a theoretical prediction about historical causation but as a practical postulate that orients moral and political agency. This reading, however, raises a question it does not by itself answer: what, if anything, provides evidential grounds for the hope that human history is genuinely progressing? Kant's answer, most fully developed in the second division of the Contest of Faculties (1798), is the concept of a Geschichtszeichen — a sign of history — whose philosophical standing has become one of the most contested topics in recent Kant scholarship. Section 5 examines that argument, surveys the major contemporary defenses of Kantian progressivism, and confronts the two most serious philosophical challenges to the entire framework: the Frankfurt School's critique of Enlightenment reason and the postcolonial charge that even a regulative progress idea carries ideological freight.

### Subsection 5.1: The Geschichtszeichen Argument and its Epistemic Standing

Kant's appeal in the Contest of Faculties to the French Revolution as a "historical sign" of moral progress is among the philosophically most discussed passages in his historical writings, in part because its evidential structure is far from transparent. Golob (2021) provides the most careful analytical reconstruction of what Kant is and is not claiming. The sign in question is not the Revolution itself — which Kant famously declines to endorse as a model for future action — but the enthusiasm of its spectators: the sympathetic moral excitement felt by observers across Europe who were not participants and stood to gain nothing materially from its outcome. Golob argues that this enthusiasm functions as a symptom or diagnostic sign, analogous in its epistemic structure to aesthetic judgment, rather than as empirical evidence in the ordinary inductive sense. It reveals a moral predisposition in humanity — a latent capacity for disinterested commitment to the ideas of freedom and justice — without grounding any prediction about future historical events. The epistemic claim is thus symptomatological rather than predictive, and its modesty is philosophically deliberate.

This reading carries significant defensive force against one class of objections. Stolzenberg (2025) deploys a structurally parallel argument against Lyotard's post-Holocaust challenge to Kant. Lyotard had argued that the catastrophes of the twentieth century — the Holocaust above all — have rendered the appeal to revolutionary enthusiasm as a historical sign morally and epistemically untenable: after Auschwitz, Kant's optimistic reading of the crowd's reaction to the Revolution becomes something to be ashamed of rather than philosophically vindicated. Stolzenberg replies that Lyotard commits what he calls a historicist fallacy: the reference to historical catastrophes is not an argument against the a priori validity of the ideas of freedom and justice that the enthusiasm was taken to express. Catastrophes, however devastating, are empirical events, and empirical events cannot count as evidence against the a priori validity of moral ideas. The Geschichtszeichen argument is not an empirical hypothesis that can be falsified; it is a claim about a moral predisposition whose reality the enthusiasm reveals and whose normative standing is secured a priori.

Williams (2024) introduces an important complication that neither Golob's nor Stolzenberg's defense fully addresses. A close reading of the Contest of Faculties text — the "Renewed Attempt to Answer the Question: Is the Human Race Continually Improving?" — shows that Kant himself retreats, in this late text, from the more ambitious claims of the 1784 essay and the earlier political writings. In those earlier works, Kant had sought to demonstrate the theoretical certainty of moral progress at the species level; in the Contest of Faculties, Williams argues, he concedes that only rightful-political progress toward global republicanism — the establishment of legal and constitutional conditions — is something we can be confident about. Moral progress in the stronger sense (genuine improvement in moral character and motivation) turns out to be something Kant remains hopeful about but cannot establish even on symptomatological grounds. This is a significant self-revision: the sign of history justifies hope for political reform more securely than it justifies hope for moral transformation in the strict Kantian sense, where what is at stake is the noumenal disposition of the will.

Williams's reading connects to a deeper conceptual problem identified by Merritt (2023): the coherence of collective moral progress as a concept within Kant's own practical philosophy. Moses Mendelssohn had argued that moral progress is possible only for individuals, not for humanity as a species — a position Kant himself labels "abderitism" and explicitly rejects. But the objection has force: if moral worth attaches only to the individual's noumenal will, how can the species as a corporate agent be said to make moral progress? Merritt argues, against Paul Guyer's claim that Kant's freedom commitments trap him in abderitism, that the Stoic background shared by both Mendelssohn and Kant illuminates why Kant can coherently posit species-level progress without requiring individual moral development to occur in historically visible ways. On Merritt's reading, individual progress cannot be fully independent of the progress of the species understood as a corporate agent, because the social and institutional conditions that enable the moral life of individuals are themselves historical products. The Geschichtszeichen argument, on this construal, speaks to the species as a moral subject in a way that is not simply reducible to the sum of individual moral trajectories.

### Subsection 5.2: Defenses of Kantian Progressivism

The major contemporary defenses of Kantian progressivism converge, from different starting points, on the regulative-practical reading: progress is not a theoretical claim about historical causation but a normative postulate that is practically indispensable. Where they differ is in the philosophical grounding they offer for that indispensability.

Kleingeld (2001) argues that the philosophy of history is morally indispensable to Kant's practical philosophy because without the claim that nature works in history toward cosmopolitan conditions, the moral demand to work for such conditions lacks the kind of empirical support that distinguishes genuine hope from mere wish. Practical hope, on this reading, requires more than moral prescription; it requires some ground for believing that the object of hope is not entirely counteracted by the structure of the world. Nature's providential guidance — understood as a regulative idea rather than a metaphysical commitment — provides that ground. Kleingeld's argument is thus not that the philosophy of history adds extra content to Kant's ethics, but that without it the ethics itself becomes practically ungrounded: moral agents cannot sustain long-term engagement in historical reform if they have no basis for believing the world is at least open to such reform.

Ypi (2010, 2024) provides the most theoretically developed extension of this line of argument into constructivist metaethics. The 2010 paper argues that Kant's appeal to nature's guarantee in Perpetual Peace is best understood as a device for practical motivation rather than theoretical prediction: the constructivist agent committed to republican ideals must presuppose that progressive history is possible in order to act coherently, since without this presupposition moral commitment to political goals becomes self-undermining. The 2024 paper deepens this argument to the level of constructivist justification itself: the concept of purposiveness — and the philosophy of history embedded within it — is not an optional supplement to Kantian constructivism but is structurally required to prevent its justification from circularity. Without a critical notion of purposiveness anchoring what it would mean for practical reasoning to succeed in history, constructivist justification of practical normativity cannot get off the ground. On Ypi's reading, Kant's philosophy of history is not an empirical supplement to an otherwise complete moral theory but a necessary internal component of any constructivist practical philosophy.

Moran (2012) addresses the collective-versus-individual problem from a different angle. Locating the grounds of moral progress in the ethical community — the "kingdom of God on earth" described in the Religion — Moran argues that progress can be collective without requiring the implausible claim that individual noumenal wills improve in historically measurable ways. Progress occurs at the level of the social and institutional conditions that enable and sustain moral agency: the building of ethical community is itself a moral achievement that accumulates historically, providing a basis for species-level progress that is genuinely moral rather than merely legal or political. This account thus answers both Mendelssohn's individualist objection (progress is possible at the species level because the relevant subject is the ethical community, not the aggregate of individual wills) and the concern Williams raises about the limits of the Geschichtszeichen argument (what we are tracking as progress includes institutional moral development, not only the moral quality of individual decisions).

Papish (2024) traces a development within Kant's own account of hope that partially corroborates Williams's developmental reading while defending its coherence. Between the Common Saying essay and Toward Perpetual Peace on the one hand and the Religion on the other, Kant revises his account of the grounds of hope for moral progress: the recognition of radical evil — the foundational propensity of human beings to invert the priority of the moral law — leads him to abandon the view that natural teleology alone can ground hope, replacing it with a moral-religious commitment that is compatible with, though not reducible to, empirical evidence for progress. Papish argues this revised account is more, not less, philosophically honest than the earlier version: it acknowledges the depth of the obstacle to moral progress while preserving the rational indispensability of hope by grounding it in practical faith rather than natural mechanism. The trajectory of Kant's thought on this question is thus not a retreat into pessimism but a more rigorous account of what hope requires.

### Subsection 5.3: The Frankfurt School Challenge and the Limits of Enlightenment Reason

The defenses surveyed above face a challenge that is structural rather than empirical. Horkheimer and Adorno's Dialectic of Enlightenment (2002) does not simply argue that progress has failed to occur or that the evidence is insufficient. It argues that the faculty of reason Kant treats as the motor of historical progress toward freedom is the same faculty whose systematization — its drive to classify, quantify, and dominate — produces the administered society and, at its limit, Auschwitz. The Dialectic's diagnosis is not external to the Kantian framework: it is an immanent critique that turns Enlightenment's own criterion of rational consistency against it, arguing that the domination of external nature through instrumental reason structurally recoils into the domination of human nature. This is not a contingent historical failure but a structural tendency encoded in what Enlightenment reason is.

Kant appears explicitly in the Dialectic's "Juliette" excursus, where Adorno and Horkheimer argue that the passage "from Kant to Sade" is philosophically necessary: Kantian moral formalism — the categorical imperative as a formal law indifferent to sensuous content — can be turned to destructive ends as readily as to enlightened ones, and Sade's philosophy simply makes explicit what is implicit in formalism's abstraction from empirical feeling and inclination. James (2018) provides the closest analytical scrutiny of this argument, reconstructing its structure with precision. His conclusion is that the argument misidentifies the relationship between Kantian moral formalism and sensuous nature: Kant does not abstract from all sensuous content in order to dominate it but rather takes seriously the demands of sensuous beings in a way that is not captured by the Dialectic's diagnosis. The move from Kant to Sade is not the necessary logical development Adorno and Horkheimer allege; it rests on a misreading of how formalism functions within Kant's moral theory.

Cook (2020) and Iago (2020) reconstruct Adorno's complex relationship to Kant's Enlightenment essay with greater nuance than the Dialectic's polemical rhetoric might suggest. Cook demonstrates that Adorno appropriates Kant's notion of intellectual maturity (Mündigkeit) while rejecting the thesis that immaturity is merely self-incurred — a thesis Adorno regards as ideologically convenient for a society whose structures systematically produce immaturity. Crucially, Cook shows that Adorno connects enlightenment not to instrumental reason's triumph but to the Kantian idea of a realm of ends, preserving the moral core of Kant's vision while dissolving its progressive optimism. Iago (2020) traces this engagement across Adorno's career, showing that the late Adorno deliberately sublates rather than simply rejects Kant's definition of enlightenment: he preserves the emancipatory demand for autonomy while negating its optimistic implication that the conditions for its realization are available to agents who simply choose to use their reason. These reconstructions suggest that the Frankfurt School critique, properly understood, is an internal critique from Kantian premises rather than a wholesale rejection of the Kantian legacy — which means that the resources for responding to it are also, in part, Kantian.

Allen (2016) draws the Frankfurt School challenge together with the postcolonial critique in ways that are harder to deflect. Allen argues that the normative foundations of critical theory — Kantian and Habermasian — are structured by a philosophy of history that takes European modernity as its implicit telos. Even when reconstructed in regulative-practical terms, the orientation toward progress carries ideological freight because the practical orientation itself is structured by assumptions about what "going well" for history looks like — assumptions that track European forms of political organization, legal rights, and rational self-determination. This is not merely the charge that Kant's early racial theory is inconsistent with his mature cosmopolitanism; it is the deeper charge that even a fully revised, regulative reading of progress remains embedded in a framework whose evaluative criteria are not culturally neutral. Marwah (2022), examined in Section 4, provides the structural analysis that underwrites Allen's broader claim: the teleological framework identifies the moral vocation of humanity with rational self-determination, and the same framework assigns different races different capacities for that development, so that severing racial hierarchy from the teleological core requires more than biographical revision — it requires restructuring the framework's evaluative criteria from the ground up.

The force of Allen's argument against the regulative reading is that it targets not the metaphysical content of the progress claim but its practical orientation. The regulative defense insulates Kant from the charge that he makes a falsifiable prediction about history; it does not insulate him from the charge that the practical orientation toward progress — what agents must presuppose in order to act coherently toward moral-political ends — is already ideologically structured. What the Geschichtszeichen argument reveals as a moral predisposition in humanity is, on Allen's reading, a predisposition that is culturally specific rather than universal in the way Kant assumes.

The philosophical standing of Kantian moral progress claims ultimately depends on whether the regulative-practical reading can meet this dual challenge: preserving the practical significance of the progress idea against the Frankfurt School's structural critique of reason, while addressing Allen's and Marwah's argument that the practical orientation is itself ideologically structured. The defenses by Ypi and Kleingeld are philosophically powerful within the Kantian framework; the challenge Allen poses is the hardest to answer from within that framework, because it calls into question the framework's evaluative presuppositions rather than its internal consistency. This is a genuinely open question in the literature, and honest assessment requires acknowledging it as such rather than treating the regulative reading as a fully adequate response to the postcolonial critique.

## Conclusion

The preceding review has traced Kant's philosophy of history across four interlocking debates: the interpretation of the 1784 essay and its mechanism of unsocial sociability; the question of systematic coherence across the Critique of Judgment, the Religion, and the Anthropology; the foundational controversy with Herder and the transformation of Kantian themes by Hegel and Marx; and the contemporary reception in political theory, including the race and postcolonial challenge. Taken together, these debates converge on a single conclusion: the most defensible reading of Kant's philosophy of history is the regulative-practical one — history as a normative orientation indispensable to practical reason, not a theoretical claim about nature's causal order. This conclusion is supported by the most sustained scholarly defenses currently available (Kleingeld 2011; Ypi 2024; Lenczewska 2025), and it is implicit in Kant's own late modesty, as Williams (2024) shows in his analysis of the "Renewed Attempt."

But the review has equally shown that this reading faces genuine pressure from three directions that cannot be fully deflected by appeal to the regulative/constitutive distinction alone. First, the internal tension between the doctrine of radical evil in the Religion and the progressive optimism of the political essays is deeper than the existing literature has systematically addressed. Anderson-Gold's resolution — that radical evil is "unnecessary" rather than naturally determined — preserves both the seriousness of the evil doctrine and the coherence of moral hope, and Buddeberg adds the institutional dimension of the ethical community. Yet neither account fully closes the gap: if the mechanism of unsocial sociability does not explain how legal arrangements generate moral conversion (James 2019), and if radical evil reflects a fundamental human propensity that is not merely individual but species-level, then the progressive narrative and the moral anthropology of the Religion remain in a tension that is frequently noted but rarely given sustained systematic treatment. This is the most significant gap in the existing scholarship. Second, the Herderian and postcolonial challenge to the universalism embedded in the very idea of cosmopolitan progress has been sharpened, in recent scholarship, by the race-teleology connection that Marwah (2022) and Lu-Adler (2022) identify: the teleological framework that tracks humanity's rational vocation assigns different capacities for that vocation to different populations, such that the "universal" subject of cosmopolitan history is structurally particular. Kleingeld's revisionism (2011) — that Kant changed his mind on race by the mid-1790s — does not fully answer this challenge, because Marwah's argument targets the teleological framework itself, not only Kant's earlier racial writings. The regulative reading deflates the metaphysical commitments of the framework but does not thereby evacuate its implicit anthropological assumptions. Third, the Frankfurt School's structural challenge — elaborated most systematically by Horkheimer and Adorno (2002) and extended into the postcolonial domain by Allen (2016) — holds that the faculty of reason Kant treats as the driver of historical progress is the same faculty whose formalization generates domination. Allen's argument is the hardest to answer: even a practically oriented progress-idea is structured by assumptions about what it would mean for history to "go well," and those assumptions, she argues, carry the ideological imprint of European modernity. The regulative reading partially answers this challenge by denying that progress is a theoretical prediction, but it does not fully answer it, because the practical orientation itself is not ideologically neutral.

The current state of the field divides, correspondingly, into two broad tendencies. A sympathetic-reconstructive tendency — represented by Kleingeld (2011), Ypi (2024), Lenczewska (2025), and Papish (2024) — defends a coherent and practically indispensable Kantian philosophy of history through careful disambiguation of its teleological, regulative, and moral-religious dimensions. A critical-revisionary tendency — represented by Marwah (2022), Lu-Adler (2022), Allen (2016), and Basevich (2021) — argues that the philosophical resources of the Kantian framework cannot be rehabilitated without confronting the structural complicity of its assumptions with European racial hierarchy. These are not merely interpretive disagreements; they reflect genuinely different assessments of what philosophical rehabilitation of a historical thinker requires.

Three open questions stand out as insufficiently addressed in the existing literature. A philosophically rigorous and systematic engagement between radical evil and the progressive narrative — one that goes beyond noting the tension to specifying precisely what would resolve or dissolve it — remains to be written. Whether Allen's challenge can be met from within a regulative-practical framework, or whether it requires revising the very conception of practical orientation that framework deploys, is an open question of significant importance for both Kantian scholarship and critical theory. And the relationship between Kant's regulative-idea approach to history and contemporary metaethical debates about moral realism and moral progress has been underexplored: if moral progress is a practical postulate rather than a descriptive claim, how does this bear on debates about whether there are mind-independent moral truths against which progress can be measured? Rosen's (2022) genealogical argument — that the philosophy-of-history tradition, including its apparently secular Kantian form, retains the shadow of providential theology — adds a further dimension to this question that the existing analytic Kant literature has yet to absorb.

Finally, the Kant-Herder dispute's structural analogy to contemporary debates between universalism and pluralism in political philosophy — between Rawlsian cosmopolitanism and communitarian critiques, between global institutionalism and political realism — deserves more systematic treatment than the existing literature provides. The Rorty and Schmidt volume (2009) remains the primary locus for mapping the secondary literature, but neither it nor the subsequent scholarship has made the structural analogy between Herder's objections and contemporary anti-cosmopolitan arguments an explicit object of analysis. This comparative-philosophical gap represents a significant opportunity for new work in the field.

## Introduction

Kant's 1784 essay "Idea for a Universal History with a Cosmopolitan Purpose" occupies an uncomfortable position in his corpus. It is not a work of the critical philosophy in the strict sense — it offers no transcendental deduction, deploys no schematism, and proceeds in a speculative register that the three Critiques carefully circumscribe. Yet it is equally misleading to read it as a merely popular or occasional piece. As Kuehn (2009) has argued, the essay is best understood as a methodological proposal for how to write universal history from a philosophical point of view — it is, in other words, a contribution to Geschichtsschreibung rather than to metaphysics. But even this deflationary characterization leaves open the question that has driven the secondary literature: what kind of claim is a "philosophy of history" licensed to make, and how does that claim stand in relation to the constraints that the critical system imposes on theoretical and practical knowledge alike? The indeterminacy of genre is not accidental. It is symptomatic of a deeper philosophical difficulty about the epistemic status of progress, the warrant for invoking nature's "plan," and the relationship between empirical history and the demands of practical reason.

The secondary literature on Kant's philosophy of history has organized itself around three interlocking tensions, and this review follows that structure rather than tracing the chronological development of Kant's writings or mapping the literature domain by domain. The first tension concerns the distinction between regulative and constitutive teleology. When Kant claims that nature "guarantees" movement toward a cosmopolitan legal order, is he making a constitutive theoretical claim about historical causation, offering a regulative heuristic for orienting inquiry, or articulating something closer to a postulate of practical reason — a normatively necessary assumption rather than an empirical prediction? This is the master-question of the field, and how one answers it determines one's reading not only of the 1784 essay but of the "Guarantee of Perpetual Peace" in the later political writings, analyzed with particular precision by Ypi (2010). The second tension concerns the systematic unity of Kant's historical thinking. Kant never wrote a treatise on history; his reflections are scattered across the 1784 essay, the third Critique's teleological sections, the Religion, the political essays of the 1790s, and the Anthropology. Whether these texts compose a coherent philosophical position or merely a set of occasional reflections whose ambitions exceed what the critical system can sustain is the central controversy in the domain of systematic interpretation. The third tension, increasingly central to the contemporary reception, concerns Kant's universalism. The cosmopolitan progress narrative is challenged from two directions: from the Herderian tradition, which holds that each culture instantiates its own form of Humanität and that no single telos of rational development can be imposed upon human diversity; and from postcolonial scholarship, which argues that Kant's teleological framework is not merely silent about but structurally implicated in racial hierarchy, such that the "universal" subject of cosmopolitan progress is a specifically European construction. Horkheimer and Adorno (2002) mounted the earliest systematic philosophical assault on this tradition, arguing that Enlightenment rationality harbors a structural tendency toward domination rather than emancipation; more recently, Allen (2016) and Marwah (2022) have pressed the specifically racial dimension of this critique against Kantian progressivism.

The review proceeds in four movements. The first examines the 1784 essay itself — its argumentative structure, the mechanism of unsocial sociability, and the competing interpretive positions (deflationary, regulative-practical, strongly integrative, naturalized) that the secondary literature has developed. The second addresses the systematic question: how Kant's historical thinking relates to the critical philosophy across the range of his writings, including the grounding offered by the third Critique and the complications introduced by the Religion's doctrine of radical evil. The third turns to the foundational controversies with Kant's contemporaries and successors — Herder's particularist challenge, the transformation of Kantian themes by Hegel, and the Marxian reversal of teleology — with particular attention to Rosen's (2022) argument that Kant's kingdom of ends and Hegel's Geist alike carry the imprint of providential theology even as they claim rational autonomy. The fourth examines the contemporary reception: the debate between regulative defenses of Kantian progressivism (Kleingeld 2001; Ypi 2010) and the postcolonial challenges that contest whether even a practically oriented progress-idea can be disentangled from the racial structure of the teleological framework that generates it (Marwah 2022; Lu-Adler 2022; Allen 2016).

The overall argument of this review is that the most defensible reading of Kant's philosophy of history is the regulative-practical one — history as a normative postulate orienting moral action rather than a theoretical claim about nature's causal order — but that this reading faces genuine pressure from two sides that it has not yet fully answered.
