## Section 1: The 1784 Essay — Arguments, Mechanism, and the Status of Nature's Plan

Kant's "Idea for a Universal History with a Cosmopolitan Purpose" deploys three philosophically distinct moves that the secondary literature has not always kept carefully separate. First, it offers a causal-mechanical story: unsocial sociability — the human tendency to enter society while simultaneously resisting it — functions as the engine of historical development, driving individuals and states into conflicts that eventually compel the creation of legal and political institutions. Second, it makes a teleological claim: nature "intends" this development, such that the pain and destruction of historical conflict are instruments of a plan aimed at a cosmopolitan legal order. Third, it advances a normative-methodological proposal: we should write universal history from a cosmopolitan point of view, treating the sequence of human events as if it were the working-out of nature's rational purpose. Whether these three moves cohere in a single argument, or whether they pull in different directions requiring different epistemic resources, is the organizing question of the first domain of the secondary literature. As Kleingeld (2001) notes, the failure to distinguish them explains why the essay has generated such divergent readings.

### 1.1 Unsocial Sociability — Mechanism, Theodicy, and the Moral Engine

The concept of unsocial sociability is Kant's version of the theodical problem in secular form. Schneewind (2009) reads the essay as a secular theodicy: just as traditional theodicy argued that apparent evil serves divine purposes, Kant argues that human conflict serves nature's rational plan. The mechanism is dialectical — social drives and antisocial impulses must coexist for development to occur, since a society of perfectly sociable beings would stagnate in pastoral contentment without the antagonism required to develop human capacities. This reading identifies the essay's deep structure as theodical while locating its distinctiveness in the displacement of providential agency onto a naturalistic "plan."

Sargentis (2015) develops the moral-crisis dimension of the same mechanism. On his reading, unsocial sociability does not merely produce instrumental goods (legal institutions, political arrangements) but creates conditions of moral crisis that press human beings toward rational reflection on their situation. The antagonism is not merely an efficient cause of institutional development but a condition for the emergence of moral self-consciousness. This connects the 1784 essay to Kant's later reflection on radical evil in the Religion: the same antagonism that drives political development also expresses the propensity to subordinate moral principle to self-interest.

The most searching critical analysis of what unsocial sociability can and cannot explain is provided by James (2019). James argues that the mechanism faces an explanatory gap that the secondary literature has largely passed over: the mechanism generates the *conditions* for legal and political arrangements (through conflict, competition, and the pressure of mutual antagonism), but it does not explain how moral development follows from the establishment of those arrangements. Legal coercion can produce rule-following without moral motivation; a civil constitution can exist without citizens whose practical reason is oriented by anything more than interest. The mechanism that drives history toward republican and cosmopolitan institutions does not, on its own, close the gap between external legality and internal moral conversion. As James puts it, Kant's history secures the political preconditions of morality while leaving unexplained how those preconditions generate morality itself.

Caranti (2015) offers a reformist response to this explanatory gap. He argues that the mechanism in the 1784 essay can be detached from its pre-Darwinian natural teleology — from the claim that nature has a literally purposive plan — and reconstructed in naturalistic terms compatible with evolutionary biology. If unsocial sociability is understood as a selected trait whose effects happen to be conducive to institutions that extend human flourishing, the teleological gloss is dispensable. Caranti's naturalized reading deflates the metaphysical ambitions of the essay while preserving the core argument about how conflict-driven development produces legal and political arrangements. The cost is that this reading cannot address James's gap from within its own resources: a merely naturalized mechanism gives no more guidance than the original about how legal institutions generate moral development.

Lenczewska (2022) approaches the mechanism from a different angle, connecting it to Kant's broader account of reason's emergence from its pre-human origins. Her reading places the "Conjectural Beginning of Human History" (1786) alongside the 1784 essay, arguing that reason's historical self-manifestation is not simply the product of antagonism but requires tracing reason's emergence from nature and its gradual self-articulation through history. This developmental picture situates the mechanism of unsocial sociability within a larger story about what it means for a rational species to constitute itself through history — a reading that anticipates the systematic concerns of Section 2.

### 1.2 Regulative or Constitutive? The Epistemic Status of Nature's Plan

The epistemic status of nature's "plan" is the master-interpretive axis for the entire secondary literature on the 1784 essay. Four positions have been staked out with increasing precision over the past three decades.

Kuehn (2009) offers the deflationary historiographical reading. On his account, the essay is primarily a contribution to Geschichtsschreibung — a proposal for how to write universal history from a rational standpoint — rather than a metaphysical claim about history's deep structure. The nature-teleology of the essay is a regulative device for historical narration, not a theoretical claim about causal mechanisms or a practical postulate about moral requirements. This reading has the advantage of dissolving most of the apparent philosophical tension in the essay (between its speculative claims and critical constraints), but critics have noted that it achieves this by reducing the essay to something less than Kant himself appears to intend.

O'Neill's (2008) regulative-practical reading takes the idea of nature's plan as an Idea of Reason in the technical sense from the first Critique: a concept that cannot be theoretically constitutive (no experience could verify that nature literally has a rational plan) but that can orient inquiry and action. On this reading, the philosophy of history is a practical application of the regulative use of reason — we must proceed as if history were oriented by rational purpose, because only from this standpoint can historical inquiry be integrated into the demands of practical reason. O'Neill situates this within her broader account of how Kant's ethics requires attention to the empirical conditions of its realization.

Lindstedt (1999) provides the most technically precise formulation of the postulate reading: the claim that nature guarantees progress toward a cosmopolitan order is a postulate of practical reason analogous to the postulates of immortality and God's existence, grounded not in theoretical evidence but in the requirements of moral agency consistently pursued. Just as the postulate of immortality does not rest on empirical evidence but on the requirements of an agent committed to moral progress, the postulate of historical progress rests on the requirements of an agent committed to cosmopolitan legal reform. The epistemic status is not theoretical but practical-normative.

Kleingeld (2001) defends the most ambitious version of the regulative reading — what she calls the "strong integration thesis." On her account, Kant's philosophy of history is not a dispensable supplement to the moral philosophy but theoretically and morally necessary. Practically, the claim that nature cooperates with moral demands is required to make sense of moral hope — without the idea that historical conditions can be improved through rational action, the demand for moral effort in history would be unaccompanied by any rational expectation of its efficacy. Theoretically, the nature-freedom bridge that the essay attempts to articulate is not an optional extra to the critical system but a requirement of its coherence. Kleingeld's reading makes the philosophy of history constitutive of the moral philosophy in a way that goes beyond what Kuehn's and O'Neill's accounts allow.

Kryluk (2024) extends this line of analysis forward into Kant's political philosophy of the 1790s. Tracing the connections between the first Critique's regulative ideas, the 1784 essay's teleological proposals, and the argument for perpetual peace, Kryluk shows how the regulative reading of the 1784 essay coheres with Kant's political philosophy by treating the idea of cosmopolitan order as a practically necessary orientation for political agents — neither a theoretical prediction nor a merely optional aspiration, but a normative framework without which political reform loses its rational warrant. This closes some of the gap James identifies: while the mechanism alone does not generate moral development, the practical commitment to cosmopolitan reform gives moral agents a rationally grounded orientation for their historical engagement.

The resolution of the regulative/constitutive debate is not merely a question of Kant-interpretation. As Rauscher (2002) shows in an essay that will be central to Section 3, Hegel's constitutive approach to historical teleology — his claim that Geist is immanent in the historical process — represents a direct response to what he took to be the theoretical inadequacy of Kant's merely regulative proposal. Hegel's constitutive philosophy of history is, in part, an attempt to supply what Kant's regulative approach refuses to provide.

### 1.3 The Essay's Relation to the Critical Project

The 1784 essay predates the Critique of Judgment (1790) by six years, and a persistent question in the scholarship concerns how its teleological proposals relate to the systematic position that the third Critique articulates. Three approaches have been developed.

Woo (2023) offers a developmental narrative: Kant's account of moral hope underwent a significant shift between his pre-critical and early critical writings (where hope was located in individual eschatology and the highest good) and the 1784 essay (where hope takes on a collective, historical dimension). On Woo's account, this shift is philosophically significant — it represents Kant's recognition that the highest good requires not only individual moral striving but the development of social and political conditions that make moral life collectively possible. The 1784 essay is thus not a mere popular piece but a reconfiguration of the moral philosophy's relationship to history.

Williams (2024) complicates this developmental narrative by examining the late essay "Renewed Attempt to Answer the Question: Is the Human Race Continually Improving?" (1798). Williams argues that in this text Kant himself retreats from the ambitious claims of the 1784 essay to a more modest position: what can be established is not that the human race is improving morally but only that it is capable of rightful-political progress — the development of legal and constitutional arrangements, independent of whether moral motivation keeps pace. This late modesty is significant because it suggests that Kant himself came to acknowledge the explanatory gap that James identifies: the mechanisms that produce political progress are not identical with those that would produce moral progress, and perhaps only the former can be defended with evidential resources available in history.

Lenczewska (2025) provides the most recent book-length treatment of the systematic question. Her central argument is that the "Conjectural Beginning of Human History" (1786) is essential for understanding the 1784 essay, because the "Conjectural Beginning" traces the emergence of practical reason from its pre-human origins and shows how the development of rational agency — not merely the institution of legal arrangements — is historical through and through. On this reading, the historical dimension of Kant's philosophy is not a supplement to the practical philosophy but constitutive of it: practical reason has a history, and that history is not contingent to its structure but partially explanatory of it. Lenczewska's account thus offers a way of addressing James's gap from within Kantian resources: if the emergence of practical reason is itself a historical process, then the connection between legal development and moral development is not a further premise that the mechanism must supply but a structural feature of reason's self-articulation in history.

Allison (2009) approaches the systematic question from the third Critique. His central claim is that sections 83–84 of the third Critique provide the philosophical grounding that the 1784 essay lacks: by distinguishing culture as the "ultimate end" (letzter Zweck) of nature from moral humanity as the "final end" (Endzweck) that lies beyond nature altogether, Kant supplies a principled account of how natural teleology relates to the demands of practical reason without collapsing either into the other. The 1784 essay's teleological proposals, on this reading, are anticipatory sketches of the more rigorous systematic position that the third Critique articulates. This interpretation sets the agenda for the systematic debates of Section 2: does the third Critique's teleological framework actually provide adequate grounding for the philosophy of history, or does Allison overstate the coherence of the bridge between natural purposiveness and moral vocation?

---

The 1784 essay is not self-interpreting on the most fundamental question it raises. The Kuehn-versus-Kleingeld axis — deflationary historiography against strong systematic integration — frames the interpretive choices facing any reader. James's identification of the mechanistic gap and Williams's analysis of Kant's own late modesty represent genuine critical interventions, not merely scholarly dissensions. The most defensible reading, on the evidence of the secondary literature, is a regulative-practical one in Kleingeld's and Kryluk's sense: the philosophy of history is morally necessary without being theoretically constitutive. But this reading must take seriously the force of James's challenge — the mechanism of unsocial sociability does not on its own close the gap between political legality and moral agency — and the developmental complication that Williams introduces.
