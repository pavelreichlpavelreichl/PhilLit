## Conclusion

The preceding review has traced Kant's philosophy of history across four interlocking debates: the interpretation of the 1784 essay and its mechanism of unsocial sociability; the question of systematic coherence across the Critique of Judgment, the Religion, and the Anthropology; the foundational controversy with Herder and the transformation of Kantian themes by Hegel and Marx; and the contemporary reception in political theory, including the race and postcolonial challenge. Taken together, these debates converge on a single conclusion: the most defensible reading of Kant's philosophy of history is the regulative-practical one — history as a normative orientation indispensable to practical reason, not a theoretical claim about nature's causal order. This conclusion is supported by the most sustained scholarly defenses currently available (Kleingeld 2011; Ypi 2024; Lenczewska 2025), and it is implicit in Kant's own late modesty, as Williams (2024) shows in his analysis of the "Renewed Attempt."

But the review has equally shown that this reading faces genuine pressure from three directions that cannot be fully deflected by appeal to the regulative/constitutive distinction alone. First, the internal tension between the doctrine of radical evil in the Religion and the progressive optimism of the political essays is deeper than the existing literature has systematically addressed. Anderson-Gold's resolution — that radical evil is "unnecessary" rather than naturally determined — preserves both the seriousness of the evil doctrine and the coherence of moral hope, and Buddeberg adds the institutional dimension of the ethical community. Yet neither account fully closes the gap: if the mechanism of unsocial sociability does not explain how legal arrangements generate moral conversion (James 2019), and if radical evil reflects a fundamental human propensity that is not merely individual but species-level, then the progressive narrative and the moral anthropology of the Religion remain in a tension that is frequently noted but rarely given sustained systematic treatment. This is the most significant gap in the existing scholarship. Second, the Herderian and postcolonial challenge to the universalism embedded in the very idea of cosmopolitan progress has been sharpened, in recent scholarship, by the race-teleology connection that Marwah (2022) and Lu-Adler (2022) identify: the teleological framework that tracks humanity's rational vocation assigns different capacities for that vocation to different populations, such that the "universal" subject of cosmopolitan history is structurally particular. Kleingeld's revisionism (2011) — that Kant changed his mind on race by the mid-1790s — does not fully answer this challenge, because Marwah's argument targets the teleological framework itself, not only Kant's earlier racial writings. The regulative reading deflates the metaphysical commitments of the framework but does not thereby evacuate its implicit anthropological assumptions. Third, the Frankfurt School's structural challenge — elaborated most systematically by Horkheimer and Adorno (2002) and extended into the postcolonial domain by Allen (2016) — holds that the faculty of reason Kant treats as the driver of historical progress is the same faculty whose formalization generates domination. Allen's argument is the hardest to answer: even a practically oriented progress-idea is structured by assumptions about what it would mean for history to "go well," and those assumptions, she argues, carry the ideological imprint of European modernity. The regulative reading partially answers this challenge by denying that progress is a theoretical prediction, but it does not fully answer it, because the practical orientation itself is not ideologically neutral.

The current state of the field divides, correspondingly, into two broad tendencies. A sympathetic-reconstructive tendency — represented by Kleingeld (2011), Ypi (2024), Lenczewska (2025), and Papish (2024) — defends a coherent and practically indispensable Kantian philosophy of history through careful disambiguation of its teleological, regulative, and moral-religious dimensions. A critical-revisionary tendency — represented by Marwah (2022), Lu-Adler (2022), Allen (2016), and Basevich (2021) — argues that the philosophical resources of the Kantian framework cannot be rehabilitated without confronting the structural complicity of its assumptions with European racial hierarchy. These are not merely interpretive disagreements; they reflect genuinely different assessments of what philosophical rehabilitation of a historical thinker requires.

Three open questions stand out as insufficiently addressed in the existing literature. A philosophically rigorous and systematic engagement between radical evil and the progressive narrative — one that goes beyond noting the tension to specifying precisely what would resolve or dissolve it — remains to be written. Whether Allen's challenge can be met from within a regulative-practical framework, or whether it requires revising the very conception of practical orientation that framework deploys, is an open question of significant importance for both Kantian scholarship and critical theory. And the relationship between Kant's regulative-idea approach to history and contemporary metaethical debates about moral realism and moral progress has been underexplored: if moral progress is a practical postulate rather than a descriptive claim, how does this bear on debates about whether there are mind-independent moral truths against which progress can be measured? Rosen's (2022) genealogical argument — that the philosophy-of-history tradition, including its apparently secular Kantian form, retains the shadow of providential theology — adds a further dimension to this question that the existing analytic Kant literature has yet to absorb.

Finally, the Kant-Herder dispute's structural analogy to contemporary debates between universalism and pluralism in political philosophy — between Rawlsian cosmopolitanism and communitarian critiques, between global institutionalism and political realism — deserves more systematic treatment than the existing literature provides. The Rorty and Schmidt volume (2009) remains the primary locus for mapping the secondary literature, but neither it nor the subsequent scholarship has made the structural analogy between Herder's objections and contemporary anti-cosmopolitan arguments an explicit object of analysis. This comparative-philosophical gap represents a significant opportunity for new work in the field.
