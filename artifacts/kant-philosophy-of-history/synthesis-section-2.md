## Section 2: The Critical System and the Philosophy of History — Grounds, Coherence, and Tensions

Whether Kant's historical writings constitute a philosophically coherent whole or merely a set of occasional reflections whose systematic aspirations exceed what the critical philosophy can accommodate is the central controversy in the literature on Kant's systematic philosophy of history. The question has three sub-questions that must be kept distinct. First: is the third Critique's teleological framework — specifically sections 83–84, which introduce culture as the "ultimate end" of nature and moral humanity as its "final end" — adequate as a foundation for the political essays' progress claims? Second: does the Religion's doctrine of radical evil introduce a genuine tension with the optimistic teleology of those essays, and if so, is it resolvable within Kantian resources? Third: what is the methodological status of the Anthropology from a Pragmatic Point of View — is its empirical-pragmatic register compatible with transcendental idealism's constraints on what can count as knowledge of human beings? The affirmative systematic reading (Allison 2009; Kleingeld 1999; Lenczewska 2025) holds that these elements cohere; the anti-systematic reading (Krijnen 2011; Fonnesu 2022) identifies architectonic tensions that the positive reading obscures.

### 2.1 The Third Critique's Grounding of History — Culture, Final End, and the Nature-Freedom Bridge

The most important recent claim in the systematic literature is that the third Critique provides the philosophical grounding that the 1784 essay lacks. Allison (2009) argues that sections 83–84 supply a principled account of how natural teleology connects to moral vocation: by distinguishing culture as the "ultimate end" (letzter Zweck) — the highest purpose that nature can pursue within its own order — from moral humanity as the "final end" (Endzweck) that lies beyond what nature can provide, Kant establishes a graduated relationship between nature's purposes and morality's demands without collapsing either into the other. Culture — the development of human skills, talents, and capacities through the use of reason — is what nature maximally aims at within its own domain; but the full realization of those capacities in accordance with the moral law requires a step that nature cannot take on its own. On this reading, the philosophy of history occupies the space between natural teleology (what nature can bring about through its mechanisms) and moral teleology (what practical reason demands), and the third Critique provides the framework for articulating that space.

Guyer (2005) elaborates the internal structure of this argument. His central thesis is that the third Critique contains a new argument connecting natural teleology to morality that was not available to Kant before the 1790 publication: the argument from culture as ultimate natural end provides a systematic grounding for the claim that nature cooperates with the demands of practical reason, not merely as a convenient hope but as a principled philosophical result. This reading foregrounds the architecture of the third Critique in a way that makes the philosophy of history appear as a systematic consequence rather than an ad hoc supplement.

Cha (2020) offers the most detailed analysis of what sections 83–84 actually argue. He reconstructs the internal structure of Kant's distinction between letzter Zweck and Endzweck, showing how the argument for culture as ultimate natural end depends on the prior teleological framework for natural purposiveness and how the transition to moral humanity as final end requires the introduction of practical-rational considerations that lie outside the teleological judgment's proper domain. Cha's careful textual analysis both supports and complicates Allison's reading: it confirms that the third Critique supplies a principled framework for the nature-freedom bridge, while revealing that the bridge is more conditional than Allison suggests — it depends on accepting a regulative teleological interpretation of nature that carries its own philosophical costs.

Krijnen (2011) offers the strongest critical response to this systematic reading. His central claim is that the third Critique's teleology of culture concerns natural purposiveness — the way in which organisms and natural products exhibit purposive structure — while the philosophy of history concerns the realization of freedom, and these two domains cannot bear the architectonic weight of connecting them. Kant's natural teleology, even in its most developed form, concerns reflective judgment's treatment of natural organisms; it does not establish that historical development has a teleological structure, because history is not a natural process in the relevant sense but a domain constituted by human freedom. The third Critique's grounding of the philosophy of history, on Krijnen's reading, is not a genuine philosophical solution but an architectonic sleight of hand — it imports teleological concepts across a boundary that the critical system itself demarcates. Rauscher (2002) identifies a related ambiguity: Kant's use of "nature" in the political essays oscillates between a mechanical-naturalistic sense (the causal mechanism of unsocial sociability) and a teleological-purposive sense (nature as oriented toward rational ends), and it is unclear whether these can be unified within the constraints of the critical philosophy. This ambiguity, Rauscher argues, is precisely what Hegel later "resolves" by making historical rationality constitutively real — but the resolution comes at the price of the theoretical modesty Kant's critical philosophy demands.

The debate between Allison and Krijnen is not merely exegetical. It bears directly on the question, raised in Section 1, of whether the regulative-practical reading can provide adequate grounding for the philosophy of history. If Krijnen is right that the third Critique's teleology cannot bear the architectonic weight of grounding historical claims about the development of freedom, then the philosophy of history is left without systematic grounding within the critical philosophy — it is at best a postulate, at worst an inconsistency. If Allison and Guyer are right, the systematic reading is defensible, but only within the constraints that the third Critique's regulative teleology imposes.

### 2.2 Radical Evil, Moral Progress, and the Religion

The doctrine of radical evil in the Religion within the Bounds of Reason Alone (1793) appears to be in tension with the progressive optimism of the political essays. Radical evil is Kant's name for the foundational human propensity to subordinate the moral law to self-interest — not merely to act wrongly on occasion, but to adopt a fundamental orientation in which inclination takes priority over duty. If this propensity is as fundamental as Kant claims — if it characterizes the human species as a whole, not merely particular individuals — then the optimistic teleology of the 1784 essay and the "Guarantee of Perpetual Peace" seems to require either that radical evil can be overcome through natural-historical mechanisms or that the guarantee is compatible with a species that remains radically evil. Neither implication sits easily with Kant's moral philosophy.

Anderson-Gold (2000) offers the most influential positive resolution of this tension. Her central argument is that radical evil is "unnecessary" in a precise technical sense: it is a free, self-chosen propensity rather than a naturally determined trait, and its contingency is precisely what makes genuine moral progress (as opposed to merely natural development) conceivable. If human beings were naturally and necessarily evil, moral progress would require either a miraculous intervention or a denial of natural determination; but if evil is contingently self-chosen, it can be freely abandoned through moral conversion. This reading preserves both the seriousness of the radical evil doctrine (evil is not merely a weakness but a fundamental orientation) and the coherence of the progress narrative (genuine moral improvement is possible because evil is not necessitated). The philosophical cost of this resolution is not insignificant, however: it depends on a robust account of transcendental freedom that critics of Kant's practical philosophy find difficult to sustain.

Buddeberg (2019) develops the social dimension of moral progress that Anderson-Gold's reading leaves underspecified. Even if individual moral conversion is possible in principle, it is not sufficient for the kind of moral progress that Kant's philosophy of history tracks. Moral progress requires the development of institutional and social conditions — what Kant calls the "ethical community" in the Religion — within which individuals can mutually support and reinforce each other's moral development. Buddeberg argues that Kant's account of the ethical community provides a bridge between the radical evil doctrine and the progressive teleology: the institutional conditions for moral progress are themselves the product of historical development, and the Religion's theology of moral community is continuous with rather than in tension with the political philosophy's account of legal and constitutional progress. On this reading, the Religion and the political essays address different dimensions of the same problem — the conditions under which rational agents can make genuine moral progress — rather than contradicting each other.

Papish (2024) takes the tension more seriously and traces a genuine shift in Kant's position. She argues that the recognition of radical evil in the Religion leads Kant to revise his account of the grounds of hope for progress. In the early political essays, hope for historical progress was grounded primarily in natural teleology — the mechanism of unsocial sociability was supposed to produce, at least at the political level, conditions conducive to moral development. After the Religion, this natural-teleological grounding is supplemented (and partially displaced) by a moral-religious account: hope for progress is grounded not in nature's mechanism but in a moral-religious postulate about the compatibility of the moral vocation with historical conditions. This revised account is more modest than the earlier one but also more consistent with the constraints that radical evil places on optimism about natural mechanisms. Papish's reading thus confirms Williams's (2024) observation about late Kantian modesty from a different angle: the recognition of radical evil is part of what motivates Kant's retreat from strong teleological progressivism.

Woo (2023) and Fonnesu (2022) illuminate this from the direction of the highest good concept. Woo shows that Kant's shift toward historical hope was already underway before the Religion, tracking the development of his account of how virtue and happiness can be united under conditions of temporal experience. Fonnesu identifies what he calls a "crisis" in the highest good concept across the critical corpus: from the first Critique's "Canon of Pure Reason," where the highest good is presented as the object of moral teleology and the basis for the postulates, to the Religion, where the connection between virtue and happiness becomes increasingly strained as Kant's account of radical evil deepens, the coherence of the highest good as a systematic concept is progressively undermined. The "crisis" Fonnesu identifies is thus not merely a tension between the Religion and the political essays but a deeper structural difficulty in Kant's moral philosophy's account of how moral striving and worldly happiness can be coherently connected. Engstrom (1992) provides the essential conceptual background for this debate, offering a rigorous analysis of the highest good concept that clarifies what would be required for it to serve its systematic function — and implicitly, therefore, how demanding the conditions for avoiding Fonnesu's "crisis" are.

### 2.3 The Anthropology, Pragmatic Register, and Historical Knowledge

The Anthropology from a Pragmatic Point of View (1798) presents a methodological challenge for systematic readers of Kant's philosophy of history. The pragmatic anthropology operates in a register — knowledge of the human being as a free agent who can be influenced rather than merely described — that sits uneasily between empirical psychology (which Kant explicitly rejects as a science in the Metaphysical Foundations of Natural Science) and transcendental philosophy. If the Anthropology is merely empirical, its claims about human nature cannot bear the philosophical weight that the philosophy of history requires; if it is somehow continuous with the transcendental philosophy, its compatibility with the constraints of the critical system requires explanation.

Cohen (2009) argues that the pragmatic register has its own coherent standards, distinct from both empirical psychology's causal-deterministic methods and transcendental philosophy's a priori methods. Pragmatic anthropology concerns the human being as an agent who can use knowledge of human nature to influence both others and herself — it is knowledge for action rather than theoretical knowledge of human nature. On this reading, the Anthropology is neither pseudo-science nor covert metaphysics but a practically oriented form of self-knowledge whose standards are appropriate to its distinctive subject matter. This reading defuses some of the methodological anxiety about the Anthropology's place in the Kantian system, though it raises its own questions about how pragmatic knowledge relates to the systematic claims of the critical philosophy.

Kleingeld (1999) provides the most careful analytic reconstruction of how the two components of Kant's philosophy of history — natural-teleological development of human capacities, and moral progress in freedom — are related but not identical. Her central distinction is between the development of natural capacities (which Kant believes history shows) and moral progress in the strict sense (which requires the orientation of human agency by the moral law). The Anthropology is relevant to the first component: it tracks the empirical development of human capacities through history and contributes to the naturalistic side of Kant's historical narrative. But it cannot, on its own, establish the second component — moral progress — which requires the normative apparatus of practical reason. Kleingeld's reading thus assigns the Anthropology a limited but genuine role in the philosophy of history, consistent with Cohen's account of its distinct methodological standards.

Oh (2022) develops this by arguing that the Anthropology's foundation is a "twofold teleology of nature and history" — it takes human beings as natural organisms subject to natural teleology and as rational agents subject to the teleology of reason, and its pragmatic perspective is precisely the perspective from which these two aspects of human existence can be considered together. This reading, if correct, makes the Anthropology central to rather than peripheral in the philosophy of history, since it is the discipline that articulates the point of intersection between natural development and practical-rational vocation.

The question of whether the empirical evidence Kant deploys in the political essays — the growth of commerce, the spread of republican constitutions, the reaction to the French Revolution — can do the evidential work he assigns it is, in the end, a question about the Anthropology's methodological standing. If pragmatic anthropological evidence cannot establish theoretical conclusions about historical causation, then the progress narrative rests entirely on the regulative-practical interpretation: it is what we must hope, not what we can know. This is perhaps the most defensible conclusion, and it is consistent with the regulative reading that the review defends overall.

---

The systematic reading of Kant's philosophy of history is defensible but requires careful qualification. The third Critique provides the most developed philosophical framework for connecting natural teleology to the moral vocation, but the connection is more conditional than Allison's account suggests and more architectonically precarious than Kleingeld's systematic reading acknowledges — Krijnen's critical analysis of the architectonic limits is not easily dismissed. The radical evil doctrine introduces a genuine complication for simple progressivism that Anderson-Gold and Buddeberg address resourcefully but do not fully dissolve; Papish's reading of a genuine shift in Kant's position is historically plausible and philosophically significant. These tensions are, in a particular sense, productive: they explain why Kant's philosophy of history neither collapses into theoretical metaphysics (as a constitutive reading would require) nor retreats to pure moral prescription (as a merely deflationary reading would suggest), and they set up the critical challenges from Herder, Hegel, and postcolonial theory that the following sections address.
