## Central Tensions Running Through the Review

Three interlocking controversies give the review its analytical spine and should be foregrounded in each section:

1. **Regulative vs. constitutive teleology**: Is Kant's claim that "nature" works toward a cosmopolitan order a constitutive theoretical claim about historical causation, a regulative heuristic for orienting practical reason, or a postulate of moral hope with a different epistemic status from either? This is the master-question that organizes the 1784 essay debates (Domain 1), the systematic question about the third Critique's grounding of history (Domains 2–3), the contrast with Hegel's constitutive alternative (Domain 5), and the defense against postcolonial critique (Domain 7).

2. **Systematic unity vs. scattered reflections**: Does Kant's corpus contain a coherent philosophy of history, or only a set of occasional essays whose teleological framework is in tension with the constraints of the critical philosophy? Yovel's systematic-unity thesis (Kant and the Philosophy of History, 1980) opened this debate; the question runs through the relationship between the Critique of Judgment (sections 83–84), the Anthropology, the Religion, and the political essays.

3. **Universalism vs. particularity, and the race complication**: Kant's universalist progressivism is contested both from the Herderian direction (each culture has its own Humanität; no single measure of progress is defensible) and from the postcolonial direction (the teleological framework constructing "progress" toward a European-modeled cosmopolitan order is racially structured). The tension between these two forms of particularist challenge and the regulative defenses of Kantian progressivism is the central critical thread.

## Introduction

**Purpose**: Frame the philosophical stakes, introduce the primary text, and establish the analytical structure of the review.

**Content**:

- Open with the peculiar generic status of the 1784 essay "Idea for a Universal History with a Cosmopolitan Purpose": neither a work of the critical philosophy proper nor a merely popular essay, but a methodological proposal for how to write universal history from the standpoint of philosophy. This indeterminacy of genre is itself philosophically significant — it is symptomatic of the deeper question of what kind of claim a "philosophy of history" is licensed to make.
- Identify the three central tensions (above) that run through the literature as a whole, and explain how the review is organized around them rather than simply tracing the chronological development of Kant's writings or following the domain structure of the literature.
- Note the scope: the review covers (1) the 1784 essay and its interpretive debates; (2) the systematic question about the coherence and grounding of Kant's historical thinking across the critical writings; (3) the foundational controversy with Herder and the transformation of Kantian themes by Hegel and Marx; (4) the contemporary reception in political theory and the postcolonial challenge.
- Briefly sketch the overall argument: that the most defensible reading of Kant's philosophy of history is the regulative-practical one (as developed by Kleingeld, Ypi, and O'Neill), but that this reading is under genuine pressure from two sides — the Herderian challenge to the universalism built into the very idea of a "cosmopolitan" progress, and the postcolonial challenge (Marwah, Lu-Adler, Allen) to the claim that even a regulative progress-idea is ideologically neutral.

## Section 1: The 1784 Essay — Arguments, Mechanism, and the Status of Nature's Plan

**Section Purpose**: Establish the primary text's argumentative structure and map the central interpretive fault-lines that all subsequent sections presuppose.

**Main Claims**:
1. The nine theses of the "Idea" essay embed three philosophically distinct moves that the secondary literature has not always kept separate: (a) the mechanism of unsocial sociability as the engine of historical development; (b) the teleological claim that nature "intends" this development; (c) the normative proposal that we should write history from a cosmopolitan point of view. Whether these three moves form a single coherent argument or pull in different directions is the organizing question.
2. Four main interpretive positions have been staked out: the historiographical/deflationary reading (Kuehn: the essay is about how to write history, not about history's deep structure); the regulative/practical reading (O'Neill, Lindstedt: progress as a practical postulate analogous to the postulates of practical reason); the strong integration thesis (Kleingeld: the philosophy of history is morally indispensable to practical reason); and the naturalized/reformist reading (Caranti: the essay's mechanism can be salvaged by detaching it from pre-Darwinian natural teleology).
3. A cross-cutting structural problem — identified most precisely by James (2019) — concerns the gap between the causal-mechanical level of unsocial sociability and the moral-motivational outcome it is supposed to produce: the mechanism pushes agents toward legal and political arrangements, but does not explain how moral conversion occurs once those arrangements are in place.

**Subsection 1.1: Unsocial Sociability — Mechanism, Theodicy, and Moral Engine**

**Papers**: schneewind2009good, james2019practical, sargentis2015crisis, caranti2015defending, lenczewska2022rationality
**Content**: Trace the philosophical work "unsocial sociability" is supposed to do — as an engine of development, a secular theodicy (Schneewind), and a dialectical condition of moral crisis (Sargentis). Address James's challenge: the mechanism does not close the explanatory gap between coercive legal arrangements and moral agency. Caranti's naturalized reading offers a reformist response; Lenczewska (2022) connects the mechanism to Kant's broader account of reason's emergence.
**Connection**: Establishing what unsocial sociability can and cannot explain sets up the later question (Section 2) of whether the Religion's doctrine of radical evil fills or deepens the gap.

**Subsection 1.2: Regulative or Constitutive? The Epistemic Status of Nature's Plan**

**Papers**: kuehn2009reason, oneill2008historical, lindstedt1999progress [note: INCOMPLETE, cite for the postulate reading but do not rely on abstract], rauscher2002regulative, kryluk2024revolution, kleingeld2001nature
**Content**: Map the regulative/constitutive contrast as the master-interpretive axis for the essay. Kuehn's historiographical reading deflates the metaphysical ambitions; O'Neill's Idea-of-Reason reading aligns progress with practical rationality; Lindstedt's postulate reading is the most technically precise formulation; Kleingeld (2001) defends the strong thesis that the philosophy of history is not optional for practical reason but theoretically and morally necessary. Kryluk (2024) connects the first Critique's regulative ideas to the political writings, showing how the regulative reading of the 1784 essay coheres with Kant's political philosophy of the 1790s.
**Connection**: The resolution of this debate determines how the essay relates both to the Groundwork (§1.3 below and Section 2) and to the Hegelian alternative (Section 3).

**Subsection 1.3: The Essay's Relation to the Critical Project**

**Papers**: woo2023hope, williams2024progress, lenczewska2025kant, allison2009teleologyHistory
**Content**: Woo (2023) offers a developmental narrative: Kant shifted from locating moral hope in individual eschatology (highest good) to collective historical progress, explaining why the 1784 essay has moral urgency beyond its ostensible topic. Williams (2024) complicates this by examining the late "Renewed Attempt," which Williams reads as Kant himself retreating to a more modest claim (rightful-political, not moral, progress). Lenczewska (2025), the most recent book-length treatment, argues the "Conjectural Beginning of Human History" (1786) illuminates the 1784 essay by tracing reason's emergence from its pre-human origins, showing the historical dimension is constitutive of the practical philosophy. Allison's essay provides the transcendental grounding via the third Critique.

**Section Summary**: The 1784 essay is not self-interpreting on the most fundamental question — whether its teleological claims have theoretical, regulative-practical, or merely historiographical import. The Kuehn-vs.-Kleingeld axis frames this; James's mechanistic gap and Williams's late-Kantian complication are important critical interventions that the synthesis must acknowledge as genuine problems, not merely scholarly disagreements.

## Section 2: The Critical System and the Philosophy of History — Grounds, Coherence, and Tensions

**Section Purpose**: Assess whether Kant's writings on history form a philosophically coherent system — grounded in the Critique of Judgment's teleology, shaped by the Religion's account of radical evil, and carried into the Anthropology — or whether the systematic aspiration is undermined by internal tensions.

**Main Claims**:
1. The question of systematic unity has three sub-questions that must be kept distinct: (a) Is the third Critique's teleological framework (specifically sections 83–84 on culture and the final end of nature) adequate as a foundation for the political essays? (b) Does the doctrine of radical evil in the Religion create a genuine internal tension with the progressive optimism of the political essays, and if so, is it resolvable? (c) What is the methodological status of the Anthropology — is its pragmatic register compatible with transcendental idealism's constraints?
2. The leading systematic reading (Yovel 1980, Allison 2009, Kleingeld 1999, Lenczewska 2025) holds that these elements form a coherent whole with the third Critique providing the teleological grounding, the Religion providing the account of the social conditions of moral development, and the Anthropology providing the empirical articulation. The anti-systematic reading (Fackenheim 1957, Krijnen 2011, Fonnesu 2022) identifies architectonic tensions that this positive reading obscures.
3. The race-and-progress critique (Basevich 2021, Papish 2024, Marwah 2022) adds a further dimension: the systematic unity thesis may be internally coherent while being normatively compromised, because the "humanity" whose development the system tracks is structurally Eurocentric.

**Subsection 2.1: The Third Critique's Grounding of History — Culture, Final End, and the Nature-Freedom Bridge**

**Papers**: allison2009teleologyHistory, guyer2005natureMorality, cha2020cultureUltimateEnd, krijnen2011teleologyCultureHistory, rauscher2002regulative
**Content**: Allison and Guyer present the affirmative case: sections 83–84 introduce a new argument connecting natural teleology to morality — culture is the "ultimate end" (letzter Zweck) of nature in the teleological sense, while moral humanity is the "final end" (Endzweck) that cannot be derived from nature's purposes alone. Cha (2020) elaborates the internal structure of this argument. Krijnen (2011) offers the strongest critical response: the third Critique's teleology concerns natural purposiveness, while the philosophy of history concerns the realization of freedom, and the architectonic cannot bear the weight of connecting them. Rauscher identifies an ambiguity in Kant's own framework here that Hegel then "resolves" by making history constitutively rational.
**Connection**: This sets up the Hegel transition in Section 3 and explains why Kant's regulative approach generates the structural vulnerability Hegel exploits.

**Subsection 2.2: Radical Evil, Moral Progress, and the Religion**

**Papers**: andersonGold2000unnecessaryEvil, buddeberg2019kantReligion, papish2024kantHope, sargentis2015crisisEvil [note: INCOMPLETE, cite sparingly], engstrom1992highestGood, woo2023kantHope, fonnesu2022highestGoodCrisis
**Content**: The doctrine of radical evil in the Religion — the claim that human beings have a foundational propensity to subordinate the moral law to self-interest — appears to be in deep tension with the political essays' teleological optimism. Anderson-Gold (2000) argues this tension is not a contradiction: evil is "unnecessary" in the sense that it is free rather than naturally determined, and its contingency is precisely what makes genuine moral progress (rather than merely natural development) conceivable. Buddeberg (2019) adds the social dimension: moral progress requires not just individual reform but the institutional conditions of an ethical community. Papish (2024) traces shifts in Kant's account of hope for progress from the early political essays to the Religion, arguing that the recognition of radical evil leads Kant to a revised account where hope is moral-religious rather than grounded in natural teleology alone. Fonnesu (2022) identifies a "crisis" in the highest good concept — from the first Critique's "canon" to the Religion, the connection between virtue and happiness becomes increasingly strained — as symptomatic of the broader tension.
**Connection**: This is the tension the review plan identifies as a genuine gap in the literature: radical evil and progressive optimism are frequently noted as being in tension, but rarely given thorough treatment. The synthesis should flag this as an open question.

**Subsection 2.3: The Anthropology, Pragmatic Register, and Historical Knowledge**

**Papers**: cohen2009pragmaticAnthropology, rossi2021highestGoodSocial [note: INCOMPLETE], kleingeld1999kantHistory, oh2022pragmaticAnthropologyTeleology [note: INCOMPLETE]
**Content**: The Anthropology from a Pragmatic Point of View operates in a methodological register — knowledge of the human being as a free agent who can be influenced — that sits uneasily between empirical psychology (which Kant rejects as science) and transcendental philosophy. Cohen (2009) argues the pragmatic register has its own coherent standards distinct from both. Kleingeld (1999) is the most careful analytic reconstruction of how Kant's two components — natural-teleological development of capacities, and moral progress in freedom — are related but not identical. The question of whether the Anthropology is compatible with transcendental idealism's constraints bears directly on whether the empirical evidence Kant invokes in the political essays (the growth of commerce, the reaction to the French Revolution) can do the evidential work he assigns it.

**Section Summary**: The systematic reading of Kant's philosophy of history is defensible but requires careful qualification. The third Critique provides grounds for connecting natural teleology to human moral vocation, but the connection is more fragile than Allison's account suggests (Krijnen). The radical evil doctrine introduces a genuine complication for simple progressivism that Anderson-Gold and Buddeberg address but do not fully dissolve. These tensions are productive: they explain why Kant's philosophy of history neither collapses into theoretical metaphysics nor retreats to pure moral prescription, and they set up the critical challenges from Herder, Hegel, and postcolonial theory.

## Section 3: Rivals and Successors — Herder's Challenge and the Kant–Hegel–Marx Trajectory

**Section Purpose**: Situate Kant's philosophy of history within the foundational controversy with Herder (its immediate rival) and the transformation into Hegelian and Marxist philosophies of history (its successors), assessing what is distinctively Kantian about the regulative approach by contrast with its alternatives.

**Main Claims**:
1. The Kant-Herder controversy is not merely a historical episode but the founding fault-line of modern philosophy of history: Herder's objections — that Kant's teleological universalism instrumentalizes individual cultures and imposes a Eurocentric measure of development — anticipate both the postcolonial critique (Section 4) and contemporary debates about universalism vs. pluralism. Understanding what Herder objects to, and whether his organic-pluralist alternative is coherent, is essential for evaluating Kant's project.
2. The transition from Kantian to Hegelian philosophy of history turns on the regulative/constitutive divide. Hegel's claim that history is the medium of Geist's self-realization involves a constitutive commitment to the rationality of historical actuality that Kant explicitly refuses. Whether this is a philosophical improvement (Rauscher's initial framing), a completion of Kantian idealism (Pippin), or an unjustified metaphysical inflation (the position the review defends as most defensible given the Kantian constraints) is the central question.
3. Marx's materialist inversion of Hegel poses a different challenge to Kant: it targets the idealism shared by both, arguing that real historical causation runs through material productive relations, not through the development of Geist or practical reason. The question is how much of this critique reaches Kant specifically (vs. Hegel primarily).

**Subsection 3.1: Herder contra Kant — Organism, Humanität, and the Structure of the Dispute**

**Papers**: zammito2002kant, barnard2003herder, sikka2007happiness, sikka2011herder, nassar2015analogy, mertel2016historicism, james2022enlightenment, mcnulty2022teleology, denby2005herder
**Content**: Zammito (2002) provides the essential historical reconstruction showing that the dispute reflects deep methodological disagreement about teleological inference and analogy in natural history. Herder's positive position — that each Volk achieves its own Humanität and that no single culture is merely instrumental for another — is defended by Barnard (2003) as a coherent qualified universalism; Sikka (2007, 2011) extends this as "enlightened relativism." The normative stakes of the dispute: Herder objects that Kant's universalist teleology makes the happiness of existing individuals subordinate to the progress of a future cosmopolitan order, and asks by what right a single measure of progress applies across cultures. James (2022) shows Herder's own position has an internal tension: he cannot avoid some evaluative ranking of historical periods even within his pluralist framework. McNulty (2022) complicates Kant's self-presentation by showing Kant's own race essays make analogous teleological speculations to those he criticizes in Herder, raising questions about the methodological contrast's coherence. Nassar (2015) argues the controversy was philosophically productive for Kant, pressuring him toward the critical teleology of the third Critique.
**Connection**: Herder's normative challenge — that Kant's universalism is covertly particularist (European) — feeds directly into Section 4's treatment of the race and postcolonial critique.

**Subsection 3.2: From Kant to Hegel — The Regulative/Constitutive Divide**

**Papers**: rauscher2002regulative, pippin1989idealism, pippin2008practical, pinkard2017history, sedgwick2023necessity, hutchings1991perpetual, avineri1972hegel, beiser2002german, rosen2022shadow
**Content**: Rauscher (2002) provides the clearest analytical framework: Kant treats progress as regulative (guiding inquiry without asserting historical patterns exist independently), Hegel makes Geist constitutively immanent in history. Rauscher initially argues this makes Hegel's approach "more consistent" for an idealist, but this framing can be reversed: consistency achieved through metaphysical inflation is not an unambiguous gain. Pippin (1989, 2008) argues that Hegel's move is already implicit in Kant's project — if normativity is constitutively historical, Kant's separation of practical reason from history is untenable. Pinkard (2017) defends a non-metaphysical Hegelianism that tries to preserve the normative gains of Hegel's constitutive approach while avoiding commitment to Geist as cosmic substance. Sedgwick (2023) directly compares Kantian and Hegelian necessity in history: Hegel's necessity is stronger than Kant's regulative proposal but shares its motivation. Rosen (2022) situates both thinkers within the broader secularization of theological structures, arguing the philosophy-of-history tradition retains a "shadow of God" even in its most secular presentations — a thesis relevant to Kant's use of "nature" and "providence."
**Connection**: The key question for the review: is the Kantian regulative approach more philosophically defensible than the Hegelian constitutive one? The synthesis should argue affirmatively, but acknowledge the force of Pippin's challenge.

**Subsection 3.3: Marx and the Limits of Idealist Philosophy of History**

**Papers**: cohen1978marx, burns2000introduction, carr2017teleology
**Content**: G.A. Cohen's analytical reconstruction of historical materialism (1978/1979) clarifies what Marx's alternative to idealist philosophy of history amounts to: real historical causation runs through the development of productive forces, not through the development of reason or Geist. Burns and Fraser (2000) survey the complex Hegel-Marx relationship, showing that "inversion" is too simple — Marx retains dialectical structure while materializing it. Carr (2017) represents the skeptical position: the entire tradition of speculative philosophy of history, including Kant's regulative version, rests on an epistemic illusion. Against Carr, the synthesis can deploy the regulative reading: Kant does not claim to know that history has a direction, but to show that we must act as if it does — which is not the illusion Carr targets (that history literally has a telos).

**Section Summary**: The regulative/constitutive divide is the organizing principle for understanding what is distinctively Kantian in the tradition of philosophy of history. Herder's challenge is to the universalism of the cosmopolitan aim; Hegel's to the theoretical modesty of the regulative approach; Marx's to the idealism shared by both. Each challenge is philosophically serious; none is obviously decisive against a well-formulated Kantian position.

## Section 4: Cosmopolitanism, Race, and the Challenge to Kantian Progressivism

**Section Purpose**: Assess the contemporary reception of Kant's cosmopolitanism and the most significant critical challenge to his philosophy of history as a whole — the argument that its teleological framework is constitutively Eurocentric and racially structured.

**Main Claims**:
1. The "guarantee article" of Perpetual Peace (1795) — which holds that nature mechanically produces the conditions for perpetual peace through commerce, mutual interest, and the republican constitution — is the theoretical hinge connecting Kant's philosophy of history to his normative political theory. It extends the unsocial sociability mechanism into an account of how international peace becomes achievable regardless of moral motivation. Contemporary scholarship divides on whether this guarantee commits Kant to a theological providentialism (Molloy) or can be read in naturalistic-regulative terms (Ypi, Kleingeld).
2. Contemporary cosmopolitanism built on Kantian foundations divides into a minimal federation camp (Kleingeld's reconstruction of Kant's own position: a voluntary league of states approximating toward a world federation) and a stronger institutionalist camp (Pogge, Beitz: robust global institutions required by human rights; Habermas: deliberative democratic reconstruction). The synthesis should assess whether the stronger readings are faithful developments of Kant's own philosophy of history or depart from its regulative-teleological structure.
3. The race and colonialism debate (Kleingeld 2007/2014, Lu-Adler 2022, Marwah 2022, Gani 2017) represents the most fundamental challenge to Kantian cosmopolitanism as a philosophical resource: if the teleological framework that generates the universal history is structurally tied to a racial hierarchy (Marwah's "white progress" argument), then the regulative reading of progress does not escape the problem by deflating the teleological commitment — the ideological freight is carried by the framework's implicit anthropology, not only by its metaphysical commitments.

**Subsection 4.1: The Guarantee Article and its Interpretations — Providence, Nature, or Practical Postulate**

**Papers**: kleingeld2001nature, ypi2010natura, molloy2017international, kleingeld2004approaching, kleingeld1998cosmopolitanlaw
**Content**: The guarantee article in Perpetual Peace operationalizes the 1784 essay's unsocial sociability mechanism at the international level: nature uses war, commerce, and self-interest to drive states toward republican constitutions and international federation even without moral motivation from political actors. The key interpretive question is whether this commits Kant to providentialism (Molloy: the guarantee depends on the practical postulate of God's existence), a naturalistic teleology (nature as a mechanism producing peace-conducive conditions), or a practical-constructivist postulate (Ypi: the guarantee is best read as what agents committed to republican ideals must presuppose, not as a theoretical prediction). Kleingeld (1998, 2004) provides the foundational analysis of cosmopolitan right and the architecture of Kant's international theory; her revisionist monograph (2011) situates the guarantee article within Kant's development toward a more consistent universalism.
**Connection**: The practical-constructivist reading of the guarantee connects to Section 5's assessment of contemporary defenses of Kantian progressivism against postcolonial critique.

**Subsection 4.2: Contemporary Cosmopolitanism — Developments and Departures**

**Papers**: kleingeld2011kant, pogge1992cosmopolitanism [note: INCOMPLETE], beitz2005cosmopolitanism [note: INCOMPLETE], finesmith2003habermas, nussbaum1997stoic [note: INCOMPLETE]
**Content**: Map the landscape of contemporary cosmopolitanism as a reception history of Kant's philosophy of history. Kleingeld (2011) defends a Kantian institutionalism that stays close to the text while arguing for more than minimal hospitality norms. Pogge (1992) and Beitz (2005) press toward global distributive justice and dispersed sovereignty, departing from Kant's own state-centric politics while appealing to his moral universalism. Fine and Smith (2003) subject Habermas's "postnational constellation" to critical scrutiny, identifying dilemmas in grounding normative cosmopolitanism without the Kantian moral metaphysics Habermas rejects. Nussbaum (1997) situates Kant within the longer Stoic tradition of cosmopolitanism. The synthesis should note that these developments tend to use Kant's moral universalism while departing from — or remaining silent on — his philosophy of history; this selective appropriation is itself a philosophical choice whose consequences deserve scrutiny.

**Subsection 4.3: Race, Teleology, and the Postcolonial Challenge**

**Papers**: kleingeld2007race, kleingeld2014colonialism, luadler2022slavery, marwah2022white, gani2017erasure, basevich2021progressRace, allen2016end
**Content**: The race debate has a clear structure that the synthesis must respect. The revisionist position (Kleingeld 2007, 2014): Kant changed his mind on race in the mid-1790s, moving from racial hierarchy to genuine egalitarianism, so the mature cosmopolitan theory is defensible even if early Kant is not. The counter-revisionist position (Lu-Adler 2022, Marwah 2022): Kant's racial views were never genuinely abandoned; the philosophy of history's teleological framework depicts non-white peoples as having limited capacity for moral-rational development, making the "universal" subject of history structurally European. Gani (2017) extends this to the cosmopolitan hospitality norm in Perpetual Peace, arguing that it performs an "erasure of race." Basevich (2021) argues the structural connection between racial hierarchy and the progress narrative is not incidental but reflects Kant's teleological framework itself. Allen (2016) situates this critique within a broader argument that the normative foundations of critical theory (Kantian and Habermasian) are shaped by a philosophy of history that takes European modernity as its telos — and that even a regulative/practical reading of progress carries this ideological freight, since the practical orientation itself is structured by assumptions about what it would mean for history to "go well."
**Connection**: The synthesis should present Allen's and Marwah's critiques as genuinely difficult challenges for the regulative reading defended in Sections 1–3, not as external political objections to an otherwise philosophically settled position.

**Section Summary**: The contemporary reception of Kantian cosmopolitanism has developed in two directions that pull against each other: a normative-extension direction (Pogge, Habermas) that presses the universalist core toward stronger institutional conclusions while leaving behind the teleological philosophy of history, and a critical-demolition direction (Marwah, Lu-Adler, Allen) that argues the teleological framework is not separable from the racial hierarchy it embeds. The synthesis must assess which direction better preserves what is philosophically defensible in Kant, and whether the regulative reading can survive the postcolonial challenge intact.

## Section 5: Moral Progress and the Grounds of Hope — Signs of History, Critique, and Defense

**Section Purpose**: Assess the philosophical standing of Kant's concept of moral progress — its evidential basis (the Geschichtszeichen argument), its coherence within the Kantian system, and the critiques that challenge its viability from the Frankfurt School and postcolonial perspectives.

**Main Claims**:
1. The "sign of history" argument in the Contest of Faculties (1798) is Kant's most carefully argued attempt to provide evidential grounds for the claim that the human species is progressing morally: the enthusiasm of spectators — not participants — for the French Revolution functions as a symptom of a moral predisposition in humanity. Golob (2021) argues this has a sophisticated epistemic structure (symptomatological rather than predictive), while Williams (2024) shows that even this modest argument involves Kant conceding that only rightful-political, not moral, progress can be established.
2. The coherence of collective moral progress as a concept is contested at the level of Kant's own practical philosophy. Merritt (2023) addresses the Mendelssohn-Kant debate on whether species-level progress is coherent given Kant's commitment to individual moral agency. Moran (2012) locates the grounds of progress in the ethical community rather than in individual moral trajectories. Ypi (2010, 2024) defends the constructivist-practical reading: progress is not a theoretical claim but a normative postulate indispensable to constructivist justification of moral normativity.
3. The Frankfurt School critique (Horkheimer/Adorno 1944/2002) is the most radical philosophical challenge: not merely that Kant's progress claims lack evidential warrant, but that the faculty of reason Kant treats as the driver of historical progress is the same faculty whose systematization produces domination and Auschwitz. Stolzenberg (2025) defends Kant against Lyotard's post-Holocaust version of this challenge, arguing that historical catastrophes do not count as evidence against the a priori validity of ideas of freedom and justice. James (2018) provides the most careful analysis of the Dialectic's "from Kant to Sade" argument.

**Subsection 5.1: The Geschichtszeichen Argument and its Epistemic Standing**

**Papers**: golob2021revolution, stolzenberg2025enthusiasm, williams2024progress, merritt2023mendelssohn
**Content**: Golob (2021) argues that the French Revolution functions as a Geschichtszeichen not by providing empirical evidence in the ordinary sense but by revealing a moral predisposition in humanity through the enthusiasm of spectators — an epistemic structure analogous to aesthetic judgment. This reading deflects the objection that the argument is empirically falsified by subsequent historical events (Stolzenberg's Lyotard response makes the same point: historical catastrophes cannot count against the a priori validity of moral ideas). Williams (2024) complicates this by showing that Kant himself, in the same text, retreats from his earlier more ambitious claims about moral progress to the more modest claim about rightful-political progress. Merritt (2023) addresses the underlying conceptual problem: how can moral progress be collective when Kant grounds morality in the noumenal will of individuals?
**Connection**: The retreat Williams identifies in late Kant connects to the developmental narrative introduced in Section 1 and to the radical evil complications from Section 2.

**Subsection 5.2: Defenses of Kantian Progressivism**

**Papers**: ypi2010natura, ypi2024moralOught, kleingeld2001nature, moran2012community, papish2024kantHope
**Content**: The major contemporary defenses of Kantian progressivism converge on the regulative-practical reading. Ypi (2010, 2024) provides the most theoretically developed defense: the concept of purposiveness and historical progress is not an optional supplement to constructivist moral justification but is structurally required to avoid circularity. Kleingeld (2001) defends the moral indispensability of the philosophy of history — without the claim that nature works in history toward cosmopolitan conditions, the moral demand for such conditions lacks the empirical support that distinguishes genuine hope from mere wish. Moran (2012) locates the grounds of progress in the ethical community, showing how progress can be collective without violating individual moral agency. Papish (2024) argues that post-Religion Kant grounds hope in a moral-religious commitment rather than natural teleology — a revised but still coherent account.

**Subsection 5.3: The Frankfurt School Challenge and the Limits of Enlightenment Reason**

**Papers**: horkheimerAdorno2002dialectic, cook2020adorno, iago2020unfettered, james2018kantToSade, allen2016end
**Content**: The Dialectic of Enlightenment's challenge to Kantian progressivism is not merely empirical (progress has not happened) but structural: the instrumental reason that drives the domination of nature is the same reason Kant treats as the motor of historical progress toward freedom. Cook (2020) and Iago (2020) reconstruct Adorno's complex relationship to Kant's Enlightenment essay, showing that Adorno's critique is an internal critique from Kantian premises (he preserves the Muendigkeit ideal and the idea of a realm of ends) rather than a wholesale rejection. James (2018) provides the most careful analysis of the "from Kant to Sade" excursus, arguing that the argument misidentifies Kantian moral formalism's relationship to sensuous nature. Allen (2016) draws the critical theory challenge together with postcolonial critique: even the emancipatory orientation of Kantian practical reason is structured by a philosophy of history that takes European modernity as its implicit telos.

**Section Summary**: The philosophical standing of Kantian moral progress claims depends on how much evidential weight the Geschichtszeichen argument can bear, and this depends in turn on whether the regulative-practical reading successfully insulates the progress idea from empirical refutation while preserving its practical significance. The defenses by Ypi and Kleingeld are powerful; the Frankfurt School and postcolonial challenges require genuine engagement rather than dismissal. Allen's argument that the regulative reading does not fully escape the ideological structure is the hardest challenge to answer, and the review should acknowledge this honestly.

## Conclusion

**Content**:
