## Introduction

Kant's 1784 essay "Idea for a Universal History with a Cosmopolitan Purpose" occupies an uncomfortable position in his corpus. It is not a work of the critical philosophy in the strict sense — it offers no transcendental deduction, deploys no schematism, and proceeds in a speculative register that the three Critiques carefully circumscribe. Yet it is equally misleading to read it as a merely popular or occasional piece. As Kuehn (2009) has argued, the essay is best understood as a methodological proposal for how to write universal history from a philosophical point of view — it is, in other words, a contribution to Geschichtsschreibung rather than to metaphysics. But even this deflationary characterization leaves open the question that has driven the secondary literature: what kind of claim is a "philosophy of history" licensed to make, and how does that claim stand in relation to the constraints that the critical system imposes on theoretical and practical knowledge alike? The indeterminacy of genre is not accidental. It is symptomatic of a deeper philosophical difficulty about the epistemic status of progress, the warrant for invoking nature's "plan," and the relationship between empirical history and the demands of practical reason.

The secondary literature on Kant's philosophy of history has organized itself around three interlocking tensions, and this review follows that structure rather than tracing the chronological development of Kant's writings or mapping the literature domain by domain. The first tension concerns the distinction between regulative and constitutive teleology. When Kant claims that nature "guarantees" movement toward a cosmopolitan legal order, is he making a constitutive theoretical claim about historical causation, offering a regulative heuristic for orienting inquiry, or articulating something closer to a postulate of practical reason — a normatively necessary assumption rather than an empirical prediction? This is the master-question of the field, and how one answers it determines one's reading not only of the 1784 essay but of the "Guarantee of Perpetual Peace" in the later political writings, analyzed with particular precision by Ypi (2010). The second tension concerns the systematic unity of Kant's historical thinking. Kant never wrote a treatise on history; his reflections are scattered across the 1784 essay, the third Critique's teleological sections, the Religion, the political essays of the 1790s, and the Anthropology. Whether these texts compose a coherent philosophical position or merely a set of occasional reflections whose ambitions exceed what the critical system can sustain is the central controversy in the domain of systematic interpretation. The third tension, increasingly central to the contemporary reception, concerns Kant's universalism. The cosmopolitan progress narrative is challenged from two directions: from the Herderian tradition, which holds that each culture instantiates its own form of Humanität and that no single telos of rational development can be imposed upon human diversity; and from postcolonial scholarship, which argues that Kant's teleological framework is not merely silent about but structurally implicated in racial hierarchy, such that the "universal" subject of cosmopolitan progress is a specifically European construction. Horkheimer and Adorno (2002) mounted the earliest systematic philosophical assault on this tradition, arguing that Enlightenment rationality harbors a structural tendency toward domination rather than emancipation; more recently, Allen (2016) and Marwah (2022) have pressed the specifically racial dimension of this critique against Kantian progressivism.

The review proceeds in four movements. The first examines the 1784 essay itself — its argumentative structure, the mechanism of unsocial sociability, and the competing interpretive positions (deflationary, regulative-practical, strongly integrative, naturalized) that the secondary literature has developed. The second addresses the systematic question: how Kant's historical thinking relates to the critical philosophy across the range of his writings, including the grounding offered by the third Critique and the complications introduced by the Religion's doctrine of radical evil. The third turns to the foundational controversies with Kant's contemporaries and successors — Herder's particularist challenge, the transformation of Kantian themes by Hegel, and the Marxian reversal of teleology — with particular attention to Rosen's (2022) argument that Kant's kingdom of ends and Hegel's Geist alike carry the imprint of providential theology even as they claim rational autonomy. The fourth examines the contemporary reception: the debate between regulative defenses of Kantian progressivism (Kleingeld 2001; Ypi 2010) and the postcolonial challenges that contest whether even a practically oriented progress-idea can be disentangled from the racial structure of the teleological framework that generates it (Marwah 2022; Lu-Adler 2022; Allen 2016).

The overall argument of this review is that the most defensible reading of Kant's philosophy of history is the regulative-practical one — history as a normative postulate orienting moral action rather than a theoretical claim about nature's causal order — but that this reading faces genuine pressure from two sides that it has not yet fully answered.
