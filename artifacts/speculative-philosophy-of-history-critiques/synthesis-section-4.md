## Section 3: The Immanent-Dialectical Critique — The Frankfurt School's Ambivalent Inheritance

What distinguishes the Frankfurt School's engagement with speculative philosophy of history from the external dismissals catalogued in Sections 1 and 2 is its fundamentally immanent character. The analytic objection charges speculative history with logical incoherence; the hermeneutic objection charges it with epistemological overreach. The Frankfurt School, by contrast, prosecutes its critique from within the Hegelian-Marxist heritage itself, turning the dialectic against its own teleological inheritance. This internal position is both the tradition's greatest philosophical strength and the source of its most persistent difficulty: if speculative reason is compromised, from where does the critique draw its own normative authority?

### 3.1 Dialectic of Enlightenment and Negative Dialectics

The methodological framework for Frankfurt School critique is laid down in Horkheimer's 1937 essay "Traditional and Critical Theory," collected in his *Critical Theory: Selected Essays* (Horkheimer 1972). Horkheimer argues that traditional theory — including Hegelian speculative idealism no less than positivism — abstracts from the social relations in which thought is embedded and thereby affirms the existing order by presenting it as rational necessity. Critical theory, by contrast, maintains a reflexive relation to society as a dynamic totality oriented toward emancipation. The implicit charge against Hegel is that the Phenomenology's narrative of Spirit achieving self-knowledge in the modern state performs precisely the affirmative function Horkheimer diagnoses: it ratifies the present as the telos of world history.

Horkheimer and Adorno radicalize this critique in *Dialectic of Enlightenment* (Horkheimer and Adorno 2002). Their argument is that reason itself — including the speculative reason that identifies the rational with the real — harbors the structure of domination it claims to have overcome. The drive toward conceptual mastery and quantification turns against itself, regressing into new mythic forms. Any philosophy of history that sees in reason a progressive force is caught within this self-undermining dialectic; the Hegelian identification of world history with reason's self-realization participates in the very pathology it purports to diagnose. Hohendahl (2013) has reconstructed Adorno's more developed treatment of progress in dialogue with Augustine, Kant, and Benjamin, showing that Adorno holds a dialectical rather than simply negative concept: he neither endorses progress nor simply rejects it, but insists on its inner contradiction as irreducible — making any teleological resolution premature and ideological.

Adorno's *Negative Dialectics* (Adorno 1973) provides the most sustained philosophical confrontation with Hegel in the tradition. Adorno reconstructs Hegelian dialectics as "identity thinking" — the compulsion to subsume particulars under concepts in ways that do violence to their nonidentity with the concept. Speculative philosophy of history is, on this account, the supreme institutional expression of identity thinking: universal history as the self-realization of Spirit totalizes suffering by assigning it a redemptive function, making catastrophe meaningful by absorbing it into a narrative of progress. Adorno's explicit formulation — that to write speculative philosophy of history after Auschwitz is obscene — is the sharpest edge of this critique. Pocius (2023) has provided a technically precise account of how Adorno's procedure is itself dialectical: the nonidentity concept sits between Kant and Hegel, prolonging Kant's critique through sociohistorical reconstruction while using Hegel's own instruments against Hegel's speculative closure. The critique is immanent, not external. Reynolds (2021), drawing on Allen's analysis, extends this reading by showing that Adorno and Horkheimer's social theory requires notions of cultivation and learning that imply a residual concept of progressive development, even while their explicit position denies any teleological resolution — a tension the review will return to below.

Against the dominant critical thrust within the School, Marcuse's *Reason and Revolution* (Marcuse 1941) occupies a different position: a sympathetic retrieval of Hegel's critical-negative potential against positivism and fascism. For Marcuse, the Hegelian dialectic is not primarily a theodicy of progress but a tool for disclosing the negativity immanent to every social form — its kinship with Marx lying precisely in this critical-negative structure. Marcuse's reading establishes the internal tension within the Frankfurt School's Hegel reception: Adorno reads Hegel's identity-thinking as a philosophical disease to be diagnosed, while Marcuse reads the dialectic's negativity as a resource to be recovered.

### 3.2 Benjamin's Messianic Critique of Progress

Walter Benjamin's "On the Concept of History" (Benjamin 1940) contributes a philosophically distinctive anti-progressivist argument that complements but cannot be reduced to Adorno's. Against historicism's "homogeneous, empty time" — the temporal medium through which progress is conceived as gradual accumulation — Benjamin articulates a messianic conception of historical time in which the present possesses a "weak messianic power" to redeem suppressed pasts by blasting open the continuum of history rather than consummating it. The angel of history, swept into the future while gazing at accumulating wreckage, figures the incompatibility of progressive teleology with the reality of catastrophe. Revolutionary action is a "tiger's leap" into the past, not a forward movement toward a predetermined end.

Löwy (2006) reads Benjamin's text as an irreducible synthesis of libertarian Marxism and Jewish messianism — neither orthodox historical materialism nor pure theology — situating each thesis in its political-historical context of the Nazi-Soviet Pact and the defeat of the European left. Bohn (2019) engages the contested question of Benjamin's theological dimension directly: against secular Marxist critics within the Frankfurt School itself (Horkheimer, Tiedemann) who dismissed the messianic element as regressive or aestheticizing, Bohn argues that only by embracing the theological dimension can the radical ethical-political implications of Eingedenken (remembrance) be grasped. The dispute about whether Benjamin's messianism is a theological remnant to be excised or a structural requirement of his anti-progressivism is itself an intra-Frankfurt School dispute about what resources the tradition possesses for resisting speculative teleology.

### 3.3 Habermas, Honneth, and the Post-Metaphysical Turn

Habermas represents a paradigm shift within the tradition rather than a continuation of its founding logic. In *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity* (Habermas 1987), he reconstructs the entire trajectory from Hegel through Adorno and poststructuralism as a series of failed attempts to resolve or escape the aporias of Enlightenment reason, each collapsing into either a philosophy of origins (Heidegger) or a performative self-contradiction (Adorno, Derrida). Against these, Habermas proposes a "paradigm shift" from subject-centered reason to communicative reason grounded in the pragmatics of linguistic interaction. Speculative philosophy of history as a project must be abandoned; what survives is a post-metaphysical social theory whose normative claims are grounded in the presuppositions of communicative action rather than in a philosophy of historical development. The performative-contradiction charge will be examined more fully in Section 4, where it functions as the central Habermasian counter to poststructuralism.

Honneth's recognition theory extends this post-metaphysical trajectory while retaining the tradition's characteristic diagnostic ambition. In "A Social Pathology of Reason" (Honneth 2004) and *Pathologies of Reason* (Honneth 2009), Honneth proposes reconstructing critical theory as the identification of "pathologies of reason" — distortions in forms of social rationality — by reference to norms already operative in social practices. This moves the tradition decisively away from speculative philosophy of history, grounding normative critique immanently rather than teleologically. The trajectory from Hegel through Adorno and Habermas to Honneth is one of progressive distancing from speculative history while retaining, in each case, something of the dialectical heritage.

### 3.4 Does Critical Theory Escape the Progress Narrative?

Whether the Frankfurt School's successive self-revisions actually escape speculative history's logic is contested by a significant body of recent work. Allen's *The End of Progress* (Allen 2016) mounts the most systematic challenge: even Habermas's and Honneth's post-metaphysical reconstructions remain implicitly committed to a progressivist normative framework. By grounding normative claims in what counts as "more developed" social rationality or as accumulated learning, they presuppose a developmental teleology that is not innocent of colonial modernity. The decolonial genealogical critique that Allen draws from Foucault and postcolonial theory aims to decolonize critical theory by abandoning this developmental logic while retaining the project of normative critique. This argument bridges directly to Section 5, where Allen and Vázquez-Arroyo (2018) are examined as part of a broader decolonial extension of the critique to the Frankfurt School's heirs: Vázquez-Arroyo argues that even the claim that all thought is historically conditioned — the "historicity of thought" — is itself deployed within critical theory in ways that privilege European historical experience as the privileged site of genuinely critical reflection.

Reynolds (2021) occupies a more ambivalent position, arguing that Adorno and Horkheimer's social theory contains notions of cultivation and resistance immanent to the negative totality that resist easy assimilation to crypto-progressivism — without, however, fully dissolving Allen's concern. Giannakakis (2021) pursues a related line, arguing that Adorno's concept of the "historical adequacy of consciousness" provides resources for a non-teleological engagement with events that shape historical awareness, offering a fragile but real alternative to both speculative progress and simple negation.

These defenses are placed under pressure from a different direction by de Caux (2024), whose analysis of "real normative antinomies" in Adorno's *Minima Moralia* attempts to dissolve the normative-foundations problem directly: critique can acknowledge the impossibility of a secure normative standpoint while still functioning as critique, because the antinomies themselves provide a dialectical foothold — neither a stable foundation nor a collapse into moral skepticism. Whether this resolution is successful, or whether it merely defers the problem, remains the section's central unresolved tension.

A further challenge comes from within Hegel scholarship rather than from the tradition's critics. Heller (2025) argues that *Dialectic of Enlightenment* misreads Hegel's concept of utility in the *Phenomenology of Spirit*: Horkheimer and Adorno identify it with pure instrumentalization, but Hegel's "troop" (*Trupp*) actually describes a mode of interaction that takes account of individual heterogeneity. If this reading is correct, the Frankfurt School's founding critique of speculative reason may be partly directed at a caricature of Hegel — a point that connects to the recurring thread in this review that many critiques target a metaphysical Hegel whose status as the actual target is itself contested (see Introduction and Section 6). The implication is not that the Frankfurt School's critique fails, but that its force depends partly on which Hegel it engages.

The Frankfurt School thus leaves the central normative question unresolved: whether critical theory can sustain its authority — its claim to identify genuine distortions of reason and genuine suffering — without implicitly presupposing some version of the speculative historical framework it has spent the better part of a century dismantling.
