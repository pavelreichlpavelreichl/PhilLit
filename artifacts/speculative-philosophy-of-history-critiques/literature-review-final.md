## Introduction

The literature on the philosophy of history has long been organized by a fundamental disciplinary division. W. H. Walsh (1951) introduced the now-canonical distinction between "speculative" (or "substantive") philosophy of history and "critical" philosophy of history. Speculative philosophy asks what history means as a whole — it claims to discern history's direction, telos, or organizing principle; critical philosophy, by contrast, asks second-order epistemological questions about how historians know, explain, and organize their material. This review maps the critical field that has grown up in response to the speculative tradition so defined: the families of objection, the positions they attack, and the defenses and rehabilitations that have contested their conclusions.

The paradigmatic specimen of speculative philosophy of history is Hegel's account in the *Lectures on the Philosophy of World History*. There, Hegel presents history as the self-actualization of *Geist* (Spirit) toward self-conscious freedom (Hegel 1975). History is not a random accumulation of events but exhibits rational structure: the World Spirit progressively realizes itself through the spirits of particular peoples (*Volksgeister*), guided by the "cunning of reason" — the mechanism by which individual passions and ambitions serve purposes that transcend the agents pursuing them. World-historical individuals, the Napoleons and Caesars of history, are thus instruments of a rationality they do not themselves fully comprehend. The sequence is geographically and culturally determinate: from the Oriental world, where only one is free, through the Greek and Roman worlds, to the Germanic world, where all are recognized as free in principle. This constitutive teleology — history understood as the actual self-realization of rational Spirit — defines what the critics are arguing against.

A decisive internal distinction within the speculative tradition, however, runs between Hegel's constitutive teleology and the regulative teleology Kant proposes in his "Idea for a Universal History with a Cosmopolitan Aim." For Kant, nature can be read *as if* it is guiding humanity through unsocial sociability toward a cosmopolitan legal order (Kant 1784). This historical teleology is explicitly regulative rather than constitutive: we cannot know that nature pursues this purpose, but adopting the perspective as a heuristic allows us to detect rational threads in what would otherwise appear as contingent chaos. As Henry Allison (2009) argues, Kantian historical teleology is grounded in the reflective judgment of the third *Critique* rather than in constitutive theoretical reason — it is a principle of orientation in action, not a claim about historical mechanism. Pauline Kleingeld (1999) further shows that Kant's teleology is primarily a theory of moral development, concerned with the gradual institutionalization of conditions for moral self-legislation. This regulative/constitutive distinction is not merely taxonomic. Several of the critiques surveyed in this review bear exclusively or most forcefully on constitutive teleology; they lose significant traction when directed at the more modest Kantian variant.

A second contested question concerns the self-understanding of the Hegelian tradition itself. The critical field has never agreed on what it is targeting. Frederick Beiser (2005) defends a metaphysical reading of Hegel: the idealism involves substantive ontological commitments — the identity of thought and being, the objective rationality of the natural and social world — and the philosophy of history is genuinely committed to *Geist* as a real, developing whole. Against this, Robert Pippin (1989) argues that Hegel's idealism should be understood as completing Kant's transcendental project rather than reverting to pre-critical metaphysics: rational self-determination is an inherently social and historical achievement, but this is not a commitment to strong metaphysical teleology in the traditional sense. Terry Pinkard (2017) extends this non-metaphysical, normative-practical reading: Hegel's philosophy of history is a theory of the historical shapes of justice, in which history "makes sense" not because Spirit mechanically drives it forward but because agents navigate breakdowns in normative life, generating new ethical communities through lived practice. This Beiser/Pippin/Pinkard dispute is not merely an internal interpretive quarrel. It determines what the target of any given critique actually is. Critics who charge Hegel with unverifiable metaphysical speculation or theological obscurantism are typically engaging the metaphysical Hegel that Beiser reconstructs; defenders in the non-metaphysical tradition frequently reply that such critics are attacking a caricature. This dynamic recurs across the critical field and is flagged throughout the review wherever a critique's force depends on a particular reading.

This review is organized into six sections, each corresponding to a family of objections defined by type of ground rather than by disciplinary provenance. Section 1 examines epistemological-methodological objections from analytic philosophy of history and from the anti-historicism of Popper, Hayek, and Berlin — critiques that charge speculative history with being unscientific, logically incoherent, or prophetically confused. Section 2 presents the hermeneutic-historicist alternative, which rejects speculative history not for failing to be scientific but for misconstruing historical knowledge as nomological or teleological rather than interpretive and particularizing. Section 3 turns to the immanent-dialectical critique mounted from within the Hegelian-Marxist tradition, through the Frankfurt School's ambivalent inheritance — a critique that deploys the dialectic against speculative progress while remaining shaped by it. Section 4 surveys the anti-foundational assault of poststructuralism and postmodernism, centering on Lyotard's rejection of grand narratives and Foucault's genealogical alternative, and their confrontation with Habermas's performative-contradiction counter. Section 5 addresses the political-ethical objections of postcolonial, decolonial, and feminist critics, who argue that speculative history's universalism is constitutively Eurocentric, racially hierarchical, and gendered in its exclusions. Section 6 presents the narrativist reframing that reclassifies speculative history as emplotment rather than cognitive inquiry, alongside the defenses and rehabilitations — non-metaphysical, regulative, neo-speculative, and decolonial — that claim elements of the tradition survive the accumulated criticisms.

Two structural features organize the field and are carried throughout. First, nearly all critical traditions converge in rejecting constitutive teleology, totalizing historical wholes, and Eurocentric universalism — but they diverge sharply on the grounds of these rejections and on whether anything survives them. Second, the metaphysical/non-metaphysical reading debate shapes the force of almost every critique. These convergences and divergences are synthesized in the Conclusion.

## Section 1: The Epistemological-Methodological Objection: Analytic Philosophy of History and the Anti-Historicism of the Philosophy of Social Science

The first major family of critiques against the speculative tradition targets its epistemological and methodological ambitions: the claim that history as a whole can be explained, and its direction rationally discerned. This family encompasses two related but distinct traditions — the analytic philosophy of history that professionalized the distinction between "critical" and "speculative" inquiry, and the anti-historicist strand in the philosophy of social science associated with Popper, Hayek, and Berlin. Both mount epistemological objections to speculative history; yet they rest on different premises and reach only partially overlapping conclusions. Crucially, this entire family accepts the unity of scientific method, differing in this respect from the hermeneutic tradition (Section 2), which rejects that very unity as the source of the error.

### 1.1 The Analytic Critique: Covering Laws, Narrative, and the Impossibility of Narrating the Future

The institutional framework for the analytic critique was established by Walsh's distinction between "speculative" (or substantive) and "critical" philosophy of history (Walsh 1951). Speculative philosophy — as exemplified by Hegel, Marx, Toynbee, and Spengler — asks what history means as a whole and claims to discern its direction or goal; critical philosophy asks second-order epistemological questions about the structure of historical knowledge and explanation. Walsh's framing delegitimized speculative history as overreaching, and the subsequent analytic literature largely accepted the distinction as foundational.

The positivist challenge came first and most sharply from Hempel's covering-law model. Hempel argues that genuinely explanatory historical accounts must conform to the deductive-nomological structure: an explanandum is explained when it follows from general laws together with antecedent conditions (Hempel 1942; Hempel and Oppenheim 1948). Historical accounts that cannot state explicit universal laws produce only "explanation sketches" — incomplete surrogates that fall short of genuine scientific explanation. The implication for speculative history is direct: teleological explanations that invoke Hegel's Geist or Kant's purposive history substitute purposive narrative for subsumption under empirical law, and thus fail the D-N standard entirely.

The most formally rigorous analytic argument against speculative history, however, comes from Danto. His concept of "narrative sentences" — sentences describing an earlier event in terms of a later one unknown to contemporaries — shows that historical description is constitutively retrospective (Danto 1965). A perfect "Ideal Chronicler" narrating the present in real time could never write such sentences, because they presuppose the completion of processes not yet finished. The speculative ambition to narrate history's future direction — the endpoint of Geist's self-actualization, the telos of Kantian progress — does not involve prophecy but rather a logical confusion about the tense-structure of historical description. Uebel's reconstruction of the trajectory of Danto's argument shows that the 1965 position was a "reasoned retreat" from historical idealism rather than an anticipation of postmodern anti-realism, clarifying that Danto's critique is analytically distinctive (Uebel 2019).

Reactions against Hempel's model carved out a "critical" middle ground that rejected both positivist scientism and Hegelian teleology. Dray defends a "rational explanation" model: to explain a historical agent's action is to show it was the appropriate response to their situation given their beliefs and goals — an account that makes no use of general empirical laws while also refusing to invoke transpersonal teleological forces (Dray 1957). Mink argues that historical comprehension is irreducibly "configurational" — it grasps events as a whole by following them from start to finish, retroactively organizing them into a unity that cannot be restated as a series of causal propositions (Mink 1970). Mandelbaum, more skeptical of narrativist claims, defends "genetic explanation" as the appropriate form of historical analysis, irreducible to nomological subsumption yet distinct from speculative narrative (Mandelbaum 1961). Gallie's thesis of "essentially contested concepts" adds a further weapon: master-concepts of speculative history — "freedom," "reason," "progress," "Spirit" — are by nature perpetually disputed and cannot be given the determinate content that Hegel's scheme requires (Gallie 1956). Beaney's retrospective survey charts how this analytic subdiscipline rose to professional dominance through the 1970s before being marginalized by hermeneutic and narrativist approaches, a historical arc that frames the trajectory of the epistemological critique (Beaney 2016).

### 1.2 Anti-Historicism in the Philosophy of Social Science: Popper, Hayek, Berlin

A second, partially independent family of epistemological objections comes from the philosophy of social science. Here the target is "historicism" in Popper's sense: the view that the principal task of the social sciences is to discover inexorable laws of historical development enabling long-range historical prophecy.

Popper's central argument in *The Poverty of Historicism* is logical rather than empirical (Popper 1957). Since the growth of knowledge cannot be predicted — to know now what we will know in the future would be to possess that knowledge already — and since history is causally dependent on the growth of knowledge, historical development is in principle unpredictable. Speculative philosophies of history that claim to discern history's direction, Hegel and Marx above all, are therefore not merely empirically unsupported but self-defeating. *The Open Society and Its Enemies* extends this argument into political philosophy, tracing the intellectual ancestry of totalitarianism to precisely the holistic and historicist traditions — Plato, Hegel, Marx — that subordinate individuals to the logic of historical wholes (Popper 1945). Popper explicitly accepts the unity of scientific method; his charge is not that historicists apply natural-science categories to social phenomena but that they fail by the standards of their own empiricist ambitions.

Hayek's critique proceeds from a different angle. In *The Counter-Revolution of Science*, Hayek targets "scientism": the uncritical transfer of natural-science methods to social phenomena, which are constituted by the meanings, plans, and expectations of individual agents and cannot be captured by the third-person observational stance of natural science (Hayek 1952). The resulting "constructivist rationalism" — the belief that social order can be rationally designed — is the intellectual engine of socialism and speculative social engineering. Hayek's *Studies on the Abuse and Decline of Reason* extends this critique, showing how the anti-scientism argument connects to the defense of spontaneous orders against rationally designed ones (Hayek 2010). Costa and Angeli's recent reconstruction demonstrates that the concept of complexity is the unifying theme running through Hayek's methodological writings, explaining why natural-science methods applied to social phenomena are bound to produce distortion (Costa and Angeli 2021).

Berlin's contribution is moral rather than purely epistemological. "Historical Inevitability," collected in *Four Essays on Liberty*, argues that any determinist account of history — Marxist, Hegelian, positivist, or providentialist — is incompatible with the ordinary moral vocabulary of praise, blame, and responsibility, which presupposes that agents could have acted otherwise (Berlin 1969). Since historians are not mere chroniclers but evaluative narrators, historical determinism would require abandoning not just historical judgment but ordinary moral discourse. Watkins provides the positive counterpart to these critiques by articulating methodological individualism: social events must ultimately be explained by reference to the dispositions and beliefs of individual human beings, not by Hegelian Volksgeist or Marxist structural laws (Watkins 1957). Lukes identifies a serious internal problem in this positive program, arguing that the non-trivial versions of methodological individualism are incoherent because they rely on social-level concepts to characterize the very individuals they invoke (Lukes 1968).

### 1.3 Internal Tensions and the Straw-Man Charge

The epistemological critique from Popper, Hayek, and Berlin is not without its own critics, and the debate exposes important ambiguities about what the target actually is.

Passmore argues that Popper's use of "historicism" is too sweeping and poorly defined, conflating at least three distinct positions: a theory about the aims of social science, a way of practicing it, and a comprehensive philosophical worldview (Passmore 1975). When examined precisely, Popper's logical argument against long-range prediction turns out to target only one of these, and imprecisely at that. Urbach presses harder, maintaining that none of Popper's arguments against historicism is in fact valid: the "growth of knowledge" argument depends on a questionable premise about the impossibility of self-prediction, the argument from the uniqueness of historical events would also rule out astronomy and meteorology, and the argument from social holism conflates two distinct theses (Urbach 1978). Suchting raises a related objection specifically for Marx, arguing that Popper systematically mischaracterizes Marx's actual theory of historical development (Suchting 1972).

Most pointedly, Keaney argues that the "historicism" attacked by Popper is largely a fictional construct (Keaney 1997). Genuine historicism — associated with the German Historical School — does not posit deterministic laws of development at all; it insists on the individuality and context-dependence of historical phenomena, the very opposite of what Popper attacks. By conflating this with speculative developmental-law theories, Popper creates a straw man that discredits a valuable tradition of historical contextualism. This observation carries implications beyond Popper's argument: it raises the possibility that the Popperian target is a construct distinct not only from genuine historicism but also from Hegel's actual philosophy of history, which defenders in the non-metaphysical tradition (Pippin 1989; Pinkard 2017) argue is not committed to the predictive developmental-law program Popper attacks.

Maar's analysis of Berlin shows a comparable vulnerability: Berlin's incompatibilist argument that determinism evacuates moral responsibility was available to standard compatibilist responses that he does not adequately address (Maar 2020). The assumption that historical determinism requires the strong incompatibilist reading is itself contestable.

Against the dominant critical consensus, Fain represents a minority view within analytic philosophy: speculative concerns — with history's overall meaning, with patterns of historical progress, with the philosophical significance of large historical sequences — are not logically confused but philosophically legitimate, and the Walsh distinction between critical and speculative philosophy of history is not as stable as its proponents assume (Fain 1970). Beaney's retrospective account confirms that the analytic consensus eventually weakened, as hermeneutic and narrativist approaches displaced the critical tradition's dominance after the 1970s (Beaney 2016).

The epistemological-methodological family of critiques is thus internally divided. The analytic anti-speculative and anti-narrative wings disagree about whether narrative comprehension (Mink, Gallie) or genetic-causal analysis (Mandelbaum) is the appropriate alternative to both positivism and speculation. The Popperian tradition's positive methodology — methodological individualism — faces internal difficulties (Lukes 1968), and the reconciliationist framework developed by List and Spiekermann (2013) suggests the individualism-holism dichotomy that animated the original debate has been partially dissolved. What unifies the family, and what distinguishes it sharply from the hermeneutic tradition that follows in Section 2, is its acceptance of the unity of scientific method: the charge against speculative history is that it fails by the standards of legitimate inquiry that this family shares with natural science, not that the standard itself is misconceived.

## Section 2: The Hermeneutic-Historicist Alternative: Understanding Against Nomological-Teleological Schemes

Where the epistemological critiques examined in Section 1 challenge speculative history for failing to meet the standards of scientific explanation, the hermeneutic and historicist traditions mount a structurally different objection: they deny that those standards apply to historical knowledge in the first place. This is not a complaint that Hegel's teleology is unscientific — it is a rejection of the unity of scientific method that makes such a complaint possible. For the hermeneutic tradition, historical knowledge is irreducibly interpretive and particularizing, embedded in tradition and oriented toward meaning rather than law. Nomological and teleological schemes alike mistake the kind of understanding that history requires.

### 2.1 Verstehen and the Autonomy of the Human Sciences

The founding gesture of this tradition is Dilthey's distinction between the Geisteswissenschaften (human sciences) and the Naturwissenschaften (natural sciences). In his *Introduction to the Human Sciences*, Dilthey (1989) argues that the objects of historical inquiry — human expressions, actions, and institutions — are constituted by meaning and must be grasped from within through understanding (*Verstehen*), not explained causally from without. All human phenomena are historical through and through, which means that any positivist account of history is not merely incomplete but self-undermining: it abstracts away the very dimension that makes historical objects what they are. This critique applies with equal force to speculative idealism: Hegel's metaphysical schemas are, for Dilthey, pre-critical impositions that bypass the empirical labor of understanding in favor of a priori construction.

In his mature hermeneutics, Dilthey (2002) develops the concept of *Wirkungszusammenhang* (effective context) and the hermeneutic circle as structural features of all historical understanding — arguing that the Geisteswissenschaften can produce genuine objective knowledge, not by imitating natural-scientific methods, but by reconstructing the coherent nexus of meaning that structures historical life-expressions. Structural coherence, on this view, is achievable through hermeneutic methods without positing a trans-historical *Geist* to carry the weight.

Contemporary philosophers have sustained and refined these claims. Harrington (2000) defends the Verstehen/Erklären distinction against both positivist collapse (the view that the distinction is merely pragmatic) and hermeneutic inflation (the view that understanding is wholly incommensurable with explanation), arguing that *Verstehen* retains a legitimate epistemic role that cannot be absorbed into hypothesis-testing. Stueber (2012) reconstructs the tradition along "re-enactivist" lines: understanding other minds requires activating one's own mentalistic capacities to simulate the reasons of agents, a genuine epistemic process irreducible to nomological subsumption. Taylor (1985) bridges the continental Verstehen tradition and analytic philosophy of social science, arguing that social practices and institutions only exist through the meanings agents give them, which means that causal-law models systematically misidentify their subject matter. Patton (2022) traces how Dilthey's mature hermeneutics represents a decisive break from the naturalistic *Völkerpsychologie* program, showing that Dilthey's Verstehen is ultimately a logical-structural achievement rather than a psychological one. Taken together, these contributions establish that the hermeneutic critique of nomological history retains philosophical credentials independent of its nineteenth-century formulations.

### 2.2 Collingwood's Re-Enactment and the Critique of Substantive Philosophy of History

R. G. Collingwood's *The Idea of History* (1946) develops the anti-speculative argument within a specifically idealist framework. The re-enactment thesis holds that historical understanding consists in the historian re-thinking the thoughts of past agents in their own mind: history is the science of mind, and its proper object — past thought — can only be grasped through imaginative reconstruction. This makes substantive philosophy of history — the imposition of developmental patterns, teleological sequences, or civilizational cycles from outside — a category error. Collingwood condemns what he calls "scissors-and-paste history," which assembles testimonies without genuinely re-enacting the thought behind them, and by extension any scheme that substitutes a philosopher's developmental logic for the historian's reconstructive task. The critique applies directly to Toynbee, Marx, and Hegel: they mistake a philosophical construction for historical knowledge.

D'Oro (2000) has shown that the re-enactment doctrine is best understood as a logical rather than psychological thesis: the historian re-enacts the *same* thought as the past agent, not a psychologically similar thought. This matters for the anti-speculative argument because it grounds historical knowledge in the identity of propositional content across time, not in empathetic projection — making it genuinely cognitive while remaining irreducibly individual. Robson (2023) revisits this distinction through the act/content relation, arguing that Collingwood's account of re-enactment as "acts of thought" provides a coherent middle ground between pure psychological empathy and purely logical identity, defending the doctrine against objections that have accumulated since D'Oro's influential reading. Guyer (2017) draws a parallel between re-enactment in the philosophy of history and Collingwood's account of artistic imagination, suggesting that the reconstructive capacities at work are not mysterious but continuous with the imaginative-constructive competences familiar from aesthetics.

Fristedt (2025) situates the re-enactment thesis in comparison with Gadamer's philosophical hermeneutics, arguing that both Collingwood and Gadamer understand historical knowledge as a form of self-knowledge: understanding the past is inseparable from understanding oneself. Where Collingwood grounds this in the re-enactment of individual thought, Gadamer grounds it in effective-historical consciousness and the fusion of horizons. Fristedt argues for a productive synthesis that preserves Collingwood's focus on individual thought while incorporating Gadamer's insight about tradition — a synthesis that would sharpen the anti-speculative case by uniting the British idealist and continental hermeneutic traditions.

### 2.3 Gadamer, Tradition, and the Problem of Historicism

Gadamer's *Truth and Method* (1989) advances the hermeneutic alternative to its most radical point. Understanding, on Gadamer's account, is not a methodological procedure but an ontological event of participation in tradition. The concept of *Wirkungsgeschichte* (effective-historical consciousness) establishes that interpreters are always already shaped by the tradition they seek to understand: there is no Archimedean standpoint outside history from which to survey the whole. The "fusion of horizons" (*Horizontverschmelzung*) is the model for how understanding actually works, replacing the ideal of methodological objectivity. This directly targets the Hegelian pretension to absolute knowledge: Hegel's claim to a standpoint from which the self-actualization of *Geist* can be narrated as a completed whole presupposes precisely the trans-historical vantage point that Gadamer's account forecloses. Warnke (1987) elaborates this challenge, examining the tensions in Gadamer between the rehabilitation of tradition and the requirements of critical reason, and arguing that Gadamer's account of prejudice as enabling rather than distorting is more defensible than Habermas's ideology-critique allows — though Gadamer requires further resources for distinguishing between legitimate and illegitimate tradition.

The hermeneutic tradition's deepest difficulty, however, is the problem of relativism. If all understanding is tradition-embedded and perspectival, what becomes of the speculative philosopher's universalist ambitions? The difficulty is not new: it recurs as the "crisis of historicism," the concern that the historicist commitment to the individual and the historically conditioned threatens to undermine normative validity altogether. Megill (1997) argues that this crisis — prominent from roughly 1900 to 1930 — was not primarily a logical crisis about self-refutation but a practical-cultural crisis precipitated by World War I and the collapse of belief in Western civilizational progress. The philosophical problem was real but was given urgency by historical disruption. Kinzel (2016) traces the Neo-Kantian attempt to resolve this tension through Windelband, whose ideographic/nomothetic distinction sought to protect value-claims from historical contingency by grounding them in a transcendental notion of normative validity — a solution Kinzel shows to be ultimately unstable given Windelband's own historicist commitments. Woodford (2012) examines how Charles Taylor negotiates the same structural dilemma in his account of "strong evaluation": Taylor seeks to defend the significance of irreducibly qualitative moral distinctions while acknowledging that the frameworks for such evaluation are historically situated, a position structurally similar to Gadamer's and similarly vulnerable to the charge of historicist circularity.

The relativism problem bears directly on the hermeneutic critique of speculative history: if historicism merely relativizes all value-schemes, including Hegel's, it does not so much refute speculative universalism as displace it into one perspective among others. The question is whether this displacement is a refutation or a stand-off. Beiser (2011) complicates the opposition by arguing that Hegel should be read not as the opponent of historicism but as a historicist himself — that his speculative scheme is an attempt to ground historical meaning immanently rather than in a trans-historical metaphysics. On this revisionist reading, the hermeneutic tradition and Hegelian speculative history share more than either side acknowledges: both ground meaning in historical development rather than timeless essences. If Beiser is right, the critique of Hegel as a naïve universalist who simply imposes a trans-historical *Geist* onto historical particulars may be misdirected — and the real debate is about whether immanent historical grounding can avoid relativism, not about whether Hegel is guilty of a category error the historicists have already exposed.

The upshot of Section 2 is that the hermeneutic-historicist alternative offers a genuinely different epistemological ground for rejecting speculative history from the analytic and anti-historicist critiques of Section 1. It does not appeal to the logic of scientific explanation or the impossibility of historical prediction; it challenges the very conception of historical knowledge that both positivism and speculative idealism share — the assumption that history yields general truths by transcending the particular. Yet the tradition is internally divided over whether its anti-universalism collapses into relativism, and Beiser's recontextualization of Hegel within historicism suggests that the opposition between hermeneutic particularism and speculative universalism may itself require revision.

## Section 3: The Immanent-Dialectical Critique: The Frankfurt School's Ambivalent Inheritance

What distinguishes the Frankfurt School's engagement with speculative philosophy of history from the external dismissals catalogued in Sections 1 and 2 is its fundamentally immanent character. The analytic objection charges speculative history with logical incoherence; the hermeneutic objection charges it with epistemological overreach. The Frankfurt School, by contrast, prosecutes its critique from within the Hegelian-Marxist heritage itself, turning the dialectic against its own teleological inheritance. This internal position is both the tradition's greatest philosophical strength and the source of its most persistent difficulty: if speculative reason is compromised, from where does the critique draw its own normative authority?

### 3.1 Dialectic of Enlightenment and Negative Dialectics

The methodological framework for Frankfurt School critique is laid down in Horkheimer's 1937 essay "Traditional and Critical Theory," collected in his *Critical Theory: Selected Essays* (Horkheimer 1972). Horkheimer argues that traditional theory — including Hegelian speculative idealism no less than positivism — abstracts from the social relations in which thought is embedded and thereby affirms the existing order by presenting it as rational necessity. Critical theory, by contrast, maintains a reflexive relation to society as a dynamic totality oriented toward emancipation. The implicit charge against Hegel is that the Phenomenology's narrative of Spirit achieving self-knowledge in the modern state performs precisely the affirmative function Horkheimer diagnoses: it ratifies the present as the telos of world history.

Horkheimer and Adorno radicalize this critique in *Dialectic of Enlightenment* (Horkheimer and Adorno 2002). Their argument is that reason itself — including the speculative reason that identifies the rational with the real — harbors the structure of domination it claims to have overcome. The drive toward conceptual mastery and quantification turns against itself, regressing into new mythic forms. Any philosophy of history that sees in reason a progressive force is caught within this self-undermining dialectic; the Hegelian identification of world history with reason's self-realization participates in the very pathology it purports to diagnose. Hohendahl (2013) has reconstructed Adorno's more developed treatment of progress in dialogue with Augustine, Kant, and Benjamin, showing that Adorno holds a dialectical rather than simply negative concept: he neither endorses progress nor simply rejects it, but insists on its inner contradiction as irreducible — making any teleological resolution premature and ideological.

Adorno's *Negative Dialectics* (Adorno 1973) provides the most sustained philosophical confrontation with Hegel in the tradition. Adorno reconstructs Hegelian dialectics as "identity thinking" — the compulsion to subsume particulars under concepts in ways that do violence to their nonidentity with the concept. Speculative philosophy of history is, on this account, the supreme institutional expression of identity thinking: universal history as the self-realization of Spirit totalizes suffering by assigning it a redemptive function, making catastrophe meaningful by absorbing it into a narrative of progress. Adorno's explicit formulation — that to write speculative philosophy of history after Auschwitz is obscene — is the sharpest edge of this critique. Pocius (2023) has provided a technically precise account of how Adorno's procedure is itself dialectical: the nonidentity concept sits between Kant and Hegel, prolonging Kant's critique through sociohistorical reconstruction while using Hegel's own instruments against Hegel's speculative closure. The critique is immanent, not external. Reynolds (2021), drawing on Allen's analysis, extends this reading by showing that Adorno and Horkheimer's social theory requires notions of cultivation and learning that imply a residual concept of progressive development, even while their explicit position denies any teleological resolution — a tension the review will return to below.

Against the dominant critical thrust within the School, Marcuse's *Reason and Revolution* (Marcuse 1941) occupies a different position: a sympathetic retrieval of Hegel's critical-negative potential against positivism and fascism. For Marcuse, the Hegelian dialectic is not primarily a theodicy of progress but a tool for disclosing the negativity immanent to every social form — its kinship with Marx lying precisely in this critical-negative structure. Marcuse's reading establishes the internal tension within the Frankfurt School's Hegel reception: Adorno reads Hegel's identity-thinking as a philosophical disease to be diagnosed, while Marcuse reads the dialectic's negativity as a resource to be recovered.

### 3.2 Benjamin's Messianic Critique of Progress

Walter Benjamin's "On the Concept of History" (Benjamin 1940) contributes a philosophically distinctive anti-progressivist argument that complements but cannot be reduced to Adorno's. Against historicism's "homogeneous, empty time" — the temporal medium through which progress is conceived as gradual accumulation — Benjamin articulates a messianic conception of historical time in which the present possesses a "weak messianic power" to redeem suppressed pasts by blasting open the continuum of history rather than consummating it. The angel of history, swept into the future while gazing at accumulating wreckage, figures the incompatibility of progressive teleology with the reality of catastrophe. Revolutionary action is a "tiger's leap" into the past, not a forward movement toward a predetermined end.

Löwy (2006) reads Benjamin's text as an irreducible synthesis of libertarian Marxism and Jewish messianism — neither orthodox historical materialism nor pure theology — situating each thesis in its political-historical context of the Nazi-Soviet Pact and the defeat of the European left. Bohn (2019) engages the contested question of Benjamin's theological dimension directly: against secular Marxist critics within the Frankfurt School itself (Horkheimer, Tiedemann) who dismissed the messianic element as regressive or aestheticizing, Bohn argues that only by embracing the theological dimension can the radical ethical-political implications of Eingedenken (remembrance) be grasped. The dispute about whether Benjamin's messianism is a theological remnant to be excised or a structural requirement of his anti-progressivism is itself an intra-Frankfurt School dispute about what resources the tradition possesses for resisting speculative teleology.

### 3.3 Habermas, Honneth, and the Post-Metaphysical Turn

Habermas represents a paradigm shift within the tradition rather than a continuation of its founding logic. In *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity* (Habermas 1987), he reconstructs the entire trajectory from Hegel through Adorno and poststructuralism as a series of failed attempts to resolve or escape the aporias of Enlightenment reason, each collapsing into either a philosophy of origins (Heidegger) or a performative self-contradiction (Adorno, Derrida). Against these, Habermas proposes a "paradigm shift" from subject-centered reason to communicative reason grounded in the pragmatics of linguistic interaction. Speculative philosophy of history as a project must be abandoned; what survives is a post-metaphysical social theory whose normative claims are grounded in the presuppositions of communicative action rather than in a philosophy of historical development. The performative-contradiction charge will be examined more fully in Section 4, where it functions as the central Habermasian counter to poststructuralism.

Honneth's recognition theory extends this post-metaphysical trajectory while retaining the tradition's characteristic diagnostic ambition. In "A Social Pathology of Reason" (Honneth 2004) and *Pathologies of Reason* (Honneth 2009), Honneth proposes reconstructing critical theory as the identification of "pathologies of reason" — distortions in forms of social rationality — by reference to norms already operative in social practices. This moves the tradition decisively away from speculative philosophy of history, grounding normative critique immanently rather than teleologically. The trajectory from Hegel through Adorno and Habermas to Honneth is one of progressive distancing from speculative history while retaining, in each case, something of the dialectical heritage.

### 3.4 Does Critical Theory Escape the Progress Narrative?

Whether the Frankfurt School's successive self-revisions actually escape speculative history's logic is contested by a significant body of recent work. Allen's *The End of Progress* (Allen 2016) mounts the most systematic challenge: even Habermas's and Honneth's post-metaphysical reconstructions remain implicitly committed to a progressivist normative framework. By grounding normative claims in what counts as "more developed" social rationality or as accumulated learning, they presuppose a developmental teleology that is not innocent of colonial modernity. The decolonial genealogical critique that Allen draws from Foucault and postcolonial theory aims to decolonize critical theory by abandoning this developmental logic while retaining the project of normative critique. This argument bridges directly to Section 5, where Allen and Vázquez-Arroyo (2018) are examined as part of a broader decolonial extension of the critique to the Frankfurt School's heirs: Vázquez-Arroyo argues that even the claim that all thought is historically conditioned — the "historicity of thought" — is itself deployed within critical theory in ways that privilege European historical experience as the privileged site of genuinely critical reflection.

Reynolds (2021) occupies a more ambivalent position, arguing that Adorno and Horkheimer's social theory contains notions of cultivation and resistance immanent to the negative totality that resist easy assimilation to crypto-progressivism — without, however, fully dissolving Allen's concern. Giannakakis (2021) pursues a related line, arguing that Adorno's concept of the "historical adequacy of consciousness" provides resources for a non-teleological engagement with events that shape historical awareness, offering a fragile but real alternative to both speculative progress and simple negation.

These defenses are placed under pressure from a different direction by de Caux (2024), whose analysis of "real normative antinomies" in Adorno's *Minima Moralia* attempts to dissolve the normative-foundations problem directly: critique can acknowledge the impossibility of a secure normative standpoint while still functioning as critique, because the antinomies themselves provide a dialectical foothold — neither a stable foundation nor a collapse into moral skepticism. Whether this resolution is successful, or whether it merely defers the problem, remains the section's central unresolved tension.

A further challenge comes from within Hegel scholarship rather than from the tradition's critics. Heller (2025) argues that *Dialectic of Enlightenment* misreads Hegel's concept of utility in the *Phenomenology of Spirit*: Horkheimer and Adorno identify it with pure instrumentalization, but Hegel's "troop" (*Trupp*) actually describes a mode of interaction that takes account of individual heterogeneity. If this reading is correct, the Frankfurt School's founding critique of speculative reason may be partly directed at a caricature of Hegel — a point that connects to the recurring thread in this review that many critiques target a metaphysical Hegel whose status as the actual target is itself contested (see Introduction and Section 6). The implication is not that the Frankfurt School's critique fails, but that its force depends partly on which Hegel it engages.

The Frankfurt School thus leaves the central normative question unresolved: whether critical theory can sustain its authority — its claim to identify genuine distortions of reason and genuine suffering — without implicitly presupposing some version of the speculative historical framework it has spent the better part of a century dismantling.

## Section 4: The Anti-Foundational Assault: Poststructuralism and Postmodernism

Where the Frankfurt School attacks speculative history from within the dialectical tradition (see Section 3), the poststructuralist and postmodern assault repudiates the very framework in which such immanent critique operates. Rather than recovering a more adequate reason, these thinkers reject the pretension that reason can serve as the tribunal of history at all. The result is the most far-reaching late-twentieth-century challenge to Hegelian and Kantian teleology — and, as the section's final turn demonstrates, a challenge that generates its own internal contestation.

### 4.1 Lyotard and the Critique of Grand Narratives

Jean-François Lyotard's diagnosis in *The Postmodern Condition* (Lyotard 1984) locates the defining feature of postmodernity in an "incredulity toward metanarratives" — the large-scale legitimating stories, above all Hegel's dialectic of Spirit and Kant's narrative of rational emancipation, by which modern knowledge justified itself. For Lyotard, these are not merely false empirical predictions; they are structures of epistemic and political violence. By subordinating local language games to a single overarching legitimating framework, speculative teleology silences what cannot be expressed in its idiom. Lyotard calls for "paralogical" forms of legitimation that attend to the incommensurability of language games rather than resolving them into unity.

The normative stakes deepen in *The Differend* (Lyotard 1988), where Lyotard argues that after the Shoah no appeal to Hegelian speculative teleology or Marxist emancipation is philosophically or ethically permissible. A "differend" is a wrong that cannot be articulated within the dominant rule of adjudication — the very wrong that speculative history, by positing a universal tribunal of reason, structurally suppresses. Speculative philosophy of history is thus condemned not only as epistemically overreaching but as constitutively unjust.

Luke O'Sullivan's recent revisionist reading (O'Sullivan 2024) complicates this picture. O'Sullivan argues that Lyotard's anti-metanarrative postmodernism is itself a species of philosophical realism — "realist" in the sense of refusing utopian projections onto historical reality. The appearance of Francis Fukuyama's neo-Hegelian "end of history" thesis, O'Sullivan shows, destabilized Lyotard's claim that metanarratives had lost their legitimacy: Fukuyama demonstrated that metanarrative could still serve the ideological requirements of liberal-democratic capitalism. Derrida's response to Fukuyama in *Specters of Marx*, on O'Sullivan's account, represents an alternative engagement with teleological history that is itself not free of metanarrative structure, even if "realist" rather than "utopian" in kind. This complicates any simple narrative of postmodernism as the terminus of the speculative tradition.

### 4.2 Nietzsche, Foucault, and Genealogy as Anti-Teleology

If Lyotard attacks teleology through the theory of language games, Nietzsche and Foucault provide a rival methodological proposal: genealogy, or what Foucault calls "effective history" (*wirkliche Historie*). Nietzsche's "On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life" (1874) is the proto-poststructuralist locus classicus. Young's (2023) reconstruction shows that Nietzsche's target was both the Hegelian developmental narrative — history as the working-out of Spirit toward its own self-knowledge — and the positivist-antiquarian accumulation of facts. Against both, Nietzsche proposes history "in the service of life," organized through the typology of monumental, antiquarian, and critical history. Jensen's (2016) book-length study of the text argues that the critique of teleological and Rankean history is inseparable from Nietzsche's positive account of cultural renewal, and that subsequent appropriations have often flattened this complexity.

Foucault's "Nietzsche, Genealogy, History" (1971) operationalizes what he presents as this Nietzschean inheritance. By replacing the search for *Ursprung* (origin) with attention to *Herkunft* (descent) and *Entstehung* (emergence), Foucault's effective history insists on rupture, contingency, and the implication of all knowledge in power relations — a direct inversion of the developmental continuities presupposed by speculative teleology. Lal (2026) argues that this essay is not merely a methodological preamble to later institutional analyses but an original theoretical event reconstituting the historian as a politically situated, embodied subject answerable to the present rather than to a narrative of progress. Hook (2005) provides a careful exposition of how effective history works against totalizing historical narratives by attending to discontinuity and the knowledge-power nexus, distinguishing it from both positivist and hermeneutic historiography. Elden (2025) traces the transition in Foucault's methodological vocabulary — from archaeology through the intermediate phase of "dynastics" to genealogy — clarifying how the Nietzschean anti-teleological impulse was gradually consolidated in Foucault's later work.

However, the genealogy connecting Nietzsche to Foucault is itself now contested. Manley (2025) argues, through a close reading of Foucault's essay alongside Nietzsche's second *Untimely Meditation*, that Foucault presents a selectively anti-metaphysical Nietzsche — one who is suspicious of all teleological origins and ahistorical forms. The actual Nietzsche, Manley contends, insists on the *necessity* of ahistorical "monumental" and "critical" history as antidotes to historicism. Foucault's anti-teleological Nietzsche is thus a Nietzsche reconstructed in Foucault's own image. Garruzzo (2025) approaches the same problem from a different angle, arguing that genealogy for both Nietzsche and Foucault is defined by the investigation of contingent origins within regimes of power — "tactical knowledge" rather than normative insight — and that any explicitly teleological genealogy is a contradiction in terms. Where Manley emphasizes the divergence between the two thinkers, Garruzzo identifies a more minimal shared core. Together, these recent contributions surface an internal genealogical dispute: the poststructuralist lineage Nietzsche–Foucault may be, as Megill (1985) recognized in tracing the "prophets of extremity," a selective intellectual construction as much as a faithful transmission.

### 4.3 Derrida, Baudrillard, Rorty

Deconstruction's relation to Hegel is more entangled than the genealogical-rupture model suggests. Mistral (2024) shows, through reconstruction of three pre-*Glas* texts, that Derrida consistently acknowledged the irreducibility of Aufhebung even while subjecting it to deconstruction. During the late 1960s, when French intellectual culture was marked by strong anti-Hegelianism, Derrida's position was received as a straightforward rejection of the dialectic; but Derrida himself denied that a clean break from Aufhebung was possible without merely repeating it. Deconstruction, on this reading, is better characterized as a radicalization of the dialectic that pursues what the dialectic cannot sublate than as a refusal of Hegel. This entanglement limits the extent to which deconstruction can serve as a simple external repudiation of speculative history.

Baudrillard (1995) offers a distinct and more radical dissolution. Where Lyotard targets the injustice of metanarrative and Foucault targets its developmental logic, Baudrillard attacks the very referential precondition of all historical teleology. In the condition of "hyperreality," signs no longer refer to a reality that history could track; they refer only to other signs. The teleological order presupposed by speculative philosophy of history — progress toward a rational end — collapses when historical reality itself implodes into simulation. Jenkins (1999) situates Baudrillard's "end of history" within the postmodern critique of historiographical legitimacy, distinguishing it sharply from Fukuyama's liberal-democratic completion: where Fukuyama marks the triumph of reason in history, Baudrillard marks the abolition of the referent that made such a narrative possible.

Rorty's anti-foundationalist pragmatism (Rorty 1989) represents the analytic-continental crossover in this assault on teleology. Rorty argues that there is no ahistorical standpoint from which to adjudicate between vocabularies or to identify the direction of history, and that Hegel's speculative dialectic is a failed attempt to provide such a standpoint. His "liberal ironist" recognizes the contingency of her own final vocabulary without abandoning solidarity — redescription rather than dialectical synthesis, a series of narrative transformations with no *telos*. This position connects poststructuralist anti-foundationalism to the Anglo-American tradition of analytic philosophy, representing it not as a foreign import but as the consequence of taking seriously the lessons of Darwin, Freud, and Wittgenstein.

### 4.4 The Habermasian Counter

Habermas's *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity* (Habermas 1987) constitutes the most sustained counter-position within this section's debate (cross-referencing the discussion in Section 3 of Habermas's post-metaphysical turn). Habermas charges that poststructuralism and postmodernism commit a "performative contradiction": the critique of reason, metanarrative, and modernity's grand narratives presupposes normative concepts — freedom, justice, the validity of critique itself — that the critique simultaneously undermines. A position that denies the normative force of reason cannot coherently advance a critical argument against teleological reason without invoking the very standards it rejects. For Habermas, the poststructuralist assault, deriving from Nietzsche's aestheticization of the critique of reason, is therefore self-defeating.

Habermas defends the emancipatory projects of Hegel and Marx as incomplete rather than exhausted, preserving the normative ambition of the speculative tradition while abandoning its metaphysical architecture in favor of communicative reason. The charge of performative contradiction does not restore speculative teleology — Habermas himself rejects the philosophy of history with practical intent — but it limits the poststructuralist critique's own coherence as a critical enterprise.

The poststructuralist assault thus generates a paradox at its core: a critique that dismantles the grounds for any normative critique. Whether this constitutes a decisive refutation or merely highlights poststructuralism's deliberately non-normative ambitions (Foucault's "tactical knowledge," Baudrillard's simulation analysis) remains contested. What is clear, as the debate across Sections 3 and 4 shows, is that the most powerful late-twentieth-century attacks on speculative history are themselves internally divided — about the Nietzschean inheritance, about whether deconstruction refuses or radicalizes the dialectic, and about whether any critical stance toward the present remains coherent once metanarrative legitimation is abandoned.

## Section 5: The Political-Ethical Objection: Postcolonial, Decolonial, and Feminist Critiques

The critiques examined in the preceding sections proceed largely on epistemological or immanent-dialectical grounds, targeting speculative history's claims to scientific status, its logic of totality, or its developmental teleology. The family of objections addressed here pursues a different diagnosis: that speculative philosophy of history is ideologically compromised at the level of its constitutive categories, not merely in the contingent historical prejudices of its authors. The claim, advanced across postcolonial, decolonial, and feminist traditions, is that the universalizing structure of speculative history — its world-historical sequence, its concept of freedom, its account of recognition — systematically excludes women, colonized peoples, and racially subordinated subjects by incorporating their exclusion into the architecture of the system itself. As the following survey shows, these critiques diverge on whether European categories can be provincially reformed or must be bypassed altogether, and on how deeply the contamination extends — to applications of the freedom concept or to the concept itself.

### 5.1 The Racial Structure Thesis

The pivotal scholarly intervention establishing the seriousness of this family of objections came from Robert Bernasconi (2000), who argued that Hegel's relegation of Africa to prehistory is not a contingent expression of eighteenth-century racial prejudice subsequently separable from the systematic philosophy. Rather, the racial hierarchy built into the Encyclopaedia's treatment of the natural soul — which assigns differential degrees of spiritual capacity to different races — is architecturally connected to the account of how Geist becomes objective in world history. Eurocentrism, on this reading, is load-bearing in the speculative edifice, not decorative.

This argument has been extended and sharpened by subsequent work. Alison Stone (2017) advances what is arguably the strongest form of the critique: it is not Hegel's empirical racial pronouncements that are the problem, but the concept of freedom itself. Stone argues that Hegel's idea of freedom as liberation from nature is internally connected to his characterization of non-European peoples as immersed in nature and therefore unfree. Because Hegel's systematic concept of freedom is defined against this constructed Other, the colonial logic is internal to the principle, not merely its application. Stone concludes that one cannot "save Hegel from himself" by excising the colonial passages while retaining the philosophy of freedom — a conclusion that bears directly on the rehabilitation attempts surveyed in Section 6. Daniel James and Franz Knappik (2025) provide the most comprehensive recent textual study of this entanglement, showing that Hegel's accounts of freedom, personhood, and the lord-bondsman dialectic are deeply intertwined with his views on colonial slavery and race, situating Hegel within neglected contexts including British abolitionism and Scottish stadial theory. Miikka Jaarte (2024) adds a further dimension by demonstrating that the progressive reappropriation of the lord-bondsman dialectic — by Marxists, feminists, and postcolonial theorists — must contend with Hegel's own use of that dialectic to argue that colonial slavery educates its victims and is therefore temporarily justifiable. The St Louis Hegelians recognized and embraced this reading, showing that the colonial-apologetic interpretation is equally internal to the text, not a subsequent distortion. Teshale Tibebu (2011), drawing on intellectual history, traces how Hegelian categories positioned Africa as "the domain of the senses" outside historical time, and how this structure shaped subsequent academic historiography in ways that continue to underwrite Eurocentrism.

The upshot of 5.1 is that the racial structure thesis, across these contributions, presses against any account that treats Hegel's Eurocentrism as merely incidental. Whether the critique targets architecture (Bernasconi), the concept of freedom (Stone), the dialectic of recognition (James and Knappik, Jaarte), or historiographical reception (Tibebu), the force of each is that revision of the colonial passages alone would leave the speculative system intact while removing a structural element.

### 5.2 Postcolonial Provincializing and the Subaltern

Postcolonial theory offers a different register of critique, one that works with, against, and through European categories rather than simply condemning them. Dipesh Chakrabarty's analysis (2000), whose INCOMPLETE entry's annotated core argument is nonetheless well established in the literature, targets what he calls "historicism" — the structure whereby non-European histories are assigned the role of catching up to what Europe already is, the "first in Europe, then elsewhere" logic that Hegel's stadial sequence paradigmatically exemplifies. Chakrabarty distinguishes "History 1," the universal abstract history of capital that subsumes local life under its categories, from "History 2," the concrete life-practices and subaltern histories that exceed and destabilize those categories. The aim of provincializing Europe is not to invert the hierarchy but to expose the provincial origins of apparently universal categories and to recover the histories that overflow them. Crucially, Chakrabarty does not simply repudiate European thought; the provincializing move retains some universals while marking their limitations — a distinction that separates his project from the decolonial delinking strategy discussed below.

Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak traces the figure of the "native informant" — the foreclosed voice that enables the production of philosophical knowledge — across Kant, Hegel, and Marx (1999). In her analysis of Hegel, Spivak argues that the absolute must exclude precisely those who cannot be integrated into the dialectical narrative: the constitutive outside of speculative reason is the subaltern subject who cannot be represented within its categories. Her earlier essay (1988), cited here cautiously from its annotated core argument, sharpens this into the claim that subaltern voices — particularly subaltern women — are doubly excluded: both from colonial historical narratives and from nationalist counter-narratives. Any philosophy of history that claims to incorporate subaltern experience re-enacts the violence of exclusion through the act of representation itself.

Achille Mbembe's "Necropolitics" (2003) extends the critique by introducing a framework in which colonial and plantation contexts gave rise to forms of sovereignty organized not around the administration of life but around the dispensation of death — "necropower." Mbembe's argument directly challenges the speculative narrative of history as the progressive unfolding of freedom by showing that modernity has always been organized around death for those assigned to the colonial exception. In *On the Postcolony* (2001), cited cautiously from its annotated argument, Mbembe elaborates a theory of postcolonial temporality as a "time of entanglement" — multiple, fractured, and heterogeneous — that is not a phase within speculative history's teleology but constitutively different from it. This is a non-teleological account of postcolonial time as such, not merely a lagging temporal position awaiting integration.

### 5.3 The Decolonial Option

Postcolonial theorists, as seen above, work critically within a space still shaped by European categorical inheritance. The decolonial tradition takes a more radical position: that modernity and coloniality are co-constitutive, and that no reform of European categories can produce the alternative required — delinking is necessary. Walter Mignolo's *Local Histories/Global Designs* (2000) argues that the production of "global designs" — universal claims about reason, progress, freedom — is always enabled by local histories of colonial violence. "Border thinking," grounded in the colonial wound, offers an alternative epistemology that begins from the subaltern colonial difference rather than from within European modernity's self-critique. In his later work (2011), cited cautiously from the annotated argument given the absence of a resolved abstract, Mignolo sharpens this into the "decolonial option": not an alternative modernity but a delinking from the modernity/coloniality matrix entirely, identifying Hegel's philosophy of history as the philosophical apex of the rhetoric of modernity. Mignolo explicitly extends his critique to Habermasian communicative reason, a line of argument taken up more fully by Allen and Vázquez-Arroyo.

Enrique Dussel's contribution enters the literature through the recent analytical study by Timmenga, Van der Zande, and Zwitter (2025), who analyze Dussel's "analectics" — a method of localizing discourse by foregrounding globally excluded perspectives — as a constructive alternative to Hegelian universal history. Dussel proposes "transmodernity" as a project of liberation starting from the exteriority of the colonial Other rather than from within European modernity's self-critique. Timmenga et al. defend Dussel's universalism against critics who charge him with dogmatic nativism, arguing that he is a relational thinker who reconciles universality with material grounding in colonial exteriority. The question raised is whether decolonial alternatives can recover universality without replicating the teleological structure they reject.

The decolonial critique extends, crucially, to the Frankfurt School heirs of Hegel, establishing the bridge back to Section 3. Amy Allen (2016) argues that Habermas and Honneth retain a teleological philosophy of history — a story of progress from primitive to modern — that remains Eurocentric in structure even when its content is radical. Drawing on Chakrabarty, Spivak, Mignolo, and Quijano, Allen proposes decolonizing critical theory by abandoning the progress narrative while preserving normative critique; her argument is that the normative-foundations problem cannot be solved by smuggling back a philosophy of history without confronting the colonial history through which that narrative was produced. Antonio Vázquez-Arroyo (2018), cited cautiously from its annotated core argument, extends this sceptically: even critical theory's deployment of the "historicity of thought" privileges European historical experience as the site of genuinely critical reflection, and the prospects for a decolonized critical theory remain doubtful. Together, Allen and Vázquez-Arroyo show that the critique of speculative history does not stop at Hegel but runs through his critical-theoretical heirs — a point of intersection with the discussion of Frankfurt School immanent critique in Section 3.

### 5.4 Feminist Critiques of the Gendered Dialectic

The feminist tradition approaches speculative history through the gendered structure of the dialectic itself. The edited collection assembled by Patricia Jagentowicz Mills (1996), cited here from its annotated core argument, brings together the major feminist philosophical engagements with Hegel: his assignment of women to natural ethical life (the family, Sittlichkeit), his characterization of women as "the irony of the community," and his exclusion of women from civil society and the state. Women are assigned to the domestic sphere in a way that is structurally analogous to Hegel's treatment of Africa and Asia as prehistorical: both are instances of immediacy, of nature not yet elevated into the mediated freedom that constitutes genuine historical agency. The parallel is not accidental — it reflects a common logic of exclusion built into the categories of the system.

Tina Chanter (2010) makes the convergence of feminist and postcolonial critiques explicit at the figure of Antigone. Hegel's reading of the Sophoclean tragedy presents Antigone as the representative of natural ethical life — the feminine principle of family piety — whose subordination to the masculine principle of law and the state is the necessary condition of ethical life's development. Chanter argues that this reading racially purifies tragedy by ignoring the play's engagement with foreignness and slavery, treating the Athenian context as one of free citizens alone. The figure of Antigone thus naturalizes both the exclusion of women from public life and the colonial naturalization of slavery: both are presented as expressions of immediacy rather than freedom, subordinate to and superseded by the mediating structures of civil society and the state. At the junction of gender and race, the speculative system's exclusions reinforce one another.

Taken together, these four subsections establish a cumulative case that the political-ethical objection targets speculative history at the level of structure rather than specific theses. The racial structure thesis, in Stone's sharpest formulation, is not a charge against an application of the freedom concept but against the concept's internal constitution — a critique that retains its force even against non-metaphysical reconstructions of Hegel (see Section 6), since it is the normative concept of freedom, not the metaphysical substrate, that is at issue. The postcolonial and decolonial traditions differ on whether European categories can be provincially reformed or must be bypassed, a disagreement mirrored in the contrast between Chakrabarty's retention of some universals and Mignolo's delinking strategy. What unites these traditions with the feminist critique is the diagnosis that speculative history's universalizing claims are constitutively produced through the exclusion of those it purports to encompass.

## Section 6: The Representational Reframing and the Question of Rehabilitation

The preceding five sections have mapped critiques that target the epistemological, methodological, dialectical, anti-foundational, and political dimensions of speculative history. This section turns to a distinct challenge — and to the responses that constitute the most developed defenses of the tradition. The narrativist turn in philosophy of history, associated above all with Hayden White and Frank Ankersmit, reframes speculative philosophy of history not as a false empirical claim but as a literary-rhetorical enterprise operating without cognitive warrant. This reframing generates its own sharp controversy. Running alongside it, a set of reconstructive and rehabilitative projects — Ricoeurian, non-metaphysical, Kantian-regulative, and decolonial — contests both the polemical critiques and the anti-realist dissolution.

### 6.1 Narrativism and Anti-Realism: Speculative History as Emplotment

White's *Metahistory* (1973) launched what remains the most structurally radical challenge to the whole genre of philosophy of history, speculative and empirical alike. White argues that the great nineteenth-century historians and philosophers of history — including Hegel and Marx — each operated with a hidden "metahistorical" code combining a dominant literary trope (metaphor, metonymy, synecdoche, irony), a mode of emplotment (romance, tragedy, comedy, satire), and a formal explanatory strategy. The implication for speculative history is acute: if Hegel's dialectical narrative is classifiable as "comic" emplotment structured by synecdoche, and Marx's as "tragic" emplotment structured by metonymy, then these grand schemes have no privileged cognitive standing. They are emplotment strategies operating at the highest scale, chosen on aesthetic and moral grounds prior to any empirical inquiry. The distinction between "critical" and "speculative" philosophy of history, introduced in the Introduction as the field's organizing division, is effectively collapsed: both are literary.

Ankersmit radicalized this in *Narrative Logic* (1983), introducing the concept of "narrative substance." For Ankersmit, historical narratives as wholes — unlike the individual statements they contain — lack truth-conditions: they propose interpretations of historical periods that have no direct referent in the past and are more appropriately evaluated aesthetically than truth-conditionally. His subsequent *Historical Representation* (2001) develops the systematic anti-representationalist corollary: historical representation operates at an irreducibly aesthetic level, introducing a "surplus of meaning" that description cannot capture, making truth-talk applicable only to singular claims, not to the interpretive whole. Domanska (2009) traces Ankersmit's subsequent trajectory from this anti-representationalist narrativism to the concept of "sublime historical experience": a pre-linguistic, traumatic rupture between historian and past that precedes and exceeds narrative integration. As Domanska shows, this turn is ambivalent for speculative history — on one reading it rehabilitates a direct encounter with the past that bypasses constructivist problems; on another, the very pre-linguistic, non-conceptual character of sublime experience is precisely what speculative history's grand narrative domesticates and suppresses. Ankersmit (2005) thus generates a double critique: speculative history fails both representationally (narrative substances do not refer) and experientially (the past exceeds narrative integration).

Jenkins (1991) and Munslow (1997) extend the anti-realist trajectory to its most thoroughgoing conclusion. Jenkins argues that history is irreducibly a present-oriented discourse, shaped by the ideological and linguistic position of the historian, with no neutral access to the past as it was. Munslow develops a taxonomy — from "reconstructionism" through "constructionism" to "deconstructionism" — that treats speculative history as the extreme form of constructionism, a fundamental self-deception about the literary nature of historical writing. On this view, speculative history's claim to reveal history's direction or meaning is a paradigm case of ideological mystification: the production of the present masquerading as discovery of the past.

### 6.2 Critiques of Narrativism and the Defense of Cognitive History

The anti-realist case, however, has not gone unanswered. Lorenz (1998) provides the most developed analytical response to White's tropological argument: the presence of rhetorical structures in historical texts does not entail that those texts lack cognitive content. Lorenz distinguishes between the "rhetoric" of historical writing — how claims are presented — and the "logic" of historical argument — whether claims are supported by evidence. White conflates the two; recognizing their independence preserves the cognitive dimension of historical writing without reverting to positivism. The point bears directly on the assessment of speculative history: if the identification of emplotment strategies does not establish anti-realism, the narrativist challenge to speculative history's epistemic credentials is substantially weaker than it first appears.

Zammito (1998) targets Ankersmit's specific notion of narrative "opacity" as incoherent: either the claim that historical texts have a holistic surplus beyond their statements is trivially true (all complex texts exhibit this) or it severs any connection between the text and the past, making historical knowledge impossible. The "hyperbole of opacity" extends correct observations about the complexity of historical writing into a general anti-epistemology that cannot sustain itself. Both Lorenz and Zammito appeared together in a 1998 issue of *History and Theory*, marking a significant moment of critical reassessment of the narrativist turn.

Ankersmit (2021) surveyed the recent state of the field, reviewing attempts to revive narrativism in a post-Whitean register. His assessment is notably skeptical of "cognitive" revival projects that restore the epistemic dimension of narrative while ignoring what he identified as the aesthetic surplus of narrative substances. Kuukkanen (2025), assessing a recent volume claiming to inaugurate a "new historical realism," argues that the confident dismissal of anti-realism displays conceptual confusion — treating anti-realism, irrealism, constructivism, and postmodernism as interchangeable — and calls for more discriminating scholarship that acknowledges anti-realism's genuine insights while avoiding its excesses. Hubálek and Kowalewski Jahromi (2025) situate the current debate in a longer genealogy running from Kant's philosophy of history through Mink's rejection of universal history to Paul Roth's naturalist-irrealist framework, proposing "three Rothian steps" (retrospectivism, irrealism, naturalism) as a constructive path beyond both strong anti-realism and naive realism. The implication for speculative history is sobering: both retrospectivism (all historical framing is present-laden) and irrealism (there is no single determinate past-as-a-whole) undermine the speculative ambition even if strong anti-realism is overstated.

### 6.3 Ricoeurian and Narrative Rehabilitation

Against both anti-realist dissolution and speculative over-determination, Ricoeur's *Time and Narrative* (1984) proposes a partial but philosophically substantive rehabilitation. Emplotment — the operation Ricoeur draws from Aristotle's *muthos* — transforms temporal succession into meaningful experience: it "follows" events rather than legislating them, providing coherence without predetermination. History is distinguished from fiction not by narrative structure but by the "documentary pact" with evidence and testimony. Speculative history errs, on this account, not in employing narrative but in treating narrative necessity as ontological necessity — in mistaking a heuristic coherence for a metaphysical claim about the direction of historical time. Mouzakitis (2014) develops this as a "third way" between positivist explanation and speculative narration: Ricoeur's concept of the "quasi-plot" of history respects contingency while supplying the coherence needed for meaningful historical understanding. Ricoeur's later *Memory, History, Forgetting* (2004) extends the framework, distinguishing documentary, explanatory-understanding, and representative stages of historical knowledge, and arguing against both total skepticism and naive correspondence — the historical condition requires neither.

Piercey (2024) argues that Ricoeur's narrativism, despite declining in fashion over four decades, remains the most defensible moderate position available, comparing favorably against both White/Ankersmit's aestheticism and the emerging naturalist alternatives. Carter (2003), drawing on Ricoeur and semiotics, argues that emplotment is not merely rhetorical decoration but is constitutive of how historians render events intelligible, and that this does not preclude truth because emplotment operates under constraints from evidence — a position that supplies tools for distinguishing defensible from indefensible speculative narratives.

The Ricoeurian rehabilitation is not, however, without internal critics. Jansen (2015) argues that Ricoeur's model presupposes a homogeneous temporality — a narrative arc from beginning through middle to end — that does not adequately capture heterogeneous, non-linear temporal structures in lived experience and literary modernism. If even Ricoeur's moderate emplotment smuggles in an unjustified temporal closure, speculative history's teleological structures face a phenomenological objection deeper than the partial rehabilitation admits.

### 6.4 Defenses and Reconstructions of the Speculative Tradition

The most direct defenses of the speculative tradition operate across several strategies, each responding to a distinct type of critique.

The non-metaphysical reconstruction, introduced in the Introduction's account of the reading debate, reasserts itself here most prominently. Pinkard (2017) reconstructs Hegel's philosophy of history not as a teleological metaphysics of Spirit but as a theory of the historical "shapes of justice": the normative orders through which freedom has been institutionalized. History "makes sense" not because Geist mechanically drives it forward but because agents navigate breakdowns in normative life, generating new shapes of ethical community through practice. On this reading, many of the epistemological objections surveyed in Sections 1 and 2 — and the anti-foundational objections of Section 4 — target a metaphysical caricature rather than the actual Hegelian position. Pippin (1989) provides the systematic philosophical grounding for this reading: Hegel's idealism completes rather than abandons Kant's transcendental project, historicizing and socializing rational self-determination rather than positing a cosmic Geist. As the Introduction flagged, the Beiser-Pippin-Pinkard dispute about which Hegel is the target is not merely exegetical — it determines the force of nearly every critique surveyed in this review.

The Kantian-regulative alternative occupies a distinct position. Kleingeld (1999) and Allison (2009) show that Kant's historical teleology, grounded in the reflective judgment of the third *Critique*, commits only to treating history "as if" it exhibits purposive structure — a regulative principle, not a constitutive claim about historical mechanism. Allison's analysis establishes that Kantian teleology is insulated from the most powerful epistemological and anti-foundational objections: if no constitutive claim about metaphysical necessity is made, neither the prophecy-impossibility arguments of Popper, the nomological objections of Hempel, nor the anti-metanarrative arguments of Lyotard have direct purchase. The regulative reading survives more of the critical terrain than any constitutive reading can.

Ankersmit (2021) himself, in "The Thorn of History," offers a neo-speculative reconstruction: Hegel's "cunning of reason" — the idea that unintended consequences serve purposes beyond the intentions of historical agents — contains a genuine philosophical insight that can be detached from metaphysical commitment to Geist. Unintended consequences are the real stuff of history, and a non-metaphysical speculative philosophy can be built from this insight. Ankersmit connects this to Anthropocene thinking, suggesting that climate change as the supreme unintended consequence reopens the question of a speculative perspective on human history. Bourke (2023), situating Hegel's philosophy in the concrete political revolutions of his era, reaffirms modernity's emancipatory potential against the cultural pessimism he traces from Nietzsche through Adorno and Foucault. Bourke's reading insists that the rational conquests of modern freedom — however imperfectly actualized — cannot be dismissed as mere ideology, and that Hegel's historical framework remains a resource for understanding those achievements even after the postcolonial, postmodern, and Frankfurt School critiques.

The postcolonial challenge, surveyed in Section 5, generates perhaps the most consequential question for any rehabilitation project: can a tradition whose concept of freedom has been argued (by Stone 2017 and James and Knappik 2025) to be constitutively Eurocentric be appropriated for emancipatory purposes? Murthy (2024) argues that it can. Asian scholars — specifically the Meiji intellectual Okakura Tenshin — used Hegel's critique of abstract individualist freedom to resist Western cultural imperialism. This suggests that what Stone diagnoses as constitutive contamination may be separable from resources within the tradition that non-Western thinkers have historically found usable against Eurocentrism. The debate between Stone and Murthy articulates the field's deepest remaining tension: whether the damage done to speculative history by the postcolonial and decolonial critiques is total or partial.

Together, these defenses converge on a common structural reply: the critiques establish serious objections to a constitutively teleological, strongly metaphysical, and Eurocentric version of speculative history; they do not necessarily establish objections to every version of the tradition. Reconstructed forms — non-metaphysical, regulative, or decolonially reappropriated — survive more of the critical terrain than their opponents typically acknowledge. The concluding section assesses which critiques, if any, apply against even the most carefully reconstructed targets.

## Conclusion

Across five critical families — epistemological-methodological, hermeneutic, immanent-dialectical, anti-foundational, and postcolonial-feminist — the literature surveyed here converges on three rejections: constitutive teleology or historical necessity, totalizing wholes that assimilate particular suffering to a rational plan, and Eurocentric universalism that projects European historical experience as the normative trajectory for humanity. Even traditions otherwise far apart share this ground. Ricoeur (1984) retains the centrality of narrative but insists that emplotment "follows" rather than "legislates" events, refusing to convert narrative coherence into ontological necessity. Frankfurt School thinkers from Adorno onward demolish the theodicy implicit in universal history while remaining within the dialectical heritage. Pinkard (2017) reconstructs Hegelian history around the normative logic of institutional breakdown and reconstruction rather than a metaphysically propelled Spirit. The question that remains after these convergences is not whether the tradition's most aggressive claims survive — they do not — but which of its elements, if any, do.

The divergences, however, are as structurally significant as the convergences, and they run along several fault lines that cannot be collapsed into a single "rejection of speculative history." The sharpest methodological divide separates the analytic tradition from postmodernism: both are hostile to speculative claims, but analytic philosophy of history, from Walsh (1951) onward, retains cognitive and realist ambitions for historical inquiry — the aim is to identify what history can legitimately know, not to abandon the epistemic project. Postmodernism, by contrast, treats the very aspiration to historical knowledge as ideologically suspect and dissolves the cognitive question into a rhetorical or political one. A second, equally important divide separates Popperian anti-historicism from hermeneutic anti-naturalism. Popper's critique of "historicism" accepts the unity of scientific method and faults speculative history for failing by empiricist standards — its developmental laws are neither falsifiable nor derivable from available evidence. Hermeneutics rejects that unity altogether: Dilthey's Geisteswissenschaften are not failed natural sciences but autonomous forms of understanding, making the Popperian tribunal simply inapplicable to the domain in which speculative history operates. These two traditions thus share a conclusion — speculative history is untenable — while disagreeing about the grounds so thoroughly that their critiques are not mutually reinforcing.

A third tension runs through the Frankfurt School and connects it to the decolonial tradition in a way that unsettles the immanent-critique strategy. Adorno, Benjamin, and Habermas each reject speculative progress while remaining within the dialectical inheritance, generating critiques that are more intimate and more corrosive than external dismissals. Yet Allen (2016) argues that this immanent strategy is not enough: even Habermas's post-metaphysical reconstruction of communicative reason presupposes a developmental logic that is crypto-progressivist and that fails to question colonial modernity. The Frankfurt School's own normative foundations, Allen contends, require a decolonial genealogical critique — meaning that the tradition's most sophisticated self-critique does not escape the very structure it targets. This charge reopens the normative-foundations problem: from where does critique draw authority if both speculative reason and its post-metaphysical inheritors are compromised?

A further distinction concerns the depth at which the critique cuts: some objections target only specific features of speculative history — necessity, the covering-law pretension, the identification of Reason with existing institutions — while leaving intact the broader aspiration to locate normative orientation in history. Others target speculative history as such. Strong anti-realism (White, Ankersmit) dissolves the question by denying that historical narratives, speculative or otherwise, have truth-conditions at the macro-level. The current realism/anti-realism debate, reopened by Kuukkanen (2025) and Hubálek and Kowalewski (2025), suggests this dissolution is unstable, which matters for evaluating how much of the speculative tradition is genuinely defeated rather than merely rhetorically displaced.

The reading-dependence point, threaded through the review, applies here with particular force. Most critiques — Popper's, the covering-law objections, Gadamer's effective-historical consciousness, Lyotard's assault on metanarrative — target a strongly metaphysical Hegel committed to constitutive teleology and a self-realizing Geist. Against a Pippin- or Pinkard-style reading, these critiques either lose traction or require reformulation: the target is no longer a self-developing world-spirit but a normative account of how historical agents navigate institutional failure. The postcolonial structural critiques advanced by Bernasconi (2000) and Stone (2017) are distinctive in this regard: they do not target the metaphysical superstructure but the concept of freedom itself, arguing that Hegel's systematic account of liberation from nature presupposes a colonial logic in which European freedom is defined against non-European unfreedom. This critique applies to the non-metaphysical Hegel no less than to the metaphysical one; it cannot be dissolved by reconstruction. Murthy's (2024) counter-argument — that Asian thinkers turned Hegel against Eurocentrism — indicates that this remains an open and actively contested question rather than a settled verdict.

The field is, in short, still live. Work published between 2024 and 2026 across multiple domains — O'Sullivan (2024) reexamining postmodern anti-metanarrative as itself a form of realism, Lal (2026) revisiting Foucault's genealogical provocation, Heller (2025) arguing that the *Dialectic of Enlightenment* misreads Hegel, Murthy (2024) tracing decolonial appropriations of Hegel — attests to ongoing revision of the positions mapped here. The decolonial critique is extending beyond Hegel to his critical-theoretical heirs, and the realism/anti-realism debate is reopening questions that the narrativist consensus of the 1990s appeared to have settled. What the preceding survey establishes is the structure of the field: its convergences are real and its core rejections are durable, but they are achieved through incompatible means, and the question of what survives — whether in the form of Kantian regulative teleology, non-metaphysical normative reconstruction, or decolonially chastened narrative — remains a matter of substantive philosophical dispute.
