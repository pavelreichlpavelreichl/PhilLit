## Section 3: Aesthetics and the Sociological Theory of Art

If Section 1 identifies "social psychology" and ideology as the mediating tiers of Plekhanov's five-fold formula, his aesthetics supplies the most fully worked-out instance of that tier in practice, and the debate it has generated in miniature reproduces the review's master-tension between rigorous determinism and interpretive nuance. Developed chiefly in "Letters Without Address" (1899–1900) and "Art and Social Life" (1912–1913) — collected in English as a single composite volume (Plekhanov and Fineberg 1957) — Plekhanov's theory of art was his attempt to extend historical materialism from politics and ideology to aesthetic experience, and it has since become a recurring test case for whether his method escapes the "vulgar Marxist" charge levelled against his philosophy of history more broadly.

### Subsection 3.1: The genetic theory and the critique of "art for art's sake"

Plekhanov's starting point is genetic and anthropological rather than evaluative: engaging critically with Karl Bücher's and Herbert Spencer's labor- and play-theories of the origin of art, he argues that among primitive peoples utilitarian labor precedes and generates aesthetic play, so that "utility precedes aesthetic enjoyment" in the historical development of humanity (Plekhanov and Fineberg 1957). From this derivation he builds his central polemical target: "art for art's sake" is not a perennial aesthetic option but a historically specific ideological response, paradigmatically that of the French intelligentsia after 1848, whose estrangement from a viable social role expressed itself as a doctrine of disinterested contemplation — to be contrasted with periods, such as the Enlightenment or Belinsky-era Russian criticism, in which utilitarian, socially engaged art predominates. Toma (2025) situates this move within a longer indigenous genealogy, tracing the politicization of art in the Russian left-wing intelligentsia from Chernyshevsky's utilitarian criticism through Plekhanov's founding of a distinctively Russian Marxist aesthetic theory to its eventual political practice under Lenin, rather than treating Plekhanov's aesthetics as a derivative application of Western Marxism. Nascimento (2014) offers a comparable reconstruction centered on "Art and Social Life," crediting Plekhanov with opening what was, prior to his intervention, an unexplored area of Marxist theory through his dialogue with the Belinsky tradition, while also identifying what he terms a "restrictive method" running through Plekhanov's criticism — a qualification that anticipates the reductionism debate taken up below.

### Subsection 3.2: The reductionism debate

The question these reconstructions leave open — whether Plekhanov's genetic and historicizing moves cohere with a defensible account of specifically aesthetic value — was posed directly in the one sustained pre-2000s English-language exchange on his aesthetics. Baxandall (1967) credits Plekhanov with producing the first systematic Marxist aesthetic theory but argues that his method of translating art into "the language of sociology" tends toward a reductive determinism that subordinates the aesthetic dimension of a work to its social and class origins. Truitt's contemporaneous reply defends Plekhanov against this charge, arguing that Baxandall's reading mischaracterizes a more historically nuanced account of the relation between social development and artistic form (Truitt 1970). Della Volpe (1978) presses a related but independently motivated critique from within Marxist aesthetics itself, developing a rival theory of art's rational, conceptual content that names Plekhanov's (and, alongside him, Lukács's) "sociological reductionism" as a target to be overcome rather than extended — evidence that the reductionism charge was not confined to non-Marxist critics. Lu Yan (2015) documents a further, distinct line of dissent: Lefebvre's "Introduction to Aesthetics" argues against Plekhanov by emphasizing the spiritual rather than the material dimension of the art–social life relation, extending the debate's afterlife into later Western Marxist aesthetics.

Sockol's more recent work reframes the entire exchange. Rather than treating Plekhanov's fin-de-siècle writings as naive reflectionism, Sockol (2023) reads them as polemical interventions directed simultaneously against Kantian aesthetic disinterestedness and against contemporary critics of Marxism, in which the stress on class conflict as formative of artistic creation issues in a deliberately activist, "tendentious" aesthetic aimed at mobilizing art for revolutionary ends rather than a mechanical reflection theory. This revisionist reading does not, however, sever Plekhanov from later doctrinal history: Sockol's earlier study argues that the same prescriptive framework — combining hostility to bourgeois and modernist art with support for realistic, utilitarian art — anticipated the tenets of Socialist Realism, tracing an intellectual line of descent that he takes to be neglected relative to other proposed origins of the Soviet doctrine (Sockol 2011). Read together, the two studies suggest a position at once more argumentatively coherent and more historically consequential than either the "vulgar Marxism" charge or a simple vindication would allow — a pattern that recurs, for the philosophy of history as a whole, in Section 5's account of the "vulgar materialist" label.
