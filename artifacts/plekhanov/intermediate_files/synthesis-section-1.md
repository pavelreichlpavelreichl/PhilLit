## Introduction

Georgi Plekhanov (1856–1918) is conventionally credited as the chief popularizer and philosophical interpreter of Marxism in 1880s Russia, the first writer to coin the term "dialectical materialism" and to draw systematic attention to the Hegelian origins of Marx's system (White 2018). Where Marx and Engels had developed historical materialism occasionally, in the course of polemical and economic writing, Plekhanov undertook to render it a total, self-consciously philosophical world-view. His three central texts — "The Development of the Monist View of History" (1895, published under censorship as *In Defence of Materialism*), "The Materialist Conception of History" (1897), and "The Role of the Individual in History" (1898) — construct a systematic account of historical explanation running from the state of the productive forces, through the economic relations these forces generate, to the socio-political order built upon them, to the "psychology of social man" this order conditions, and finally to the ideologies that crystallize and express that psychology (Plekhanov 1940). This "five-fold formula," together with a compatibilist solution to the problem of freedom and necessity modeled on Spinoza and Hegel, forms the central object of the present review.

The stakes of reconstructing this schema are twofold. First, philosophically, Plekhanov's formula was designed to occupy a middle position between economic reductionism and the "subjectivist" sociology of the Narodnik theorists Mikhailovsky and Lavrov, who held that history is made by "critically thinking individuals" applying a moral ideal. Second, historically, Plekhanov's interpretation of Marxism — with its emphasis on the deterministic, "scientific" side of Marx's writings — became, transmitted through Lenin, the basis of official Soviet Marxist-Leninist orthodoxy (White 2018). Yet Plekhanov himself ended his life a Menshevik opponent of the October Revolution his own theory of the proletariat's revolutionary role had helped make conceivable. This is the paradox that structures both his reception and the scholarly debates surveyed here: a figure whose determinism was rigorous enough to found a state ideology, and yet whose own reading of that determinism led him to reject the revolution made in its name.

Anglophone scholarship on Plekhanov remains comparatively thin and unevenly dated. No dedicated entry in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy or the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy treats him as a primary subject; where he appears, it is only in passing, in entries on Spinoza's political philosophy or on Isaiah Berlin. Samuel Baron's 1963 biography, *Plekhanov: The Father of Russian Marxism*, and Andrzej Walicki's synthetic *A History of Russian Thought from the Enlightenment to Marxism* (1979) remain the standard framings of his place in the transition from Populism to Marxism, but much of the surrounding literature dates to the Cold War and carries its interpretive commitments. A 2023 popular reassessment argues that Plekhanov remains comparatively neglected despite his continuing, largely unacknowledged influence (White 2023). Given this unevenness, the present review does not aim to settle the disputes it surveys but to map the positions and tensions that structure them.

The review proceeds from history outward. Section 1 reconstructs Plekhanov's philosophy of history as the analytical core; Section 2 establishes the ontological and epistemological foundations underwriting it; Section 3 examines his aesthetics as an instance of its ideology level; Section 4 traces its political operationalization; and Section 5 assesses its influence and the historiographical verdicts it has attracted.
