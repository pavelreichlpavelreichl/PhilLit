## Section 5: Influence, Reception, and the Historiographical Verdict

If the philosophy of history is where Plekhanov's determinism is constructed, the literature on his influence and reception is where it is judged — and judged inconsistently. Two registers of assessment need distinguishing. The first traces how Plekhanov's materialism was transmitted, retained, or revised as it passed through Lenin into Soviet orthodoxy and laterally into Western Marxism. The second is meta-historiographical: the recurring labels "vulgar materialist," "father of Bolshevism," and "tragic Menshevik" are not neutral descriptions but verdicts shaped by the political commitments of the critics who issued them. Both registers return the review's master-tension — determinism versus agency, and the charge of vulgar mechanism — to the surface, since it is precisely Plekhanov's compatibilist synthesis and its political enactment that later assessors argue about.

### 5.1 Lenin, reflection theory, and Soviet diamat

Lenin's 1908 *Materialism and Empirio-Criticism* adopted Plekhanov's reflection theory of knowledge and his polemical posture toward "Machism," making Plekhanov's epistemology the philosophical basis of what would become Soviet dialectical materialism. Whether this debt persisted is contested. Bonilla (2020) argues that Lenin's 1915 commentaries on Hegel's *Science of Logic* present a different, more dialectical theory of reflection than the 1908 text: reflection becomes an adaptive, reciprocal relation in which the movement of knowledge follows and influences the movement of reality, a "moment" of human objectification rather than a passive copying of an external world. On this reading, Lenin's Hegelian turn constitutes a substantive philosophical rupture with the Plekhanov-derived epistemology of his earlier work. Oittinen (2018) argues the opposite on two fronts. First, there is no textual evidence that Lenin changed position after 1914, since his post-1917 criticism of Bukharin's failure to apply dialectics shows continuity with the 1907 framework. Second, and more pointedly, Oittinen denies that Plekhanov was ever the source of Lenin's governing conception of dialectics — "concrete analysis of a concrete situation" — which he traces instead to the Narodnik writer Alexander Herzen. Bonilla and Oittinen therefore disagree not only about whether a rupture occurred but about whether Plekhanov was ever the operative source to rupture from, leaving the Lenin–Plekhanov relationship without a settled account in the literature.

The stakes of this disagreement extend beyond textual exegesis to the question of whether Soviet Marxism-Leninism inherited a rigid or a self-correcting epistemology. Pateman (2019) intervenes on this question directly, arguing through textual analysis that Lenin's epistemology is fallibilist rather than dogmatic: *Materialism and Empirio-Criticism* affirms uncertainty across the natural, philosophical, and social sciences and is inconsistent with the "vulgarizer" caricature routinely applied to Lenin — and, by extension, to the Plekhanovite materialism he adopted. This defense stands against the Western critical consensus, discussed below, that treats the reflection-theoretic inheritance as the root of Marxism-Leninism's dogmatism. Antonov (2021) shows that the underlying philosophical content remains unsettled on its own terms: revisiting the Lenin–empiriocritic dispute over the definition and mode of existence of matter, he argues that Lenin considered the question only ontologically and gnoseologically, and that Lenin's own principle of the identity of dialectics, logic, and theory of knowledge requires a logical supplement that would revise the canonical definition — treating the Plekhanov/Lenin account of matter as still-contested philosophy rather than closed orthodoxy. The reflection theory's institutional afterlife is documented, with more caution given its INCOMPLETE sourcing, by Mareeva (2016), who characterizes Mikhail Lifshitz's Soviet-era "ontognoseology" — developed within Lukács's Moscow circle in the 1930s — as an elaboration of Lenin's theory of reflection within Soviet aesthetic philosophy, tracing the doctrine's survival well beyond the epistemological dispute that produced it and into the aesthetics domain treated in Section 3.

### 5.2 Lateral and Western Marxist reception

Plekhanov's materialism also had lateral relations and a hostile reception outside the Leninist line. Savino (2018) documents that Antonio Labriola was a significant theoretical authority for Russian Social Democrats abroad, and that Gramsci's concept of praxis and rejection of positivism derive substantially from Labriola while also engaging Russian Marxism — establishing a connection between Plekhanov's tradition and Italian anti-positivist "philosophy of praxis" that ran mainly through Labriola's broader influence rather than direct exchange between the two theorists. Colletti (1971) gives this current a precise textual anchor by defining "the Marxism of the Second International" narrowly as the Marxism of Kautsky and Plekhanov, and by locating much of it already in Engels's late works — situating Plekhanov's codification of dialectical materialism as continuous with late Engelsian materialism rather than an independent departure.

That codification became, for council communists and later the Frankfurt School, the principal target of Western Marxist anti-Leninism. Savin (2020) reconstructs how Anton Pannekoek's *Lenin as Philosopher* attacked Lenin's — and, behind him, Plekhanov's — natural-scientific materialism as a bourgeois-Enlightenment inheritance rather than genuine historical materialism, a critique transmitted to the Frankfurt School chiefly through Marcuse's *Soviet Marxism* (1958) and thereafter standard in Western-left framings of Soviet diamat. Georg Lukács offers the most developed alternative within this current: his Hegelian-praxis reading of Lenin, presented in *History and Class Consciousness* and *Lenin: A Study on the Unity of His Thought* (Lukács 1971 [1924]), substitutes a dialectic of totality and praxis for reflection-theoretic materialism, a shift traced biographically by Szabados and Sautkin (2021) from Lukács's early aestheticism through his 1918 turn to Marxism and the failure of the 1919 Hungarian Soviet Republic. Le Blanc (2013) complicates the standard placement of this work as an "ultra-left" or "proto-Stalinist" deviation, arguing that Lukács's 1923–29 writings form a coherent Leninist-Trotskyist political position developed during his factional leadership, alongside Jenő Landler, against Béla Kun's sectarianism in the Hungarian Communist Party — reframing Lukács's Hegelianism as concretely factional-political rather than a purely philosophical alternative to Plekhanovite materialism. The broader post-war Marxological synthesis of these critiques appears, with the caution warranted by their INCOMPLETE sourcing, in Kolakowski (1978) and Acton (1955), both of which place Plekhanov's and Kautsky's codification of dialectical materialism at the origin of a Marxism-Leninism subsequently judged, from within Western Marxism and from liberal-analytic philosophy respectively, as scientistic or internally incoherent.

### 5.3 The historiographical verdicts — "vulgar materialist," "father of Bolshevism," "tragic Menshevik"

The meta-level literature treats these three labels as contested verdicts rather than settled descriptions, and it divides along the lines of who is doing the judging. Within Soviet historiography itself, Baron (1974a) documents that "Plekhanovism" was treated pejoratively under Stalin because of Plekhanov's Menshevism and wartime "defencism," then selectively rehabilitated after Stalin's death, with his theoretical authority reaffirmed even as his political record remained officially condemned; Baron (1974b) extends the analysis by showing that Plekhanov's historiographical standing was entangled with the parallel, shifting fate of Trotsky in official Soviet memory. Ulam (2024 [1964]) supplies the sharpest formulation of the resulting dilemma, comparing Plekhanov's position in Soviet historiography to "the bride's divorced father who shows up at the wedding": honored as the father of Russian Marxism yet unable to be treated as a straightforward forerunner of Bolshevism, he must be seated somewhere, with a degree of reverence that shifting party doctrine could never specify in advance.

Politically engaged Marxist assessments share a rhetoric of tragedy while locating its cause differently, and the outline's staged debate is worth making explicit. Deutscher (1964) credits Plekhanov with a "brilliant intellectual conquest" in transplanting Marxism to Russia and casts him, via a comparison to Erasmus, as possessing refined theoretical intelligence but lacking the "oaken strength" revolutionary practice required — so that his 1903 stand with Lenin, his subsequent rejection of Lenin's peasant strategy as a lapse into Populism, and his 1914 "social patriotism" together exhibit a coherent mismatch between intellectual temperament and revolutionary ruthlessness. Cliff (1957) locates the failure elsewhere: Plekhanov's decisive weakness, on this reading, was his lack of direct experience organizing workers, which left him a theoretician isolated from the proletariat and pushed him toward compromise with the liberal bourgeoisie. North and Volkov (2016) locate it differently again, in the internal logic of Plekhanov's own theory: his identification of the working class as Russia's decisive revolutionary force was a theoretical achievement that made October conceivable even as Plekhanov personally opposed it, because his two-stage theory of revolution was overtaken by events rather than abandoned through personal weakness — a reading the authors connect explicitly to the genesis of Trotsky's theory of permanent revolution. Temperament, organizational isolation, and theoretical overtaking by events thus compete as causal accounts of the same "tragedy," and none is treated in the literature as having settled the question.

A further, reflexive layer questions the assessors themselves. Carr's essay judges Plekhanov against Lenin's standard and endorses, per Berlin's contemporaneous review, the Communist historian A. Rothstein's verdict that Plekhanov's "unforgivable sin" was refusing to follow his former pupil into October (Carr 2021 [1950]). Berlin (1951) responds that Carr, previously a historian of ironic detachment, has fallen "under the spell" of the "Welthistorisch individuals who make history," and reads Carr's harshness toward Plekhanov as a symptom of pro-Lenin sympathy rather than dispassionate judgment — making Berlin's review itself a data point about how Cold War historiographical loyalties shaped Plekhanov's reputation. Western scholarly reception of the Baron biographical project registered a comparatively steady assessment: McNeal (1964) reviews Baron's biography favorably, judging its treatment of the revisionism and economism debates lucid and the whole a study worthy of an important subject. Against this backdrop, White (2023) argues in a recent reassessment that Plekhanov remains comparatively neglected in Western scholarship despite a continuing, largely unacknowledged influence, framing his legacy as paradoxical: politically rejected in 1917, yet theoretically preserved by the very Soviet leadership that defeated him.

Taken together, this section shows that the labels "vulgar materialist," "father of Bolshevism," and "tragic Menshevik" each encode a specific judgment about the same underlying problem this review has tracked from Section 1 onward — whether Plekhanov's determinism was too rigid to accommodate agency, and whose agency (Lenin's, the working class's, Plekhanov's own) exposed that rigidity when history moved. The reception debates do not resolve this question; they relocate it into competing narratives about cause and blame, setting up the conclusion's return to the philosophical problem beneath the historiography.
