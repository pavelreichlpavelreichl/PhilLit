## Conclusion

Across the four substantive domains surveyed here — the foundational ontology, the aesthetics, the political trajectory, and the reception history — a consolidating finding emerges: the charge that Plekhanov's materialism is "vulgar" or mechanistic is increasingly contested rather than confirmed. Bassin (1992) shows that Plekhanov's engagement with Ratzel's environmental determinism operates through mediating social categories rather than direct geographic causation, complicating readings that reduce his materialism to a narrow economism. Sockol (2023) reframes the aesthetic theory examined in Section 3 as an anti-Kantian, activist polemic rather than a naive reflectionism, against the Baxandall–Truitt line that treated it as reductive "translation." Steinberg (2024) offers a non-partisan assessment of Plekhanov's Spinozist monism as a defensible, non-reductive reading among Russian Marxists rather than a crude economistic substitute for dialectic. Pateman (2019) mounts a fallibilist defense of the reflection-theoretic epistemology against the dogmatism charge leveled at Lenin and, by extension, Plekhanov. Hu's reconstruction likewise resists reading Plekhanov's "social organism" model as flatly determinist. Taken together, these positions converge on a revisionist current that softens the caricature without eliminating a deeper, unresolved problem: the determinism/individual-agency tension identified independently by Shaw (1988), working from within analytic philosophy of history, and Hook, testing the same fault line historically through the counterfactual role of Lenin in October 1917. That two critics working from different disciplinary starting points arrive at convergent skepticism about whether Plekhanov's compatibilism can accommodate genuine individual causal efficacy indicates that this tension, rather than the vulgar-materialist label, is the more durable interpretive problem — one Plekhanov's own political life, oscillating between doctrinal fixity and repeated decisive improvisation in 1883, 1903, and 1914, appears to enact rather than resolve.

Several questions remain genuinely open. The five-fold formula and its "social psychology" mediating tier, reconstructed in Section 1, have not received a rigorous dedicated study; treatment of the formula is distributed across biographical (Baron), synthetic (Walicki), and more recent (Brazevich) accounts, none of which isolates the tier's explanatory work as a philosophical problem in its own right. The coherence of Plekhanov's compatibilism — whether "freedom is the recognition of necessity" can be squared with the causal weight he assigns to individuals — remains unsettled despite Shaw's and Hook's parallel critiques. The dispute over whether Lenin's post-1914 turn to Hegel constituted a rupture with Plekhanovian orthodoxy (Bonilla) or a continuity sourced elsewhere, in Herzen rather than Plekhanov (Oittinen), has not been adjudicated. And post-Soviet, Russian-language reassessments of Plekhanov appear underrepresented in the Anglophone literature surveyed, leaving open how far the revisionist current identified above extends beyond the English-language record consulted here.

These debates connect at a structural level: the historiographical verdicts on Plekhanov — "father of Bolshevism," "vulgar materialist," "tragic Menshevik" — are inseparable from judgments about his political trajectory, and those judgments are in turn shaped by Cold War and factional-Marxist commitments rather than by philosophical analysis of the five-fold schema alone. Evaluating Plekhanov's philosophy of history on its own terms therefore requires disentangling the philosophical assessment of his determinism and compatibilism from the political verdict on his Menshevism, a separation the literature surveyed here does not consistently maintain. A philosophically focused re-examination of the five-fold formula and the freedom/necessity synthesis, brought into contact with contemporary agency–structure frameworks, would allow Plekhanov to be read as a qualified mediation-theorist — one who built layered, non-reductive causal mechanisms while leaving the place of individual agency within them incompletely resolved — rather than as the crude determinist of received caricature.
