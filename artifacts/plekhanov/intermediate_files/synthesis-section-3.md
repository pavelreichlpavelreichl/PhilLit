## Section 2: Philosophical Foundations — Monism, Epistemology, and Fidelity

The five-fold formula reconstructed in Section 1 rests on two further claims that require independent scrutiny: an ontological claim that reality and history form a single, non-dualistic material order, and an epistemological claim that this order is genuinely knowable despite the mediated character of sensation. Both claims were contested by Plekhanov's contemporaries, and both bear on a further question — whether the resulting system, which Plekhanov named "dialectical materialism," faithfully transmits Marx's and Engels's own views or substantially revises them.

### 2.1 Monism via Spinoza

Plekhanov grounded his monism partly through a materialist reading of Spinoza, treating substance monism as a theologically clothed anticipation of dialectical materialism whose "theological" vocabulary he dismissed as a historical relic of Spinoza's own epoch (Steila 2021). Steila situates this reading against Lunacharsky's rival interpretation, which took Spinoza's religious language seriously as evidence that Spinoza's "religious feelings" — not merely his ontology — made him a genuine forerunner of Marxism, showing that Spinoza's status was contested territory even among self-identified materialists. Steinberg (2024) evaluates this reading comparatively against those of Plekhanov's followers Deborin and Akselrod: Deborin's identification of Spinozist Nature with matter fails as Spinoza interpretation, and Akselrod's asymmetric-dependence account of the idea/body relation fails both as interpretation and as materialism, leaving Plekhanov's reading — which treats materialism as compatible with panpsychism — as, in Steinberg's assessment, the most plausible of the three, while noting that Spinoza's own metaphysics is not best described as materialism at all. This comparative verdict does not settle the matter internally to Russian Marxism, however. Soboleva (2021) reconstructs Bogdanov's contemporaneous objection to Plekhanov's alignment of Spinoza with Marxism, distinguishing a hermeneutical charge — that Plekhanov dogmatizes and instrumentalizes historical concepts by disregarding their original context — from a systematic one concerning the proper relation between extension and thought. Out of this second objection Bogdanov developed his rival "empiriomonist" theory of cognition. The Spinozist grounding of Plekhanov's monism was thus contested from its first articulation, both on grounds of textual fidelity to Spinoza and on grounds of philosophical coherence.

### 2.2 The hieroglyph theory of knowledge and its critics

Plekhanov's epistemology combined this ontological monism with a Helmholtzian "hieroglyph" or sign theory of sensation, on which sensations are conventional signs correlating reliably with an external, mind-independent reality rather than literal copies or "images" of it (Wagner 1993, reporting Steila's monograph-length reconstruction). This move let Plekhanov hold a strong realist ontology together with an admission that perception is mediated, but it drew a documented objection from Engels that treating sensations as mere hieroglyphs risked reintroducing Kantian agnosticism about the thing-in-itself. The objection proved durable. Rybas (2023) shows that Russian empiriocritics — Bogdanov, Yushkevich, Lesevich, and Lunacharsky — opposed Plekhanov's identification of the thing-in-itself with the material object on the grounds that this identification is itself an unwarranted metaphysical assumption, incapable of refuting either dualism or Kantian agnosticism; from their monistic standpoint, the thing-in-itself should instead be understood as a necessary form of the organization of experience. Rumyantseva (2025) extends this critical assessment by examining Plekhanov's polemics against the German Neo-Kantians and Eduard Bernstein, arguing that Plekhanov's professed "defence" of Kant was chiefly instrumental — aimed at shielding Marx and Engels from revisionism — and that Plekhanov himself, in the process, misinterpreted aspects of Kant's own doctrine of the thing-in-itself. The stakes of this epistemological dispute extended beyond the nature of sensation to the nature of Marxist theory itself: Pavlov (2017) reconstructs the parallel Plekhanov–Bogdanov debate over the status of truth, framing it as a contest over whether Marxism operates with a revisable, scientific theory of truth or transmits a set of absolute truths as doctrine. The hieroglyph controversy also had a long afterlife: Sériot (2022) traces how the Marxist-Leninist dispute over the sign/referent relation — inaugurated by Lenin's 1909 polemic against Mach and, by extension, against hieroglyph-style theories of sensation — resurfaced in the 1930s–1950s Soviet charge that Saussure's linguistics itself constituted a "bourgeois idealist" theory of hieroglyphics, indicating that the epistemological question Plekhanov's sign theory raised remained unsettled well into the Soviet period rather than being resolved by Lenin's own reflection theory (examined further in Section 5).

### 2.3 Fidelity to Marx, Engels, and Hegel

These foundational commitments raise a further question: how much of "dialectical materialism" reflects Marx's and Engels's own views, and how much is Plekhanov's systematization or revision of them? White (1996) offers a genealogy of dialectical materialism that traces its path from Hegel and the Left Hegelians through Marx's own writings to the Russian Social-Democratic milieu of the 1890s from which Plekhanov's formulation, and subsequently Lenin's, emerged, and in doing so questions any unbroken continuity between Marx's own thought and the doctrine Plekhanov named and systematized. Moore (1971) locates a comparable difficulty already within Marx himself, independent of Plekhanov: he dates the origin of dialectical materialism to Marx's 1857 return to studying Hegel, when Marx combined a realist epistemology opposed to Hegel with a Hegel-influenced dialectical method for deriving the labor theory of value, producing a tension between materialism and dialectic that, on Moore's reading, Marx never fully resolved. This suggests that whatever incoherence critics find in Plekhanov's monism may partly inherit an unresolved tension in Marx's own texts rather than originate with Plekhanov's exposition of them. Van Ree (2013) documents a more specific and textually grounded case of substantive revision on Plekhanov's part: comparing "Socialism and the Political Struggle" (1883) with "Our Differences" (1885), he argues that Plekhanov — while presenting himself as simply applying the Communist Manifesto's revolutionary scenario to Russian conditions — was in fact the first Marxist to reject Marx's original scenario of proletarian revolution by numerically small proletariats, instead predicating revolution on the prior proletarianization and industrialization of the majority of the population. Taken together, these three studies suggest that Plekhanov functioned as a systematizer and occasional reviser of the Marx-Hegel-Feuerbach lineage rather than a neutral transmitter of a settled orthodoxy — a finding that reframes the question, taken up in Section 5, of whether Plekhanov should be credited with originating "dialectical materialism" or charged with distorting it.

The philosophical foundations examined here are accordingly more contested, and in some respects more sophisticated, than the "vulgar materialist" label implies: the Spinozist monism was defended comparatively well against rival Marxist readings even as it drew serious internal objections, and the hieroglyph epistemology represented a considered attempt to reconcile realism with mediated perception rather than a naive reflectionism. Yet both the coherence of the monism and the fidelity of the resulting system to Marx's own texts remain genuinely open questions — questions that the philosophy of history's five-fold formula and its treatment of individual agency (Section 1) inherit rather than resolve.
