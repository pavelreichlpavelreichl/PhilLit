## Section 4: Political Trajectory and the Determinism/Voluntarism Problem

Plekhanov's political career supplies the practical counterpart to the deterministic philosophy of history reconstructed in Section 1, and it does so in a way that repeatedly strains against that philosophy rather than simply illustrating it. The 1883 founding of the Emancipation of Labour group already displays the pattern: Plekhanov and his co-founders broke with Narodism not by deducing a party program from the five-fold schema but by taking a practical stand against individual terrorism. Gaido and Bosch Alessio (2015) show that this founding commitment was reaffirmed two decades later when Vera Zasulich, one of the group's five founders, published a 1902 polemic against the newly formed Socialist-Revolutionary Party's revival of terrorist tactics, a polemic that aligned the Emancipation of Labour veterans with the organizational, mass-party logic Lenin was then developing in *What Is to Be Done?*. Gaido and Bosch Alessio read this alignment as complicating any narrative in which Menshevism was anti-organizational from the outset: Plekhanov's later break with Lenin was a dispute over the *form* of centralization, not over its principle.

That dispute crystallized at the Second Congress of 1903. Gaido and Jozami (2017) argue that the centralized "cadre party" model usually credited to Lenin alone was in fact the collective project of the Iskra editorial board, on which Plekhanov sat, adapting German Social Democratic practice to Russian clandestine conditions and to factional struggle against the Economists and the Bund. On this reading, Plekhanov's subsequent decision to side with Martov's minority against Lenin's party-rules proposal was a rupture within a shared organizational project rather than the expression of a prior theoretical disagreement about the class character of the coming revolution. The split did not settle the matter: Elwood (2011) documents the International Socialist Bureau's July 1914 Brussels "Unity" conference, the last pre-war attempt to reconcile the factions, at which Plekhanov, Trotsky, Aksel'rod, and Martov confronted Lenin's delegate Inessa Armand. Elwood treats the RSDLP split as an actively contested organizational problem still unresolved on the eve of the war, rather than a fact settled in 1903 — a reminder that Plekhanov's political positioning continued to be negotiated in real time rather than derived once and for all from doctrine.

The theoretical premise underwriting Plekhanov's Menshevik strategy was his account of Russia's belated and uneven capitalist development. Baron (1958) reconstructs Plekhanov's picture of Russia as a society shaped by "Oriental" or Asiatic state forms onto which Western capitalist relations were subsequently and unevenly imposed — a framework used to argue that Russia had entered, but not completed, the capitalist stage presupposed by orthodox Marxist theory, and hence that a bourgeois-democratic revolution had to precede any socialist one. (This account survives only through the paper's bibliographic notes rather than a recoverable abstract, but Baron's standing as Plekhanov's principal biographer, together with the substantive claims transmitted in the note, warrants cautious reliance on it here.) This "two-stage" schema was not simply asserted; Avila and Gaido (2017) reconstruct how Plekhanov himself subjected it to a real test in October 1906, circulating a questionnaire to the leaders of international Social Democracy asking about the class character of the 1905 Revolution. Twelve replies overwhelmingly endorsed the Menshevik view that the revolution was bourgeois and that cooperation with liberal opposition was warranted — but Kautsky's reply rejected any coalition with the bourgeoisie, undercutting the Menshevik position from within the very poll Plekhanov had organized to confirm it. Thatcher (2021) shows that Menshevik theorists nonetheless pursued the schema empirically after 1905, producing a largely overlooked collective study analyzing the revolution class by class, evidence that stagism functioned as an ongoing research program rather than static doctrine even as its international standing had already been contested by Kautsky. Day (2009) situates this fracture within a wider field of live alternatives, tracing "permanent revolution" as a rival schema developed by Trotsky and Parvus that collapsed the bourgeois and socialist stages Plekhanov's theory kept separate — evidence that Menshevik stagism was one contested position among several, not an uncontroversial deduction from historical materialism.

The final and starkest instance of the pattern is 1914 "defencism." Baron (1981) traces Plekhanov's support for Russia's war effort and his consequent break with the Zimmerwald-aligned internationalist wing of Russian and European socialism, a position that isolated him politically through 1917 and that Baron reads as continuous with, rather than a rupture from, his prior political trajectory. Across these episodes — the 1883 break, the 1903 split, the 1906 questionnaire's unintended exposure of stagism, and 1914 defencism — no single work in this literature (nor, as a brief conference overview by Zhuravleva et al. 2019 confirms, the broader biographical record) treats the relationship between Plekhanov's historical determinism and his own decisive political improvisation as its central subject. It emerges instead as a background problem that the political narrative continually poses without resolving: a theorist committed to a fixed sequence of historical stages who nonetheless had, at each hinge point, to act, split, or dissent before that sequence could confirm or refute him. This gap sets the empirical stakes for the "tragedy" narratives examined in Section 5.
