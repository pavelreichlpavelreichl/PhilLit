## Introduction

Georgi Plekhanov (1856–1918) is conventionally credited as the chief popularizer and philosophical interpreter of Marxism in 1880s Russia, the first writer to coin the term "dialectical materialism" and to draw systematic attention to the Hegelian origins of Marx's system (White 2018). Where Marx and Engels had developed historical materialism occasionally, in the course of polemical and economic writing, Plekhanov undertook to render it a total, self-consciously philosophical world-view. His three central texts — "The Development of the Monist View of History" (1895, published under censorship as *In Defence of Materialism*), "The Materialist Conception of History" (1897), and "The Role of the Individual in History" (1898) — construct a systematic account of historical explanation running from the state of the productive forces, through the economic relations these forces generate, to the socio-political order built upon them, to the "psychology of social man" this order conditions, and finally to the ideologies that crystallize and express that psychology (Plekhanov 1940). This "five-fold formula," together with a compatibilist solution to the problem of freedom and necessity modeled on Spinoza and Hegel, forms the central object of the present review.

The stakes of reconstructing this schema are twofold. First, philosophically, Plekhanov's formula was designed to occupy a middle position between economic reductionism and the "subjectivist" sociology of the Narodnik theorists Mikhailovsky and Lavrov, who held that history is made by "critically thinking individuals" applying a moral ideal. Second, historically, Plekhanov's interpretation of Marxism — with its emphasis on the deterministic, "scientific" side of Marx's writings — became, transmitted through Lenin, the basis of official Soviet Marxist-Leninist orthodoxy (White 2018). Yet Plekhanov himself ended his life a Menshevik opponent of the October Revolution his own theory of the proletariat's revolutionary role had helped make conceivable. This is the paradox that structures both his reception and the scholarly debates surveyed here: a figure whose determinism was rigorous enough to found a state ideology, and yet whose own reading of that determinism led him to reject the revolution made in its name.

Anglophone scholarship on Plekhanov remains comparatively thin and unevenly dated. No dedicated entry in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy or the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy treats him as a primary subject; where he appears, it is only in passing, in entries on Spinoza's political philosophy or on Isaiah Berlin. Samuel Baron's 1963 biography, *Plekhanov: The Father of Russian Marxism*, and Andrzej Walicki's synthetic *A History of Russian Thought from the Enlightenment to Marxism* (1979) remain the standard framings of his place in the transition from Populism to Marxism, but much of the surrounding literature dates to the Cold War and carries its interpretive commitments. A 2023 popular reassessment argues that Plekhanov remains comparatively neglected despite his continuing, largely unacknowledged influence (White 2023). Given this unevenness, the present review does not aim to settle the disputes it surveys but to map the positions and tensions that structure them.

The review proceeds from history outward. Section 1 reconstructs Plekhanov's philosophy of history as the analytical core; Section 2 establishes the ontological and epistemological foundations underwriting it; Section 3 examines his aesthetics as an instance of its ideology level; Section 4 traces its political operationalization; and Section 5 assesses its influence and the historiographical verdicts it has attracted.

## Section 1: The Philosophy of History

Plekhanov's philosophy of history was constructed to block two errors at once: the economic reductionism that would let ideas and institutions be read straight off the state of production, and the "subjectivist" voluntarism that would let critically minded individuals redirect history by moral fiat. His three central texts — *In Defence of Materialism: The Development of the Monist View of History* (1895), *The Materialist Conception of History* (1897), and *The Role of the Individual in History* (1898) — build, in response, a multi-tiered monist schema in which each level conditions but does not determine the content of the next (Plekhanov 1947). The result is a theory whose determinism is qualified at almost every joint, yet whose treatment of individual agency remains, on the assessment of both its analytic and its historical critics, unresolved.

### 1.1 The five-fold formula and the "social psychology" mediating level

The architecture usually called the "five-fold formula" runs from the state of the productive forces, through the economic relations these forces generate, to the socio-political order built on those relations, to the "psychology of social man" conditioned by that order, and finally to the ideologies that crystallize and express that psychology (Plekhanov 1947). Plekhanov's insistence, at each stage, that the prior term only "sets limits and tendencies" rather than dictating content is the schema's load-bearing claim: it is what distinguishes his historical materialism from the crude base-superstructure reductionism his Narodnik opponents attributed to Marxism. The fourth term — "social psychology" — does the most theoretical work in this respect. It is posited as an irreducible mediating layer between economic relations and ideology precisely because ideas cannot, on Plekhanov's account, be inferred directly from economic position; a social psychology shaped by, but not identical with, the socio-political order is needed to explain why a given economic structure can support a range of ideological expressions rather than a single determinate one.

Brazevich's (2021) reconstruction of the intellectual genealogy behind this schema shows how deliberately assembled it was: Plekhanov drew the concept of class struggle from the French Restoration historians, the idea of "social environment" as a proto-economic-relations concept from the same source, the dialectical treatment of social change from Hegel, and the notion of social progress from the utopian socialists, treating each as a partial approximation superseded by Marx's synthesis. This lineage matters analytically because it shows the five-fold formula was not simply asserted but built to answer a specific historiographical problem — how prior "monist" theories of history had each isolated one factor (ideas, great men, race, the physical environment) as causally decisive, and how Marx's schema, on Plekhanov's reading, integrated these prior candidates into a single ordered chain rather than discarding them. Soboleva's (2021) account of Plekhanov's Spinozist reading of Marxism — the claim, reportedly made to Engels, that "Marxism is a variety of Spinozism" — supplies the metaphysical warrant behind calling this a *monist* view of history at all: the five terms are conceived as expressions of a single underlying substance rather than as externally related causes, a point taken up more fully as the ontological substructure of the schema in Section 2. A recent reconstruction by Hu (2026), drawing on Plekhanov's concept of society as a "social organism," argues — cautiously, since the underlying article's abstract could not be recovered and the claim rests on its bibliographic annotation — that Plekhanov's framework distinguishes "conditions" from "causes" in a way that embeds even the first term of the formula, the productive forces, within mediated natural and ecological constraints, rather than treating productive forces as a self-standing economic starting point.

What is notable, however, is the absence of a sustained, dedicated philosophical treatment of the formula as a whole: discussion of its structure is distributed piecemeal across Baron's biographical framing, Walicki's intellectual history, and Brazevich's genealogical reconstruction, none of which subjects the five terms themselves to systematic logical scrutiny — a gap the conclusion returns to.

### 1.2 Freedom, necessity, and the role of the individual

Plekhanov's solution to the problem of historical agency is explicitly compatibilist, modeled on Spinoza and Hegel: freedom is "the recognition of necessity," and outstanding individuals are causally efficacious only within, and because of, an underlying necessity of social-economic development (Plekhanov 1940). In *The Role of the Individual in History*, he argues that talented individuals can accelerate or retard the tempo of a historical process whose overall direction remains fixed, and that their significance is itself a function of contingent features of social organization — most famously, the contingent absence of equally talented rivals who might otherwise have occupied the same historical role (Plekhanov 1940).

This is precisely the point at which Shaw's (1988) philosophical reconstruction locates an instability. Shaw argues that the very conditions Plekhanov invokes to explain why a given individual mattered threaten to undo the theory from within: if an individual's causal significance depends on the contingent absence of rivals capable of performing the same historical function, then either that contingency is itself compatible with strict determinism — in which case Plekhanov owes an account of why such contingencies do not simply relocate historical necessity one level down without altering its force — or the individual's contribution is genuinely contingent in a way that makes the appeal to necessity look considerably weaker than the "role of the individual" essay's rhetoric suggests. Shaw's critique is sympathetic to the project of finding a middle path between rigid determinism and unconstrained voluntarism, but it presses on the seam where the theory is least stable: exactly the cases where an individual's presence appears to matter are the cases hardest to reconcile with the claim that historical direction was already fixed.

Hook's *The Hero in History* (2023 [1943]) reaches a structurally similar conclusion by an entirely different route. Rather than analyzing Plekhanov's text philosophically, Hook treats the Russian Revolution as an empirical test case for orthodox Marxist determinism, arguing that while the February 1917 revolution can plausibly be read as an inevitable outcome of prior conditions, the October seizure of power was, on the best historical evidence, contingent on the specific personality and decisions of Lenin in a way that strict Plekhanovian determinism cannot readily absorb. That an analytic critic working from Plekhanov's own conceptual apparatus (Shaw) and a historically minded critic working from the test case of 1917 (Hook) converge on skepticism about the individual/necessity synthesis — from different directions and without deriving one line of criticism from the other — indicates that the tension is not an artifact of a single interpretive approach but recurs across independent modes of assessment.

Mayer's (1993) account of the evolution of the concept of "dictatorship of the proletariat" from Plekhanov to Lenin supplies one instance of what this instability could mean in practice. According to Mayer's reconstruction — cited here cautiously via its bibliographic annotation, since no abstract could be recovered — Plekhanov's insistence on the necessity of Russia passing through determinate developmental stages before socialism was possible shaped a stagist, deterministic reading of the dictatorship-of-the-proletariat concept that Lenin's more voluntarist appropriation, and eventual departure from it after 1917, effectively overtook. The doctrinal continuity-and-rupture Mayer traces is a political enactment of the same fault line Shaw and Hook diagnose conceptually and historically: a determinism that grants individuals a circumscribed accelerating role finds that role, in practice, difficult to keep circumscribed.

### 1.3 The critique of Narodnik subjectivist sociology

Plekhanov developed his monist determinism polemically, above all against the Narodnik ("subjectivist") sociology of N. K. Mikhailovsky and P. L. Lavrov, who held that history is made by "critically thinking individuals" applying a moral ideal rather than by impersonal social-economic laws (Plekhanov 1947). Teslya's (2021) reconstruction of the early formation of Mikhailovsky's "subjective sociology" complicates the picture Plekhanov's polemics convey. On Teslya's account, Mikhailovsky's theory was a considered response to Darwinism and to sociological organicism, and — unlike Lavrov's — was explicitly non-teleological: Mikhailovsky treated history as "logical" yet free of strict determinism, resisting the assimilation of social to natural-scientific law rather than simply asserting that ideal-driven individuals could remake history at will. If Teslya's reconstruction is accurate, Plekhanov's critique targets a simplified version of subjective sociology, one that elides Mikhailovsky's own resistance to naive voluntarism in favor of a more polemically convenient opponent.

Walicki's (1979) synthetic account situates this polemic within the broader Populism-to-Marxism transition, treating Lavrov, Mikhailovsky, and Plekhanov within a single continuous narrative in which Plekhanov's monist historical materialism is constructed specifically as a rebuttal of the claim that Russia's historical destiny remained open to being shaped by the moral will of an enlightened minority. Walicki's framing — continuity and rupture at once — allows the fairness question to be posed sharply: Plekhanov's determinism was forged in direct opposition to a subjectivist rival whose own account of historical explanation, per Teslya, was not the untethered voluntarism the polemic requires it to be. This raises a question-begging structure internal to the critique itself. If Mikhailovsky already denied that individuals could redirect history by will alone, then Plekhanov's insistence on necessity as the corrective to subjectivism proves less against Mikhailovsky's actual position than against a caricature — and it does so by presupposing precisely the determinism that is supposed to be established by the argument. The polemical origin of Plekhanov's determinism, on this reading, is inseparable from a degree of question-begging that recurs, in a different register, in the individual/necessity tension examined in 1.2.

### 1.4 How deterministic was it? Stagism, geography, and mediation

The abstract determinism of the five-fold schema was operationalized, in Plekhanov's political writings, as a specifically Russian "stagism": the claim that Russia's social-economic development must pass through determinate stages — a bourgeois-democratic revolution, then a protracted period of capitalist development — before socialism becomes possible. Van Ree's (2013) analysis of the shift between "Socialism and Political Struggle" (1883) and "Our Differences" (1885) argues that this was not a straightforward application of the *Communist Manifesto*'s scenario of proletarian revolution led by a small industrial working class, but a substantive departure: Plekhanov predicated the future socialist revolution on the prior proletarianization of the majority of the Russian population and full industrialization, a novel strategic reading rather than a derivative one. This matters for assessing the determinism question because it shows Plekhanov's philosophy of history was not merely descriptive but generated a specific, falsifiable prediction about Russian development — one that sharpens, rather than settles, how deterministic the theory actually was in practice.

A second, adjudicated dispute concerns whether Plekhanov's materialism should be read as narrowly economic or as incorporating a more mediated environmental layer. Bassin (1992) argues that Plekhanov placed environmentalist principles, drawn substantially from the geographer Friedrich Ratzel, at the very foundation of his social theory, while insisting that this geographical determinism operated within, rather than against, Marx's own framework: geography sets initial conditions and limits the possibilities open to a society, but the causally decisive factors remain the economic relations of production, since environmental factors alone cannot explain class dynamics or revolutionary change. On this reading, Plekhanov used geographical determinism specifically to explain the "backwardness" of Russia relative to Western Europe, not to displace historical materialism's economic core. Peet (1993), publishing a formal rejoinder in the same journal, disputes Bassin's characterization, representing what Bassin's own summary calls the "economic primacy" pole against Bassin's "environment-conditions-economy" reading; the specific textual argument of Peet's critique beyond this framing is not recoverable from the available record and is not asserted further here. The exchange itself is significant independently of which side is judged correct: it demonstrates that even the comparatively narrow question of Plekhanov's geographical determinism remains a live, specialist disagreement rather than a settled matter, and it complicates any simple identification of Plekhanov's materialism with "vulgar" economic reductionism.

Hu's (2026) reconstruction of Plekhanov's "social organism" concept — again cited cautiously from its annotated summary — extends this complication by proposing that Plekhanov's distinction between "conditions" and "causes" allowed him to situate natural and ecological constraints within mediated processes of social reproduction, avoiding both crude environmental determinism and unconstrained social constructivism. Read together, van Ree's stagism, the Bassin–Peet exchange, and Hu's mediation thesis suggest that Plekhanov's determinism, whatever its ultimate coherence, was considerably more layered — geographically, developmentally, and organically — than a flat "productive forces determine everything" caricature allows.

**Section summary.** Across the five-fold formula, the individual/necessity dialectic, the Narodnik polemic, and the stagist-geographical operationalization of the theory, a consistent pattern emerges: Plekhanov's determinism is qualified, mediated, and historically specific rather than mechanical at almost every level he articulates it explicitly. Yet the qualification does not resolve into a stable account once individual agency is asked to do real explanatory work — Shaw's philosophical critique and Hook's historical test case converge independently on this point — and the determinism's polemical origin against a Narodnik target reconstructed more carefully by Teslya suggests the critique that grounds it may itself presuppose what it claims to demonstrate. This fault line, more than any simple charge of economic reductionism, is what the rest of this review tracks through Plekhanov's epistemology, his aesthetics, his political trajectory, and his reception.

## Section 2: Philosophical Foundations: Monism, Epistemology, and Fidelity

The five-fold formula reconstructed in Section 1 rests on two further claims that require independent scrutiny: an ontological claim that reality and history form a single, non-dualistic material order, and an epistemological claim that this order is genuinely knowable despite the mediated character of sensation. Both claims were contested by Plekhanov's contemporaries, and both bear on a further question — whether the resulting system, which Plekhanov named "dialectical materialism," faithfully transmits Marx's and Engels's own views or substantially revises them.

### 2.1 Monism via Spinoza

Plekhanov grounded his monism partly through a materialist reading of Spinoza, treating substance monism as a theologically clothed anticipation of dialectical materialism whose "theological" vocabulary he dismissed as a historical relic of Spinoza's own epoch (Steila 2021). Steila situates this reading against Lunacharsky's rival interpretation, which took Spinoza's religious language seriously as evidence that Spinoza's "religious feelings" — not merely his ontology — made him a genuine forerunner of Marxism, showing that Spinoza's status was contested territory even among self-identified materialists. Steinberg (2024) evaluates this reading comparatively against those of Plekhanov's followers Deborin and Akselrod: Deborin's identification of Spinozist Nature with matter fails as Spinoza interpretation, and Akselrod's asymmetric-dependence account of the idea/body relation fails both as interpretation and as materialism, leaving Plekhanov's reading — which treats materialism as compatible with panpsychism — as, in Steinberg's assessment, the most plausible of the three, while noting that Spinoza's own metaphysics is not best described as materialism at all. This comparative verdict does not settle the matter internally to Russian Marxism, however. Soboleva (2021) reconstructs Bogdanov's contemporaneous objection to Plekhanov's alignment of Spinoza with Marxism, distinguishing a hermeneutical charge — that Plekhanov dogmatizes and instrumentalizes historical concepts by disregarding their original context — from a systematic one concerning the proper relation between extension and thought. Out of this second objection Bogdanov developed his rival "empiriomonist" theory of cognition. The Spinozist grounding of Plekhanov's monism was thus contested from its first articulation, both on grounds of textual fidelity to Spinoza and on grounds of philosophical coherence.

### 2.2 The hieroglyph theory of knowledge and its critics

Plekhanov's epistemology combined this ontological monism with a Helmholtzian "hieroglyph" or sign theory of sensation, on which sensations are conventional signs correlating reliably with an external, mind-independent reality rather than literal copies or "images" of it (Wagner 1993, reporting Steila's monograph-length reconstruction). This move let Plekhanov hold a strong realist ontology together with an admission that perception is mediated, but it drew a documented objection from Engels that treating sensations as mere hieroglyphs risked reintroducing Kantian agnosticism about the thing-in-itself. The objection proved durable. Rybas (2023) shows that Russian empiriocritics — Bogdanov, Yushkevich, Lesevich, and Lunacharsky — opposed Plekhanov's identification of the thing-in-itself with the material object on the grounds that this identification is itself an unwarranted metaphysical assumption, incapable of refuting either dualism or Kantian agnosticism; from their monistic standpoint, the thing-in-itself should instead be understood as a necessary form of the organization of experience. Rumyantseva (2025) extends this critical assessment by examining Plekhanov's polemics against the German Neo-Kantians and Eduard Bernstein, arguing that Plekhanov's professed "defence" of Kant was chiefly instrumental — aimed at shielding Marx and Engels from revisionism — and that Plekhanov himself, in the process, misinterpreted aspects of Kant's own doctrine of the thing-in-itself. The stakes of this epistemological dispute extended beyond the nature of sensation to the nature of Marxist theory itself: Pavlov (2017) reconstructs the parallel Plekhanov–Bogdanov debate over the status of truth, framing it as a contest over whether Marxism operates with a revisable, scientific theory of truth or transmits a set of absolute truths as doctrine. The hieroglyph controversy also had a long afterlife: Sériot (2022) traces how the Marxist-Leninist dispute over the sign/referent relation — inaugurated by Lenin's 1909 polemic against Mach and, by extension, against hieroglyph-style theories of sensation — resurfaced in the 1930s–1950s Soviet charge that Saussure's linguistics itself constituted a "bourgeois idealist" theory of hieroglyphics, indicating that the epistemological question Plekhanov's sign theory raised remained unsettled well into the Soviet period rather than being resolved by Lenin's own reflection theory (examined further in Section 5).

### 2.3 Fidelity to Marx, Engels, and Hegel

These foundational commitments raise a further question: how much of "dialectical materialism" reflects Marx's and Engels's own views, and how much is Plekhanov's systematization or revision of them? White (1996) offers a genealogy of dialectical materialism that traces its path from Hegel and the Left Hegelians through Marx's own writings to the Russian Social-Democratic milieu of the 1890s from which Plekhanov's formulation, and subsequently Lenin's, emerged, and in doing so questions any unbroken continuity between Marx's own thought and the doctrine Plekhanov named and systematized. Moore (1971) locates a comparable difficulty already within Marx himself, independent of Plekhanov: he dates the origin of dialectical materialism to Marx's 1857 return to studying Hegel, when Marx combined a realist epistemology opposed to Hegel with a Hegel-influenced dialectical method for deriving the labor theory of value, producing a tension between materialism and dialectic that, on Moore's reading, Marx never fully resolved. This suggests that whatever incoherence critics find in Plekhanov's monism may partly inherit an unresolved tension in Marx's own texts rather than originate with Plekhanov's exposition of them. Van Ree (2013) documents a more specific and textually grounded case of substantive revision on Plekhanov's part: comparing "Socialism and the Political Struggle" (1883) with "Our Differences" (1885), he argues that Plekhanov — while presenting himself as simply applying the Communist Manifesto's revolutionary scenario to Russian conditions — was in fact the first Marxist to reject Marx's original scenario of proletarian revolution by numerically small proletariats, instead predicating revolution on the prior proletarianization and industrialization of the majority of the population. Taken together, these three studies suggest that Plekhanov functioned as a systematizer and occasional reviser of the Marx-Hegel-Feuerbach lineage rather than a neutral transmitter of a settled orthodoxy — a finding that reframes the question, taken up in Section 5, of whether Plekhanov should be credited with originating "dialectical materialism" or charged with distorting it.

The philosophical foundations examined here are accordingly more contested, and in some respects more sophisticated, than the "vulgar materialist" label implies: the Spinozist monism was defended comparatively well against rival Marxist readings even as it drew serious internal objections, and the hieroglyph epistemology represented a considered attempt to reconcile realism with mediated perception rather than a naive reflectionism. Yet both the coherence of the monism and the fidelity of the resulting system to Marx's own texts remain genuinely open questions — questions that the philosophy of history's five-fold formula and its treatment of individual agency (Section 1) inherit rather than resolve.

## Section 3: Aesthetics and the Sociological Theory of Art

If Section 1 identifies "social psychology" and ideology as the mediating tiers of Plekhanov's five-fold formula, his aesthetics supplies the most fully worked-out instance of that tier in practice, and the debate it has generated in miniature reproduces the review's master-tension between rigorous determinism and interpretive nuance. Developed chiefly in "Letters Without Address" (1899–1900) and "Art and Social Life" (1912–1913) — collected in English as a single composite volume (Plekhanov and Fineberg 1957) — Plekhanov's theory of art was his attempt to extend historical materialism from politics and ideology to aesthetic experience, and it has since become a recurring test case for whether his method escapes the "vulgar Marxist" charge levelled against his philosophy of history more broadly.

### 3.1 The genetic theory and the critique of "art for art's sake"

Plekhanov's starting point is genetic and anthropological rather than evaluative: engaging critically with Karl Bücher's and Herbert Spencer's labor- and play-theories of the origin of art, he argues that among primitive peoples utilitarian labor precedes and generates aesthetic play, so that "utility precedes aesthetic enjoyment" in the historical development of humanity (Plekhanov and Fineberg 1957). From this derivation he builds his central polemical target: "art for art's sake" is not a perennial aesthetic option but a historically specific ideological response, paradigmatically that of the French intelligentsia after 1848, whose estrangement from a viable social role expressed itself as a doctrine of disinterested contemplation — to be contrasted with periods, such as the Enlightenment or Belinsky-era Russian criticism, in which utilitarian, socially engaged art predominates. Toma (2025) situates this move within a longer indigenous genealogy, tracing the politicization of art in the Russian left-wing intelligentsia from Chernyshevsky's utilitarian criticism through Plekhanov's founding of a distinctively Russian Marxist aesthetic theory to its eventual political practice under Lenin, rather than treating Plekhanov's aesthetics as a derivative application of Western Marxism. Nascimento (2014) offers a comparable reconstruction centered on "Art and Social Life," crediting Plekhanov with opening what was, prior to his intervention, an unexplored area of Marxist theory through his dialogue with the Belinsky tradition, while also identifying what he terms a "restrictive method" running through Plekhanov's criticism — a qualification that anticipates the reductionism debate taken up below.

### 3.2 The reductionism debate

The question these reconstructions leave open — whether Plekhanov's genetic and historicizing moves cohere with a defensible account of specifically aesthetic value — was posed directly in the one sustained pre-2000s English-language exchange on his aesthetics. Baxandall (1967) credits Plekhanov with producing the first systematic Marxist aesthetic theory but argues that his method of translating art into "the language of sociology" tends toward a reductive determinism that subordinates the aesthetic dimension of a work to its social and class origins. Truitt's contemporaneous reply defends Plekhanov against this charge, arguing that Baxandall's reading mischaracterizes a more historically nuanced account of the relation between social development and artistic form (Truitt 1970). Della Volpe (1978) presses a related but independently motivated critique from within Marxist aesthetics itself, developing a rival theory of art's rational, conceptual content that names Plekhanov's (and, alongside him, Lukács's) "sociological reductionism" as a target to be overcome rather than extended — evidence that the reductionism charge was not confined to non-Marxist critics. Lu Yan (2015) documents a further, distinct line of dissent: Lefebvre's "Introduction to Aesthetics" argues against Plekhanov by emphasizing the spiritual rather than the material dimension of the art–social life relation, extending the debate's afterlife into later Western Marxist aesthetics.

Sockol's more recent work reframes the entire exchange. Rather than treating Plekhanov's fin-de-siècle writings as naive reflectionism, Sockol (2023) reads them as polemical interventions directed simultaneously against Kantian aesthetic disinterestedness and against contemporary critics of Marxism, in which the stress on class conflict as formative of artistic creation issues in a deliberately activist, "tendentious" aesthetic aimed at mobilizing art for revolutionary ends rather than a mechanical reflection theory. This revisionist reading does not, however, sever Plekhanov from later doctrinal history: Sockol's earlier study argues that the same prescriptive framework — combining hostility to bourgeois and modernist art with support for realistic, utilitarian art — anticipated the tenets of Socialist Realism, tracing an intellectual line of descent that he takes to be neglected relative to other proposed origins of the Soviet doctrine (Sockol 2011). Read together, the two studies suggest a position at once more argumentatively coherent and more historically consequential than either the "vulgar Marxism" charge or a simple vindication would allow — a pattern that recurs, for the philosophy of history as a whole, in Section 5's account of the "vulgar materialist" label.

## Section 4: Political Trajectory and the Determinism/Voluntarism Problem

Plekhanov's political career supplies the practical counterpart to the deterministic philosophy of history reconstructed in Section 1, and it does so in a way that repeatedly strains against that philosophy rather than simply illustrating it. The 1883 founding of the Emancipation of Labour group already displays the pattern: Plekhanov and his co-founders broke with Narodism not by deducing a party program from the five-fold schema but by taking a practical stand against individual terrorism. Gaido and Bosch Alessio (2015) show that this founding commitment was reaffirmed two decades later when Vera Zasulich, one of the group's five founders, published a 1902 polemic against the newly formed Socialist-Revolutionary Party's revival of terrorist tactics, a polemic that aligned the Emancipation of Labour veterans with the organizational, mass-party logic Lenin was then developing in *What Is to Be Done?*. Gaido and Bosch Alessio read this alignment as complicating any narrative in which Menshevism was anti-organizational from the outset: Plekhanov's later break with Lenin was a dispute over the *form* of centralization, not over its principle.

That dispute crystallized at the Second Congress of 1903. Gaido and Jozami (2017) argue that the centralized "cadre party" model usually credited to Lenin alone was in fact the collective project of the Iskra editorial board, on which Plekhanov sat, adapting German Social Democratic practice to Russian clandestine conditions and to factional struggle against the Economists and the Bund. On this reading, Plekhanov's subsequent decision to side with Martov's minority against Lenin's party-rules proposal was a rupture within a shared organizational project rather than the expression of a prior theoretical disagreement about the class character of the coming revolution. The split did not settle the matter: Elwood (2011) documents the International Socialist Bureau's July 1914 Brussels "Unity" conference, the last pre-war attempt to reconcile the factions, at which Plekhanov, Trotsky, Aksel'rod, and Martov confronted Lenin's delegate Inessa Armand. Elwood treats the RSDLP split as an actively contested organizational problem still unresolved on the eve of the war, rather than a fact settled in 1903 — a reminder that Plekhanov's political positioning continued to be negotiated in real time rather than derived once and for all from doctrine.

The theoretical premise underwriting Plekhanov's Menshevik strategy was his account of Russia's belated and uneven capitalist development. Baron (1958) reconstructs Plekhanov's picture of Russia as a society shaped by "Oriental" or Asiatic state forms onto which Western capitalist relations were subsequently and unevenly imposed — a framework used to argue that Russia had entered, but not completed, the capitalist stage presupposed by orthodox Marxist theory, and hence that a bourgeois-democratic revolution had to precede any socialist one. (This account survives only through the paper's bibliographic notes rather than a recoverable abstract, but Baron's standing as Plekhanov's principal biographer, together with the substantive claims transmitted in the note, warrants cautious reliance on it here.) This "two-stage" schema was not simply asserted; Avila and Gaido (2017) reconstruct how Plekhanov himself subjected it to a real test in October 1906, circulating a questionnaire to the leaders of international Social Democracy asking about the class character of the 1905 Revolution. Twelve replies overwhelmingly endorsed the Menshevik view that the revolution was bourgeois and that cooperation with liberal opposition was warranted — but Kautsky's reply rejected any coalition with the bourgeoisie, undercutting the Menshevik position from within the very poll Plekhanov had organized to confirm it. Thatcher (2021) shows that Menshevik theorists nonetheless pursued the schema empirically after 1905, producing a largely overlooked collective study analyzing the revolution class by class, evidence that stagism functioned as an ongoing research program rather than static doctrine even as its international standing had already been contested by Kautsky. Day (2009) situates this fracture within a wider field of live alternatives, tracing "permanent revolution" as a rival schema developed by Trotsky and Parvus that collapsed the bourgeois and socialist stages Plekhanov's theory kept separate — evidence that Menshevik stagism was one contested position among several, not an uncontroversial deduction from historical materialism.

The final and starkest instance of the pattern is 1914 "defencism." Baron (1981) traces Plekhanov's support for Russia's war effort and his consequent break with the Zimmerwald-aligned internationalist wing of Russian and European socialism, a position that isolated him politically through 1917 and that Baron reads as continuous with, rather than a rupture from, his prior political trajectory. Across these episodes — the 1883 break, the 1903 split, the 1906 questionnaire's unintended exposure of stagism, and 1914 defencism — no single work in this literature (nor, as a brief conference overview by Zhuravleva et al. 2019 confirms, the broader biographical record) treats the relationship between Plekhanov's historical determinism and his own decisive political improvisation as its central subject. It emerges instead as a background problem that the political narrative continually poses without resolving: a theorist committed to a fixed sequence of historical stages who nonetheless had, at each hinge point, to act, split, or dissent before that sequence could confirm or refute him. This gap sets the empirical stakes for the "tragedy" narratives examined in Section 5.

## Section 5: Influence, Reception, and the Historiographical Verdict

If the philosophy of history is where Plekhanov's determinism is constructed, the literature on his influence and reception is where it is judged — and judged inconsistently. Two registers of assessment need distinguishing. The first traces how Plekhanov's materialism was transmitted, retained, or revised as it passed through Lenin into Soviet orthodoxy and laterally into Western Marxism. The second is meta-historiographical: the recurring labels "vulgar materialist," "father of Bolshevism," and "tragic Menshevik" are not neutral descriptions but verdicts shaped by the political commitments of the critics who issued them. Both registers return the review's master-tension — determinism versus agency, and the charge of vulgar mechanism — to the surface, since it is precisely Plekhanov's compatibilist synthesis and its political enactment that later assessors argue about.

### 5.1 Lenin, reflection theory, and Soviet diamat

Lenin's 1908 *Materialism and Empirio-Criticism* adopted Plekhanov's reflection theory of knowledge and his polemical posture toward "Machism," making Plekhanov's epistemology the philosophical basis of what would become Soviet dialectical materialism. Whether this debt persisted is contested. Bonilla (2020) argues that Lenin's 1915 commentaries on Hegel's *Science of Logic* present a different, more dialectical theory of reflection than the 1908 text: reflection becomes an adaptive, reciprocal relation in which the movement of knowledge follows and influences the movement of reality, a "moment" of human objectification rather than a passive copying of an external world. On this reading, Lenin's Hegelian turn constitutes a substantive philosophical rupture with the Plekhanov-derived epistemology of his earlier work. Oittinen (2018) argues the opposite on two fronts. First, there is no textual evidence that Lenin changed position after 1914, since his post-1917 criticism of Bukharin's failure to apply dialectics shows continuity with the 1907 framework. Second, and more pointedly, Oittinen denies that Plekhanov was ever the source of Lenin's governing conception of dialectics — "concrete analysis of a concrete situation" — which he traces instead to the Narodnik writer Alexander Herzen. Bonilla and Oittinen therefore disagree not only about whether a rupture occurred but about whether Plekhanov was ever the operative source to rupture from, leaving the Lenin–Plekhanov relationship without a settled account in the literature.

The stakes of this disagreement extend beyond textual exegesis to the question of whether Soviet Marxism-Leninism inherited a rigid or a self-correcting epistemology. Pateman (2019) intervenes on this question directly, arguing through textual analysis that Lenin's epistemology is fallibilist rather than dogmatic: *Materialism and Empirio-Criticism* affirms uncertainty across the natural, philosophical, and social sciences and is inconsistent with the "vulgarizer" caricature routinely applied to Lenin — and, by extension, to the Plekhanovite materialism he adopted. This defense stands against the Western critical consensus, discussed below, that treats the reflection-theoretic inheritance as the root of Marxism-Leninism's dogmatism. Antonov (2021) shows that the underlying philosophical content remains unsettled on its own terms: revisiting the Lenin–empiriocritic dispute over the definition and mode of existence of matter, he argues that Lenin considered the question only ontologically and gnoseologically, and that Lenin's own principle of the identity of dialectics, logic, and theory of knowledge requires a logical supplement that would revise the canonical definition — treating the Plekhanov/Lenin account of matter as still-contested philosophy rather than closed orthodoxy. The reflection theory's institutional afterlife is documented, with more caution given its INCOMPLETE sourcing, by Mareeva (2016), who characterizes Mikhail Lifshitz's Soviet-era "ontognoseology" — developed within Lukács's Moscow circle in the 1930s — as an elaboration of Lenin's theory of reflection within Soviet aesthetic philosophy, tracing the doctrine's survival well beyond the epistemological dispute that produced it and into the aesthetics domain treated in Section 3.

### 5.2 Lateral and Western Marxist reception

Plekhanov's materialism also had lateral relations and a hostile reception outside the Leninist line. Savino (2018) documents that Antonio Labriola was a significant theoretical authority for Russian Social Democrats abroad, and that Gramsci's concept of praxis and rejection of positivism derive substantially from Labriola while also engaging Russian Marxism — establishing a connection between Plekhanov's tradition and Italian anti-positivist "philosophy of praxis" that ran mainly through Labriola's broader influence rather than direct exchange between the two theorists. Colletti (1971) gives this current a precise textual anchor by defining "the Marxism of the Second International" narrowly as the Marxism of Kautsky and Plekhanov, and by locating much of it already in Engels's late works — situating Plekhanov's codification of dialectical materialism as continuous with late Engelsian materialism rather than an independent departure.

That codification became, for council communists and later the Frankfurt School, the principal target of Western Marxist anti-Leninism. Savin (2020) reconstructs how Anton Pannekoek's *Lenin as Philosopher* attacked Lenin's — and, behind him, Plekhanov's — natural-scientific materialism as a bourgeois-Enlightenment inheritance rather than genuine historical materialism, a critique transmitted to the Frankfurt School chiefly through Marcuse's *Soviet Marxism* (1958) and thereafter standard in Western-left framings of Soviet diamat. Georg Lukács offers the most developed alternative within this current: his Hegelian-praxis reading of Lenin, presented in *History and Class Consciousness* and *Lenin: A Study on the Unity of His Thought* (Lukács 1971 [1924]), substitutes a dialectic of totality and praxis for reflection-theoretic materialism, a shift traced biographically by Szabados and Sautkin (2021) from Lukács's early aestheticism through his 1918 turn to Marxism and the failure of the 1919 Hungarian Soviet Republic. Le Blanc (2013) complicates the standard placement of this work as an "ultra-left" or "proto-Stalinist" deviation, arguing that Lukács's 1923–29 writings form a coherent Leninist-Trotskyist political position developed during his factional leadership, alongside Jenő Landler, against Béla Kun's sectarianism in the Hungarian Communist Party — reframing Lukács's Hegelianism as concretely factional-political rather than a purely philosophical alternative to Plekhanovite materialism. The broader post-war Marxological synthesis of these critiques appears, with the caution warranted by their INCOMPLETE sourcing, in Kolakowski (1978) and Acton (1955), both of which place Plekhanov's and Kautsky's codification of dialectical materialism at the origin of a Marxism-Leninism subsequently judged, from within Western Marxism and from liberal-analytic philosophy respectively, as scientistic or internally incoherent.

### 5.3 The historiographical verdicts: "vulgar materialist," "father of Bolshevism," "tragic Menshevik"

The meta-level literature treats these three labels as contested verdicts rather than settled descriptions, and it divides along the lines of who is doing the judging. Within Soviet historiography itself, Baron (1974a) documents that "Plekhanovism" was treated pejoratively under Stalin because of Plekhanov's Menshevism and wartime "defencism," then selectively rehabilitated after Stalin's death, with his theoretical authority reaffirmed even as his political record remained officially condemned; Baron (1974b) extends the analysis by showing that Plekhanov's historiographical standing was entangled with the parallel, shifting fate of Trotsky in official Soviet memory. Ulam (2024 [1964]) supplies the sharpest formulation of the resulting dilemma, comparing Plekhanov's position in Soviet historiography to "the bride's divorced father who shows up at the wedding": honored as the father of Russian Marxism yet unable to be treated as a straightforward forerunner of Bolshevism, he must be seated somewhere, with a degree of reverence that shifting party doctrine could never specify in advance.

Politically engaged Marxist assessments share a rhetoric of tragedy while locating its cause differently, and the outline's staged debate is worth making explicit. Deutscher (1964) credits Plekhanov with a "brilliant intellectual conquest" in transplanting Marxism to Russia and casts him, via a comparison to Erasmus, as possessing refined theoretical intelligence but lacking the "oaken strength" revolutionary practice required — so that his 1903 stand with Lenin, his subsequent rejection of Lenin's peasant strategy as a lapse into Populism, and his 1914 "social patriotism" together exhibit a coherent mismatch between intellectual temperament and revolutionary ruthlessness. Cliff (1957) locates the failure elsewhere: Plekhanov's decisive weakness, on this reading, was his lack of direct experience organizing workers, which left him a theoretician isolated from the proletariat and pushed him toward compromise with the liberal bourgeoisie. North and Volkov (2016) locate it differently again, in the internal logic of Plekhanov's own theory: his identification of the working class as Russia's decisive revolutionary force was a theoretical achievement that made October conceivable even as Plekhanov personally opposed it, because his two-stage theory of revolution was overtaken by events rather than abandoned through personal weakness — a reading the authors connect explicitly to the genesis of Trotsky's theory of permanent revolution. Temperament, organizational isolation, and theoretical overtaking by events thus compete as causal accounts of the same "tragedy," and none is treated in the literature as having settled the question.

A further, reflexive layer questions the assessors themselves. Carr's essay judges Plekhanov against Lenin's standard and endorses, per Berlin's contemporaneous review, the Communist historian A. Rothstein's verdict that Plekhanov's "unforgivable sin" was refusing to follow his former pupil into October (Carr 2021 [1950]). Berlin (1951) responds that Carr, previously a historian of ironic detachment, has fallen "under the spell" of the "Welthistorisch individuals who make history," and reads Carr's harshness toward Plekhanov as a symptom of pro-Lenin sympathy rather than dispassionate judgment — making Berlin's review itself a data point about how Cold War historiographical loyalties shaped Plekhanov's reputation. Western scholarly reception of the Baron biographical project registered a comparatively steady assessment: McNeal (1964) reviews Baron's biography favorably, judging its treatment of the revisionism and economism debates lucid and the whole a study worthy of an important subject. Against this backdrop, White (2023) argues in a recent reassessment that Plekhanov remains comparatively neglected in Western scholarship despite a continuing, largely unacknowledged influence, framing his legacy as paradoxical: politically rejected in 1917, yet theoretically preserved by the very Soviet leadership that defeated him.

Taken together, this section shows that the labels "vulgar materialist," "father of Bolshevism," and "tragic Menshevik" each encode a specific judgment about the same underlying problem this review has tracked from Section 1 onward — whether Plekhanov's determinism was too rigid to accommodate agency, and whose agency (Lenin's, the working class's, Plekhanov's own) exposed that rigidity when history moved. The reception debates do not resolve this question; they relocate it into competing narratives about cause and blame, setting up the conclusion's return to the philosophical problem beneath the historiography.

## Conclusion

Across the four substantive domains surveyed here — the foundational ontology, the aesthetics, the political trajectory, and the reception history — a consolidating finding emerges: the charge that Plekhanov's materialism is "vulgar" or mechanistic is increasingly contested rather than confirmed. Bassin (1992) shows that Plekhanov's engagement with Ratzel's environmental determinism operates through mediating social categories rather than direct geographic causation, complicating readings that reduce his materialism to a narrow economism. Sockol (2023) reframes the aesthetic theory examined in Section 3 as an anti-Kantian, activist polemic rather than a naive reflectionism, against the Baxandall–Truitt line that treated it as reductive "translation." Steinberg (2024) offers a non-partisan assessment of Plekhanov's Spinozist monism as a defensible, non-reductive reading among Russian Marxists rather than a crude economistic substitute for dialectic. Pateman (2019) mounts a fallibilist defense of the reflection-theoretic epistemology against the dogmatism charge leveled at Lenin and, by extension, Plekhanov. Hu's reconstruction likewise resists reading Plekhanov's "social organism" model as flatly determinist. Taken together, these positions converge on a revisionist current that softens the caricature without eliminating a deeper, unresolved problem: the determinism/individual-agency tension identified independently by Shaw (1988), working from within analytic philosophy of history, and Hook, testing the same fault line historically through the counterfactual role of Lenin in October 1917. That two critics working from different disciplinary starting points arrive at convergent skepticism about whether Plekhanov's compatibilism can accommodate genuine individual causal efficacy indicates that this tension, rather than the vulgar-materialist label, is the more durable interpretive problem — one Plekhanov's own political life, oscillating between doctrinal fixity and repeated decisive improvisation in 1883, 1903, and 1914, appears to enact rather than resolve.

Several questions remain genuinely open. The five-fold formula and its "social psychology" mediating tier, reconstructed in Section 1, have not received a rigorous dedicated study; treatment of the formula is distributed across biographical (Baron), synthetic (Walicki), and more recent (Brazevich) accounts, none of which isolates the tier's explanatory work as a philosophical problem in its own right. The coherence of Plekhanov's compatibilism — whether "freedom is the recognition of necessity" can be squared with the causal weight he assigns to individuals — remains unsettled despite Shaw's and Hook's parallel critiques. The dispute over whether Lenin's post-1914 turn to Hegel constituted a rupture with Plekhanovian orthodoxy (Bonilla) or a continuity sourced elsewhere, in Herzen rather than Plekhanov (Oittinen), has not been adjudicated. And post-Soviet, Russian-language reassessments of Plekhanov appear underrepresented in the Anglophone literature surveyed, leaving open how far the revisionist current identified above extends beyond the English-language record consulted here.

These debates connect at a structural level: the historiographical verdicts on Plekhanov — "father of Bolshevism," "vulgar materialist," "tragic Menshevik" — are inseparable from judgments about his political trajectory, and those judgments are in turn shaped by Cold War and factional-Marxist commitments rather than by philosophical analysis of the five-fold schema alone. Evaluating Plekhanov's philosophy of history on its own terms therefore requires disentangling the philosophical assessment of his determinism and compatibilism from the political verdict on his Menshevism, a separation the literature surveyed here does not consistently maintain. A philosophically focused re-examination of the five-fold formula and the freedom/necessity synthesis, brought into contact with contemporary agency–structure frameworks, would allow Plekhanov to be read as a qualified mediation-theorist — one who built layered, non-reductive causal mechanisms while leaving the place of individual agency within them incompletely resolved — rather than as the crude determinist of received caricature.
