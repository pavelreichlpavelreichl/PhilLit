## Section 1: Marx's Critique of Hegel and the Young Hegelian Mediation — The Textual Record

Before any secondary theoretical school intervenes, Marx's own texts already present a divided record of what "inverting" Hegel accomplishes. This section traces that record from the 1843 manuscript on Hegel's *Philosophy of Right*, through the Feuerbach- and Young-Hegelian-mediated polemics of 1844–46, to the *Grundrisse* and *Capital*'s 1873 afterword, showing that close textual scholars disagree not only about how to evaluate Marx's critique but about what, exegetically, it consists in.

### Subsection 1.1: The 1843 Critique and the Inversion Formula

Marx's 1843 commentary on Hegel's account of the state (§§261–313 of the *Philosophy of Right*) charges Hegel with converting the real, empirical subject — civil society, the family, the state — into a mere predicate of an abstract logical Subject, the Idea; Fine's survey of post-1960s revisionist scholarship (2001) glosses this as a charge of "logical, pantheistic mysticism," and Mikkelsen's (1981) paragraph-by-paragraph analysis finds the subject-predicate inversion charge sustained with reasonable consistency across the manuscript. Fine's later essay (2009), however, resists reading the 1843 text as a completed refutation: Marx wants simultaneously to retain Hegel's account of ethical life as a normative standard and to reject Hegel's speculative method of deriving it, leaving the critique internally unresolved — neither a clean method-transfer nor a simple content-substitution, but an unfinished combination of both.

Barbour (2023) supplies underappreciated context: Marx's engagement with Adolf Trendelenburg's 1840 *Logische Untersuchungen*, a prior influential attack on Hegelian dialectical logic, meaning the inversion critique drew on an existing controversy over Hegelian logic's validity rather than arising purely from Marx's own trajectory. Flohr (2023) revises the standard periodization of "alienation," typically dated to 1844, by relocating its origin to the 1843 Critique, where Marx already treats the modern sovereign state as an alienation of civil society rooted in private property. MacGregor (1992/2019) complicates the resulting narrative from the other direction: the mature, *Capital*-era Marx partially reclaims Hegelian arguments about the state, class, and factory legislation that the 1843 manuscript rejected, given the transformed historical role of the Victorian state — a later partial return, not a one-way trajectory of increasing distance.

The 1873 afterword's formula — Hegel's dialectic "standing on its head," containing a "rational kernel within the mystical shell" — is directly interrogated by Horn (2013) and Caligaris and Starosta (2014). Horn argues Marx's own formulations of what is extracted and what discarded are not fully consistent across texts, producing a genuine interpretive "enigma" rather than a self-explanatory slogan. Caligaris and Starosta stake an intermediate position between strong "homology thesis" readings that map *Capital*'s categories one-to-one onto Hegel's *Logic* and readings that treat the Hegelian debt as merely rhetorical: Marx appropriates specific logical forms — the movement from abstract to concrete — while transforming their content and function.

### Subsection 1.2: Feuerbach and the Young Hegelian Milieu

Feuerbach's "transformative method" — restoring predicates illegitimately hypostatized as an independent subject (Spirit, God) to their true subject, sensuous humanity — supplies the formal technique Marx redirects from theology toward the state and political economy. But the relation is one of debt and break together: Brudney (1998) argues that in the "Theses on Feuerbach" Marx aims not merely to correct Feuerbach's materialism but to leave the entire genre of philosophical demonstration Hegel and Feuerbach both still practiced, retaining the demand that thought be practically efficacious while rejecting Feuerbach's residual contemplative stance. Fromm and Almeida (2015) sharpen this epistemologically: *The German Ideology*'s appeal to "real, positive science" is neither naive empiricism nor a return to speculation but a distinct reconception of scientific knowledge, developed from an outlook still indebted to Hegelian resources even while opposing Hegelian idealism.

Bauer's relationship to Marx is increasingly read as genuine intellectual partnership prior to polemical dissolution. Kangal (2020), using Marx's unpublished "Bonn Notebooks," reconstructs the 1839–42 collaboration as ambivalent and mutually formative, showing that the purely oppositional image drawn from *The Holy Family* presupposes years of shared work. Barbour (2021) reframes the eventual break as political-theological rather than a graduation from abstract philosophy to concrete materialism: the decisive shift was from a coded political theology of institutional struggle to a politics of mass democratic mobilization.

Stirner's egoism receives comparable revisionist treatment. Thomas (1975) established that the *Sankt Max* section attacking Stirner — roughly three-quarters of *The German Ideology*'s original text, though long omitted from abridged translations — is integral, not peripheral, to the work's argument, forcing Marx to specify what conception of individuality and freedom historical materialism could coherently endorse. Whyman (2022) extends this: the disproportionate length reflects genuine intellectual affinity, since Marx and Engels credit Stirner with real advances on the critique of reason and on egoistic self-interest, holding only that he gets their relation "the wrong way around" — a diagnosis that compels Marx and Engels to clarify communism by "turning Stirner on his head." Browning (1999) connects the polemic to the emerging materialist theory of history, since refuting Stirner's ahistorical egoism required specifying a materialist account of development still in dialogue with Hegel's philosophy of history. Breckman (1999) frames Feuerbach, Bauer, and Stirner together within a longer German debate over the "personalist" conception of God, whose critiques of religious and political personality supplied the categories through which Marx articulated his critique of civil society and the state; Leopold (2007) offers a comparably integrated treatment of all three as simultaneous resources and obstacles for the young Marx's account of alienation and flourishing.

### Subsection 1.3: From the 1844 Manuscripts to the *Grundrisse* and *Capital*'s Afterword

Whether Marx's mature economic writings sustain or abandon dialectical derivation from Hegel's *Logic* divides close readers of the *Grundrisse* and *Capital* directly. Reichelt (2007), writing within the West German *Neue Marx-Lektüre*, defends a strong continuity thesis: *Capital*'s presentation of value, money, and capital is not merely expository but a genuine logical derivation modeled on Hegel's *Science of Logic*, developed via Hegel, Simmel, and Adorno on the concept of validity. Rosenthal (1999) argues the opposite from the same terrain. Against readings following Roman Rosdolsky that treat the *Grundrisse* as evidence of deep continuing dependence on Hegelian logic, Rosenthal contends the "Notebook on Method" dramatizes Marx's practical *escape*: his "dialectical derivations" of economic categories repeatedly reach theoretical dead ends, and real progress comes only when he abandons them for "transcendental" reasoning from given market phenomena — prices, profit — back to their conditions of possibility. On this account, Hegelian formulae persist in *Capital*'s opening chapter owing to the ontological peculiarity of money as object of inquiry, not a sustained methodological choice.

The same fault line runs through the reception of the 1857 "Introduction." Postone and Reinicke (1974) contest Martin Nicolaus's influential framing of that text's reflections on the abstract and the concrete, offering a different account of their relation to Hegelian categories. Avineri (1968) supplies the chronological and conceptual scaffolding against which these disputes can be situated, treating Marx's mature categories — civil society, alienation, the state — as transformed rather than discarded Hegelian categories, a continuity thesis at the level of intellectual biography that the Reichelt/Rosenthal dispute recapitulates at the level of method.

**Section Summary**: Marx's own texts do not yield a single, stable doctrine of "materialist inversion." The 1843 inversion charge is textually coherent (Mikkelsen) yet internally unresolved (Fine); the Feuerbach and Young Hegelian polemics are constitutive rather than merely destructive of Marx's positive theory (Whyman, Thomas, Kangal); and scholars agreed that *Capital* retains Hegelian vocabulary divide sharply over whether this reflects genuine categorial necessity (Reichelt) or its practical abandonment (Rosenthal). This instability at the primary-text level is the review's organizing premise.
