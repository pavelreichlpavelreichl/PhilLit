## Section 4: The Dialectical Method in *Capital* — Systematic Dialectics vs. Analytical Marxism

Section 1 showed that even close readers of the 1843 *Critique* and the *Grundrisse* disagree about whether Marx transfers a Hegelian method into his mature work or merely retains scattered Hegelian vocabulary. This disagreement resurfaces, in sharper and more technical form, around *Capital* itself. Two broad camps dispute whether the commodity-money-capital exposition of *Capital*'s opening chapters is dialectically necessitated in a sense continuous with Hegel's *Science of Logic*, or whether "dialectic" here names nothing more than a loosely metaphorical, expository device — or worse, a source of systematic obscurity. What distinguishes this debate from Section 1's textual disputes is that the disagreement is not confined to a two-sided contest between dialectically sympathetic and dialectically hostile readers: dialectically inclined Marxists dispute the isomorphism thesis among themselves, just as analytical Marxists have offered partial as well as wholesale rejections of dialectical reasoning.

### Subsection 4.1: The Systematic-Dialectics/Value-Form Program

Arthur's (2004) "New Dialectic" supplies the most sustained statement of the isomorphism thesis: *Capital*'s movement from the commodity through the value-form to money and capital is not a historical narrative but a systematic exposition whose logical structure parallels categories from Hegel's logic of essence, even though Marx's subject matter — the capital-relation — is historically specific rather than the Idea itself. Reuten (2000) supplies the methodological vocabulary that much of this literature presupposes, distinguishing "systematic dialectics" (the reconstruction of a social system's necessary conceptual order) from "historical dialectics" (the reconstruction of that system's actual genesis), and arguing that value-form theory's turn toward the former does not abandon historical materialism but specifies its proper relation to systematic theory. Murray (2009) extends this program by tracing how Marx's derivation of value, money, and capital draws on Hegelian categories of "immediate" and "reflected" determination to explain how value acquires an independent, objective form — money — that comes to dominate its own social substance. Reichelt (2007), writing from within the German Neue Marx-Lektüre, offers a related but distinct derivation of the money-form, arguing that *Capital*'s development of abstract labor and value contains unresolved tensions concerning the "validity" (Geltung) of value as a category, and proposing a reconstruction that draws on Hegel, Simmel, and Adorno to explain value and money as totality and abstraction. Keikhaee (2021) addresses the question of whether this systematic method is continuous across Marx's career, arguing against readings that treat the 1857 "Introduction" as offering a transhistorical abstract-to-concrete method later abandoned in favor of the commodity-first exposition of *Capital*; on his account, a critical-historicist approach to economic categories forms the stable core of Marx's dialectical method from the *Grundrisse* through *Capital*, and the post-1857 shift to the commodity as starting point does not mark a methodological rupture.

### Subsection 4.2: Internal Marxist Critiques of the Isomorphism Thesis

The isomorphism thesis is contested from within the dialectical camp itself. Rosenthal (1999), read here alongside his treatment of the *Grundrisse* in Section 1, argues that systematic-dialectics readers — including Arthur — are compelled, on close inspection, to relax the strict categorial necessity they claim to find in Hegel's *Logic*, effectively "escaping from Hegel" while retaining Hegelian vocabulary; on his account, the opening chapters of *Capital* are better explained by non-dialectical, "transcendental" argument from given market phenomena than by any dialectical derivation. Carchedi (2009) mounts a distinct, politically economic critique of the same program, arguing that Arthur's New Dialectic mistakes a purely logical reordering of categories for the historically and materially grounded dialectic Marx actually employs, and that in severing systematic dialectics from historical-materialist content, the New Dialectic empties value, money, and capital of their determinate economic content, reducing them to formal logical relations. Rosenthal's and Carchedi's objections differ in emphasis — Rosenthal targets the derivational necessity claimed for the exposition, Carchedi targets its economic content — but both conclude that the systematic-dialectics program's Hegelian debt is looser, or more damaging, than its proponents allow.

### Subsection 4.3: The Analytical-Marxist Rejection and Its Qualified Defenses

A parallel but distinct line of criticism comes from analytical Marxism, whose founding statements are G. A. Cohen's *Karl Marx's Theory of History* and Jon Elster's *Making Sense of Marx*. Both texts lack retrievable abstracts in the present bibliography and are accordingly excluded from direct citation here; likewise Sean Sayers's direct reply to Cohen, *Marxism and the Dialectical Method*, cannot be cited directly. This is a significant limitation for a section whose subject is precisely the analytical/dialectical divide, and Cohen's functionalist position is represented below only indirectly, through his own later reply to Elster (cohen2013reply), cross-referenced from Section 5's discussion of functional explanation. Elster's (2011) mature statement of the charge — distinguishing "hard obscurantism" (deliberately opaque prose masking empty content) from "soft obscurantism" (loose, unfalsifiable use of concepts like contradiction and totality that trade on equivocation between literal and metaphorical senses) — sharpens rather than softens the original indictment, and Elster explicitly characterizes analytical Marxism's own trajectory as having revealed "non-bullshit Marxism" to be nearly an empty set, suggesting the rejection of dialectic did not, on his own retrospective account, leave a stable alternative theory intact. Not every analytical Marxist reply to dialectic is wholly dismissive, however. Van Parijs (1982), writing from within the analytical-Marxist September Group, argues that certain dialectical claims about capitalism's self-undermining "contradictions" can be reconstructed, without dialectical vocabulary, as instances of perverse or counter-finalized collective effects structurally similar to prisoner's-dilemma-type unintended aggregate outcomes — a partial, qualified vindication of dialectical description using non-dialectical, game-theoretic tools that complicates any strict two-camp narrative of the debate. Kirkpatrick (1994) offers a metatheoretical diagnosis of why the two camps largely talk past one another: tracing Cohen's and Elster's methodological commitments to a broader analytic-philosophical background, he argues that Analytical Marxism's clarificatory apparatus is itself corrosive to the practical-active meaning central to Marx's own thought, so that the project of rendering Marx's theory "clear" through non-dialectical, individualist methods risks dissolving rather than defending it.

Taken together, this section's material shows that the question of Hegel's survival in *Capital* does not reduce to a single fault line between "dialectical" and "analytical" readers. Systematic-dialectics theorists divide over how much categorial necessity *Capital*'s exposition actually sustains (Arthur, Reuten, Murray, Reichelt, and Keikhaee against Rosenthal and Carchedi), while analytical Marxists divide over whether dialectical claims should be rejected outright, translated into non-dialectical terms, or diagnosed as a symptom of a deeper methodological mismatch (Elster against Van Parijs and Kirkpatrick). No position surveyed here commands unqualified assent even within its own camp — a pattern that recurs, as Section 5 shows, in the closely related dispute over whether Cohen's functional explanation of historical materialism reintroduces a covert, Hegelian-style teleology.
