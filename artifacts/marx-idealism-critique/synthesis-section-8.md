## Conclusion

This review has traced Marx's critique of Hegelian idealism from the 1843 *Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right* through the 1873 afterword to *Capital*, and across the secondary debates that critique has generated — continuity versus rupture, Western Marxism and its anti-humanist critics, the dialectical status of *Capital*'s method, historical materialism's relation to teleology, and the most recent MEGA2-informed scholarship. At every level examined, the meaning of Marx's "materialist inversion" of Hegel has proven to be an object of dispute rather than a settled conclusion. The 1873 formula itself — standing Hegel's dialectic "on its head" to extract a "rational kernel" from its "mystical shell" — supplied this review's anchoring image, but Horn's (2013) close reading shows that Marx's own formulations of what counts as kernel and what as shell are not fully consistent across texts, so the formula names an interpretive problem rather than resolving one. Whether "inversion" designates a change of philosophical content, a transfer of formal method, a restructuring of totality's ground, or some combination of these remains open, and no position surveyed here commands consensus.

A second finding concerns the trajectory of the debate itself: positions one generation treats as settled are regularly reopened by the next, and philological advances tend to multiply rather than close off interpretive options. Confident earlier readings of the *Grundrisse*'s continuity with Hegel's *Logic* have been contested by later close readers who argue the "Notebook on Method" instead dramatizes a practical escape from dialectical derivation — a reversal internal to the systematic-dialectics literature, not merely an external objection to it. The break/continuity question shows the same dynamic: Cowling (2023) re-litigates claims about Marx's alienation theory that earlier participants had treated as adjudicated, defending a qualified discontinuity thesis against renewed continuity arguments. Musto's (2020) framing of a post-2010 "Marx revival," grounded in MEGA2 philology rather than inherited twentieth-century orthodoxies, exemplifies this pattern at the level of the field as a whole: archival access has not converged scholarship on a revised consensus but has opened new sites — philological, value-theoretic, logical, ecological, global — at which the Marx-Hegel relationship is renegotiated.

Three cross-cutting questions remain unresolved. First, whether "method" can be coherently separated from "content" in assessing what Marx retains from Hegel — the question dividing systematic-dialectics readers who locate Hegel's survival at the level of categorial presentation from critics who argue this separation empties Marx's categories of determinate content. Boveiri's (2024) argument for structural continuity between Hegelian and Marxian totality, mediated by inversion of metaphysical ground rather than outright rejection of Hegel's holism, suggests one route through this impasse without settling the narrower value-form disputes it touches. Second, whether functional or teleological-sounding explanation is eliminable from historical materialism without loss of explanatory power — sharpened rather than resolved across four decades of dispute over functionalist reconstructions of Marx and their critics. Third, whether the German New Reading's value-form emphasis is compatible with, or supersedes, Anglophone systematic dialectics — a divide this review has traced without finding grounds, internal to the literature, for treating either as having absorbed the other.

The present project takes up this landscape not by adjudicating among its positions but by treating the persistence of contestation itself as the phenomenon requiring explanation: why a formula as compact as "standing on its head" has sustained over a century and a half of divergent, still-active reconstruction, and what this durability suggests about the relation between Marx's critique of Hegel and the critique of idealist philosophy of history more broadly.
