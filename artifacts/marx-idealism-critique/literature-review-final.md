## Introduction

Marx's relationship to Hegel is conventionally summarized by a single image: dialectic "standing on its head," to be "turned right side up again" so as to discover "the rational kernel within the mystical shell," as Marx put it in the 1873 afterword to the second German edition of *Capital*. This review pursues two connected aims. The first is to reconstruct Marx's own critique of Hegelian idealism and of the idealist philosophy of history across his career, from the 1843 *Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right* through the 1873 afterword's retrospective formula. The second is to survey the extensive secondary literature this critique has generated — debates over continuity and rupture in Marx's development, the status of dialectical method in *Capital*, the relation of Hegelian and Western Marxism to anti-humanist readings, the coherence of historical materialism as an alternative to idealist philosophy of history, and recent New Reading and MEGA2-based scholarship.

The 1873 formula serves as this review's anchor image, but not as a settled point of departure. Horn (2013) treats the "rational kernel"/"mystical shell" metaphor itself as a textual problem: Marx's scattered formulations of what is to be extracted from Hegel's dialectic, and what discarded, are not fully consistent with one another, so that the metaphor names an interpretive task rather than announcing its own solution. This review's organizing premise follows from Horn's diagnosis — the "materialist inversion" is not a fixed doctrine that scholarship merely applies to particular texts, but is itself the object of sustained and, at every level, unresolved dispute. Fine (2001) frames the resulting field in terms of a historiographical division between an older narrative of a clean, total break between Hegel and Marx and a body of "revisionist" scholarship that instead finds *Capital* retaining substantive methodological debts to Hegelian dialectic. Where a text like Fine's positions itself along this axis structures how later, more specialized interventions in the debate should be read.

This contestation is not confined to method. Avineri (1968) supplies an early and influential account of Marx's mature categories — civil society, alienation, the state — as transformed rather than simply rejected Hegelian categories, establishing a chronological and conceptual scaffolding against which later, more skeptical readings can be measured. Boveiri (2024) extends this question of inheritance to the level of totality, arguing that Marx retains a structurally holistic conception of society and history from Hegel's *Science of Logic* even while relocating its ground from Spirit's self-determination to the material relations and contradictions of social life. Musto (2020), reassessing Marx's key concepts in light of the MEGA2 edition and previously neglected manuscripts, shows that even the most recent philological turn in Marx scholarship multiplies rather than resolves these questions of inheritance and inversion.

The review proceeds from this premise through six stages: the primary-text development of Marx's critique of Hegel and the Young Hegelian mediation; the continuity/rupture debate inaugurated by Althusser; Hegelian and Western Marxism's alienation-centered readings against anti-humanist responses; the status of dialectical method in *Capital*, including the analytical-Marxist rejection of dialectic; historical materialism's contested relation to idealist philosophy of history; and recent New Reading and MEGA2 scholarship. At each stage, the throughline is the same: what counts as the "rational kernel" of Hegel's philosophy, and what counts as its discardable "mystical shell," remains a live question rather than a settled inheritance.

## Section 1: Marx's Critique of Hegel and the Young Hegelian Mediation: The Textual Record

Before any secondary theoretical school intervenes, Marx's own texts already present a divided record of what "inverting" Hegel accomplishes. This section traces that record from the 1843 manuscript on Hegel's *Philosophy of Right*, through the Feuerbach- and Young-Hegelian-mediated polemics of 1844–46, to the *Grundrisse* and *Capital*'s 1873 afterword, showing that close textual scholars disagree not only about how to evaluate Marx's critique but about what, exegetically, it consists in.

### 1.1 The 1843 Critique and the Inversion Formula

Marx's 1843 commentary on Hegel's account of the state (§§261–313 of the *Philosophy of Right*) charges Hegel with converting the real, empirical subject — civil society, the family, the state — into a mere predicate of an abstract logical Subject, the Idea; Fine's survey of post-1960s revisionist scholarship (2001) glosses this as a charge of "logical, pantheistic mysticism," and Mikkelsen's (1981) paragraph-by-paragraph analysis finds the subject-predicate inversion charge sustained with reasonable consistency across the manuscript. Fine's later essay (2009), however, resists reading the 1843 text as a completed refutation: Marx wants simultaneously to retain Hegel's account of ethical life as a normative standard and to reject Hegel's speculative method of deriving it, leaving the critique internally unresolved — neither a clean method-transfer nor a simple content-substitution, but an unfinished combination of both.

Barbour (2023) supplies underappreciated context: Marx's engagement with Adolf Trendelenburg's 1840 *Logische Untersuchungen*, a prior influential attack on Hegelian dialectical logic, meaning the inversion critique drew on an existing controversy over Hegelian logic's validity rather than arising purely from Marx's own trajectory. Flohr (2023) revises the standard periodization of "alienation," typically dated to 1844, by relocating its origin to the 1843 Critique, where Marx already treats the modern sovereign state as an alienation of civil society rooted in private property. MacGregor (1992/2019) complicates the resulting narrative from the other direction: the mature, *Capital*-era Marx partially reclaims Hegelian arguments about the state, class, and factory legislation that the 1843 manuscript rejected, given the transformed historical role of the Victorian state — a later partial return, not a one-way trajectory of increasing distance.

The 1873 afterword's formula — Hegel's dialectic "standing on its head," containing a "rational kernel within the mystical shell" — is directly interrogated by Horn (2013) and Caligaris and Starosta (2014). Horn argues Marx's own formulations of what is extracted and what discarded are not fully consistent across texts, producing a genuine interpretive "enigma" rather than a self-explanatory slogan. Caligaris and Starosta stake an intermediate position between strong "homology thesis" readings that map *Capital*'s categories one-to-one onto Hegel's *Logic* and readings that treat the Hegelian debt as merely rhetorical: Marx appropriates specific logical forms — the movement from abstract to concrete — while transforming their content and function.

### 1.2 Feuerbach and the Young Hegelian Milieu

Feuerbach's "transformative method" — restoring predicates illegitimately hypostatized as an independent subject (Spirit, God) to their true subject, sensuous humanity — supplies the formal technique Marx redirects from theology toward the state and political economy. But the relation is one of debt and break together: Brudney (1998) argues that in the "Theses on Feuerbach" Marx aims not merely to correct Feuerbach's materialism but to leave the entire genre of philosophical demonstration Hegel and Feuerbach both still practiced, retaining the demand that thought be practically efficacious while rejecting Feuerbach's residual contemplative stance. Fromm and Almeida (2015) sharpen this epistemologically: *The German Ideology*'s appeal to "real, positive science" is neither naive empiricism nor a return to speculation but a distinct reconception of scientific knowledge, developed from an outlook still indebted to Hegelian resources even while opposing Hegelian idealism.

Bauer's relationship to Marx is increasingly read as genuine intellectual partnership prior to polemical dissolution. Kangal (2020), using Marx's unpublished "Bonn Notebooks," reconstructs the 1839–42 collaboration as ambivalent and mutually formative, showing that the purely oppositional image drawn from *The Holy Family* presupposes years of shared work. Barbour (2021) reframes the eventual break as political-theological rather than a graduation from abstract philosophy to concrete materialism: the decisive shift was from a coded political theology of institutional struggle to a politics of mass democratic mobilization.

Stirner's egoism receives comparable revisionist treatment. Thomas (1975) established that the *Sankt Max* section attacking Stirner — roughly three-quarters of *The German Ideology*'s original text, though long omitted from abridged translations — is integral, not peripheral, to the work's argument, forcing Marx to specify what conception of individuality and freedom historical materialism could coherently endorse. Whyman (2022) extends this: the disproportionate length reflects genuine intellectual affinity, since Marx and Engels credit Stirner with real advances on the critique of reason and on egoistic self-interest, holding only that he gets their relation "the wrong way around" — a diagnosis that compels Marx and Engels to clarify communism by "turning Stirner on his head." Browning (1999) connects the polemic to the emerging materialist theory of history, since refuting Stirner's ahistorical egoism required specifying a materialist account of development still in dialogue with Hegel's philosophy of history. Breckman (1999) frames Feuerbach, Bauer, and Stirner together within a longer German debate over the "personalist" conception of God, whose critiques of religious and political personality supplied the categories through which Marx articulated his critique of civil society and the state; Leopold (2007) offers a comparably integrated treatment of all three as simultaneous resources and obstacles for the young Marx's account of alienation and flourishing.

### 1.3 From the 1844 Manuscripts to the *Grundrisse* and *Capital*'s Afterword

Whether Marx's mature economic writings sustain or abandon dialectical derivation from Hegel's *Logic* divides close readers of the *Grundrisse* and *Capital* directly. Reichelt (2007), writing within the West German *Neue Marx-Lektüre*, defends a strong continuity thesis: *Capital*'s presentation of value, money, and capital is not merely expository but a genuine logical derivation modeled on Hegel's *Science of Logic*, developed via Hegel, Simmel, and Adorno on the concept of validity. Rosenthal (1999) argues the opposite from the same terrain. Against readings following Roman Rosdolsky that treat the *Grundrisse* as evidence of deep continuing dependence on Hegelian logic, Rosenthal contends the "Notebook on Method" dramatizes Marx's practical *escape*: his "dialectical derivations" of economic categories repeatedly reach theoretical dead ends, and real progress comes only when he abandons them for "transcendental" reasoning from given market phenomena — prices, profit — back to their conditions of possibility. On this account, Hegelian formulae persist in *Capital*'s opening chapter owing to the ontological peculiarity of money as object of inquiry, not a sustained methodological choice.

The same fault line runs through the reception of the 1857 "Introduction." Postone and Reinicke (1974) contest Martin Nicolaus's influential framing of that text's reflections on the abstract and the concrete, offering a different account of their relation to Hegelian categories. Avineri (1968) supplies the chronological and conceptual scaffolding against which these disputes can be situated, treating Marx's mature categories — civil society, alienation, the state — as transformed rather than discarded Hegelian categories, a continuity thesis at the level of intellectual biography that the Reichelt/Rosenthal dispute recapitulates at the level of method.

**Section Summary**: Marx's own texts do not yield a single, stable doctrine of "materialist inversion." The 1843 inversion charge is textually coherent (Mikkelsen) yet internally unresolved (Fine); the Feuerbach and Young Hegelian polemics are constitutive rather than merely destructive of Marx's positive theory (Whyman, Thomas, Kangal); and scholars agreed that *Capital* retains Hegelian vocabulary divide sharply over whether this reflects genuine categorial necessity (Reichelt) or its practical abandonment (Rosenthal). This instability at the primary-text level is the review's organizing premise.

## Section 2: Continuity or Rupture? The Althusser Debate

Beyond disputes over what Marx's texts themselves say about Hegel, a second-order controversy asks how Marx's own intellectual development should be periodized: does the transition from the 1844 *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts* to the mature critique of political economy constitute a decisive rupture with Marx's own earlier, Hegelian-humanist thought, or an underlying continuity beneath changing vocabulary? Louis Althusser's thesis of an "epistemological break" occurring around 1845 reframes this question as internal to Marx's biography rather than external to it: on this view, the young Marx of the manuscripts is himself still enclosed within an "ideological" problematic derived from Feuerbach and Hegel, and the mature Marx of *Capital* achieves a scientific rupture with that problematic rather than a mere refinement of it.

A significant evidentiary limitation must be flagged at the outset of this section. Althusser's own founding statements of the break thesis—*For Marx* and, with Étienne Balibar, *Reading Capital*—lack retrievable abstracts in the current bibliography and are excluded from direct citation per project convention, as are the principal continuity-side rejoinders: E. P. Thompson's *The Poverty of Theory*, Norman Geras's *Marx and Human Nature*, István Mészáros's *Marx's Theory of Alienation*, and Bertell Ollman's 1971 *Alienation*. These are not minor omissions; they are the debate's namesake texts on both sides. The present section therefore characterizes the break thesis and its reception primarily through three works that discuss and evaluate that debate rather than through the primary combatants themselves. Readers seeking the substantive continuity-side case should consult Section 3's discussion of alienation, where Ollman's 1977 restatement of his relational-ontology argument and Dunayevskaya's Marxist-Humanist account of alienation as a permanent, Hegel-derived category are cited directly, since both carry retrievable abstracts.

Within the citable literature, Balibar (1978)—himself Althusser's collaborator on *Reading Capital*—traces the "epistemological break" concept's genealogy from Bachelard's philosophy of science through Canguilhem before its appropriation for reading Marx, scrutinizing how the concept's meaning shifted as it migrated from the history of the natural sciences into historical materialism. This internal genealogical audit suggests that the break concept's applicability to Marx was, from within Althusser's own circle, treated as requiring justification rather than as self-evidently transferable. Musto (2015) approaches the question philologically rather than conceptually, examining the international reception history of the 1844 Manuscripts and arguing that both continuity readings (which treat the manuscripts as a finished statement of Marx's whole philosophy) and Althusserian discontinuity readings (which treat them as superseded ideology) rest on an indefensible treatment of what was, in fact, an unfinished, exploratory text. On Musto's account, "the young Marx" invoked by both sides of the debate is substantially a retrospective construction rather than a textually grounded unity. Cowling (2023) most recently re-weighs the accumulated arguments for and against continuity, explicitly revisiting Althusser's position alongside Meszaros's and Geras's, and defends a qualified discontinuity thesis: that alienation theory, while recurring occasionally in vocabulary after 1847, does not perform load-bearing theoretical work in Marx's mature political economy.

Taken together, these three sources indicate that the break/continuity question has not converged toward resolution; if anything, Musto's and Cowling's more recent interventions suggest the debate increasingly turns on contested premises about what kind of textual unity "the young Marx" ever possessed, rather than on a settled reading of either side's primary texts.

## Section 3: Hegelian/Western Marxism, Alienation, and the Anti-Humanist Response

If the Althusserian break thesis treats Marx's Hegelian inheritance as ideology to be excised, the twentieth-century tradition running from Lukács through Korsch to the Frankfurt School reverses the valence entirely: on this reading, Marxism's task is not to purge Hegel but to complete him, retaining dialectical method and, for some figures in this tradition, the category of alienation as Hegelian-derived resources rather than pre-scientific residue. This section surveys that tradition's major statements and the alienation/humanism debate it generates, before noting how thinly the opposing anti-humanist pole is represented in the citable literature available here.

### 3.1 Lukács and Reification

Georg Lukács's reconstruction of reification as the central category of a Hegelian Marxism is often read as a straightforward "return to Hegel" against Second and Third International orthodoxy. Rockmore (1992) complicates this narrative by tracing Lukács's dialectic to Neo-Kantian and Fichtean sources — particularly Emil Lask's mediation of the thing-in-itself problem — arguing that Lukács's *History and Class Consciousness* solves a specifically Kantian epistemological difficulty via an appropriation of Fichte as much as via direct engagement with Hegel. On Rockmore's account, calling Lukács's Marxism simply "Hegelian" obscures this heterogeneous philosophical genealogy. The substantive content of Lukács's reification concept has itself become a site of dispute in its later reception: Chari (2010) defends the political-economic core of Lukács's category — its grounding in the commodity form and revolutionary class praxis — against Axel Honneth's recognition-theoretic reinterpretation of reification as a "forgetting" of prior intersubjective recognition, arguing that Honneth's move sacrifices the critical purchase on capitalism that made Lukács's original formulation effective. Dahbour (2017) situates this dispute within a longer genealogy, tracing how Hegelian totality and reason pass from Lukács through subsequent critical theory down to Honneth, and framing the Chari-Honneth disagreement as one instance of a recurring question about how much Hegelian inheritance critical theory can shed while remaining dialectical rather than merely normative.

### 3.2 Korsch and the Anti-Positivist Thesis

Karl Korsch's *Marxism and Philosophy* (1923) supplies a second founding statement of Western Marxism, arguing that Marxist theory must remain "philosophy" — a moment internal to revolutionary practice — rather than becoming a set of empirically verifiable propositions on the model of positive science. Negt (1975) reconstructs this thesis in terms of the "constitution" of Marxist theory out of concrete class struggle, arguing that Korsch's insistence on theory-practice unity is what commits Marxism to a dialectically self-reflective character irreducible to positivist or mechanistic readings. Browner (1977) situates this anti-positivist stance historically, showing how Korsch's argument that Marxism must remain philosophy was received as a scandal by party orthodoxy in the aftermath of 1917, and how its historicizing conception of Marxist theory anticipated central themes of subsequent Western Marxism. Devlin (2021), however, challenges this standard genealogy directly: rereading *Marxism and Philosophy* as an intervention in interwar debates over Marxist orthodoxy rather than as Marx exegesis proper, Devlin argues Korsch's work presupposed revolutionary possibility rather than the defeatist cultural retreat later associated with "Western Marxism," and that the retrospective grouping of Korsch with Lukács and the Frankfurt School under that label obscures rather than clarifies his actual argument.

### 3.3 The Frankfurt School's Divergent Hegelianisms

The Frankfurt School's relation to Hegel is not uniform across its members. Finlayson (2017) offers a differentiated account of Horkheimer, Adorno, and Marcuse: the young Horkheimer endorses only a qualified Hegelianism, treating Hegel's idealist metaphysics as superseded by sociology, psychology, and materialist historiography; Adorno remains the most committed Hegelian of the three, understanding his own dialectical method as an immanent critique continuous with Hegel's; and Marcuse offers the most charitable reading, emphasizing the critical rather than systematic moment in Hegel's conception of reason. This last claim is the specific subject of Anderson's (1993) reassessment of Marcuse's *Reason and Revolution* fifty years after its publication: Anderson argues that Marcuse's central thesis — that Hegel's dialectic of negativity anticipates and is completed by Marx's critical social theory — remains a defensible corrective to positivist social theory even where particular readings of Hegel's texts have since been revised. Taken together, Finlayson's and Anderson's accounts show that "the Frankfurt School's Hegelianism" names not a single position but a spectrum, with Adorno and Marcuse retaining more of Hegel's dialectic than Horkheimer.

### 3.4 The Alienation/Humanism Debate

The question of whether alienation is a Hegelian category Marx retains and deepens throughout his corpus, or an immature humanist residue superseded by the mature critique of political economy, organizes a distinct but related debate. Dunayevskaya (2018) states the Marxist-Humanist position directly: alienation is inseparable from Marx's debt to the Hegelian dialectic of negativity — the "negation of the negation" — and attacks on the young Marx's Hegelian-inflected humanism are, on her account, simultaneously attacks on the revolutionary core of Hegel's dialectic that Marx inherited and preserved. Ollman (1977) develops a systematic version of the continuity thesis grounded not in Dunayevskaya's dialectic of negativity but in Marx's "philosophy of internal relations," a Hegelian-derived relational ontology: alienation names the rupture of an internally related unity between the worker and his productive activity, product, species-being, and fellow workers, a unity Ollman argues is restored rather than abandoned as the organizing logic of Marx's mature economic categories. Postone (1993) rejects both the Lukácsian and the straightforwardly humanist framings of this inheritance while still affirming a Hegelian debt: he reinterprets Marx's mature critique as fundamentally Hegelian but non-humanist and non-class-subject-centered, targeting the abstract, quasi-automatic domination exercised by value and abstract labor time. On Postone's reading, both "traditional Marxism," which affirms labor as trans-historically emancipatory, and Lukács's positing of the proletariat as the Hegelian identical subject-object of history, misread this structure by locating emancipation in labor's self-realization rather than in the abolition of value-mediated domination itself. Arthur (2000) stakes out a further, distinct position: Hegel's survival in Marx belongs to the level of dialectical method, not humanist content, so that *Capital*'s categories of value-form and capital as automatic subject preserve a genuinely Hegelian dialectical logic even as Marx abandons Hegel's idealist content — a reading that, like Postone's, cuts across the polarity between the humanist-continuity and anti-Hegelian-scientistic camps.

No single position dominates this literature. Lukács's reification thesis is itself contested from within the tradition it founded (Chari against Honneth's revision), Korsch's place within "Western Marxism" is disputed by revisionist intellectual history (Devlin against the standard genealogy), the Frankfurt School's Hegelianism fractures along Finlayson's Horkheimer-Adorno-Marcuse spectrum, and the alienation/humanism debate yields at least four distinguishable positions — Dunayevskaya's dialectic-of-negativity humanism, Ollman's relational-ontology humanism, Postone's Hegelian but anti-humanist and anti-Lukácsian reconstruction, and Arthur's method-only Hegelianism. Postone's and Arthur's positions in particular indicate that the humanist/anti-humanist axis, as usually posed, is not exhaustive: both affirm a substantial Hegelian debt while explicitly rejecting the humanist-alienation reading that debt is often taken to require. A significant limitation of the present bibliography should be flagged here: the anti-humanist pole proper — Lucio Colletti's argument that Hegelian dialectical logic is incompatible with Marx's materialism, and Althusser's rejection of alienation as ideological — is thinly represented in citable material, since the founding texts on this side lack retrievable abstracts under current metadata. This gap is only partially mitigated by Section 2's engagement with Balibar's, Musto's, and Cowling's characterization of the Althusserian break thesis; a fuller reconstruction of the anti-humanist case against Hegelian Marxism would require targeted bibliographic remediation.

## Section 4: The Dialectical Method in *Capital*: Systematic Dialectics vs. Analytical Marxism

Section 1 showed that even close readers of the 1843 *Critique* and the *Grundrisse* disagree about whether Marx transfers a Hegelian method into his mature work or merely retains scattered Hegelian vocabulary. This disagreement resurfaces, in sharper and more technical form, around *Capital* itself. Two broad camps dispute whether the commodity-money-capital exposition of *Capital*'s opening chapters is dialectically necessitated in a sense continuous with Hegel's *Science of Logic*, or whether "dialectic" here names nothing more than a loosely metaphorical, expository device — or worse, a source of systematic obscurity. What distinguishes this debate from Section 1's textual disputes is that the disagreement is not confined to a two-sided contest between dialectically sympathetic and dialectically hostile readers: dialectically inclined Marxists dispute the isomorphism thesis among themselves, just as analytical Marxists have offered partial as well as wholesale rejections of dialectical reasoning.

### 4.1 The Systematic-Dialectics/Value-Form Program

Arthur's (2004) "New Dialectic" supplies the most sustained statement of the isomorphism thesis: *Capital*'s movement from the commodity through the value-form to money and capital is not a historical narrative but a systematic exposition whose logical structure parallels categories from Hegel's logic of essence, even though Marx's subject matter — the capital-relation — is historically specific rather than the Idea itself. Reuten (2000) supplies the methodological vocabulary that much of this literature presupposes, distinguishing "systematic dialectics" (the reconstruction of a social system's necessary conceptual order) from "historical dialectics" (the reconstruction of that system's actual genesis), and arguing that value-form theory's turn toward the former does not abandon historical materialism but specifies its proper relation to systematic theory. Murray (2009) extends this program by tracing how Marx's derivation of value, money, and capital draws on Hegelian categories of "immediate" and "reflected" determination to explain how value acquires an independent, objective form — money — that comes to dominate its own social substance. Reichelt (2007), writing from within the German Neue Marx-Lektüre, offers a related but distinct derivation of the money-form, arguing that *Capital*'s development of abstract labor and value contains unresolved tensions concerning the "validity" (Geltung) of value as a category, and proposing a reconstruction that draws on Hegel, Simmel, and Adorno to explain value and money as totality and abstraction. Keikhaee (2021) addresses the question of whether this systematic method is continuous across Marx's career, arguing against readings that treat the 1857 "Introduction" as offering a transhistorical abstract-to-concrete method later abandoned in favor of the commodity-first exposition of *Capital*; on his account, a critical-historicist approach to economic categories forms the stable core of Marx's dialectical method from the *Grundrisse* through *Capital*, and the post-1857 shift to the commodity as starting point does not mark a methodological rupture.

### 4.2 Internal Marxist Critiques of the Isomorphism Thesis

The isomorphism thesis is contested from within the dialectical camp itself. Rosenthal (1999), read here alongside his treatment of the *Grundrisse* in Section 1, argues that systematic-dialectics readers — including Arthur — are compelled, on close inspection, to relax the strict categorial necessity they claim to find in Hegel's *Logic*, effectively "escaping from Hegel" while retaining Hegelian vocabulary; on his account, the opening chapters of *Capital* are better explained by non-dialectical, "transcendental" argument from given market phenomena than by any dialectical derivation. Carchedi (2009) mounts a distinct, politically economic critique of the same program, arguing that Arthur's New Dialectic mistakes a purely logical reordering of categories for the historically and materially grounded dialectic Marx actually employs, and that in severing systematic dialectics from historical-materialist content, the New Dialectic empties value, money, and capital of their determinate economic content, reducing them to formal logical relations. Rosenthal's and Carchedi's objections differ in emphasis — Rosenthal targets the derivational necessity claimed for the exposition, Carchedi targets its economic content — but both conclude that the systematic-dialectics program's Hegelian debt is looser, or more damaging, than its proponents allow.

### 4.3 The Analytical-Marxist Rejection and Its Qualified Defenses

A parallel but distinct line of criticism comes from analytical Marxism, whose founding statements are G. A. Cohen's *Karl Marx's Theory of History* and Jon Elster's *Making Sense of Marx*. Both texts lack retrievable abstracts in the present bibliography and are accordingly excluded from direct citation here; likewise Sean Sayers's direct reply to Cohen, *Marxism and the Dialectical Method*, cannot be cited directly. This is a significant limitation for a section whose subject is precisely the analytical/dialectical divide, and Cohen's functionalist position is represented below only indirectly, through his own later reply to Elster (cohen2013reply), cross-referenced from Section 5's discussion of functional explanation. Elster's (2011) mature statement of the charge — distinguishing "hard obscurantism" (deliberately opaque prose masking empty content) from "soft obscurantism" (loose, unfalsifiable use of concepts like contradiction and totality that trade on equivocation between literal and metaphorical senses) — sharpens rather than softens the original indictment, and Elster explicitly characterizes analytical Marxism's own trajectory as having revealed "non-bullshit Marxism" to be nearly an empty set, suggesting the rejection of dialectic did not, on his own retrospective account, leave a stable alternative theory intact. Not every analytical Marxist reply to dialectic is wholly dismissive, however. Van Parijs (1982), writing from within the analytical-Marxist September Group, argues that certain dialectical claims about capitalism's self-undermining "contradictions" can be reconstructed, without dialectical vocabulary, as instances of perverse or counter-finalized collective effects structurally similar to prisoner's-dilemma-type unintended aggregate outcomes — a partial, qualified vindication of dialectical description using non-dialectical, game-theoretic tools that complicates any strict two-camp narrative of the debate. Kirkpatrick (1994) offers a metatheoretical diagnosis of why the two camps largely talk past one another: tracing Cohen's and Elster's methodological commitments to a broader analytic-philosophical background, he argues that Analytical Marxism's clarificatory apparatus is itself corrosive to the practical-active meaning central to Marx's own thought, so that the project of rendering Marx's theory "clear" through non-dialectical, individualist methods risks dissolving rather than defending it.

Taken together, this section's material shows that the question of Hegel's survival in *Capital* does not reduce to a single fault line between "dialectical" and "analytical" readers. Systematic-dialectics theorists divide over how much categorial necessity *Capital*'s exposition actually sustains (Arthur, Reuten, Murray, Reichelt, and Keikhaee against Rosenthal and Carchedi), while analytical Marxists divide over whether dialectical claims should be rejected outright, translated into non-dialectical terms, or diagnosed as a symptom of a deeper methodological mismatch (Elster against Van Parijs and Kirkpatrick). No position surveyed here commands unqualified assent even within its own camp — a pattern that recurs, as Section 5 shows, in the closely related dispute over whether Cohen's functional explanation of historical materialism reintroduces a covert, Hegelian-style teleology.

## Section 5: Historical Materialism vs. Idealist Philosophy of History: Teleology, Determinism, Structure/Agency

If Sections 1 through 4 examine whether Marx's *method* remains Hegelian, this section addresses the question posed at the level of *content*: what, precisely, does historical materialism reject and retain from Hegel's philosophy of history, and does it succeed in replacing providential teleology with a genuinely non-teleological account of historical change? The debate proceeds in layers. Hegel's own philosophy of history must first be specified with some precision, since looser characterizations of "teleology" make the subsequent charge against Marx either too easy or too vague to assess. Popper's historicism charge then treats Hegel and Marx as instances of a single logical structure, a claim contested on textual grounds. Cohen's functionalist reconstruction of historical materialism becomes the central flashpoint of the secondary literature, generating four decades of dispute over whether functional explanation of relations of production by productive-force development smuggles back a covert, quasi-Hegelian directionality. Finally, the base/superstructure architecture and the structure/agency question face difficulties that persist independently of, and beyond, the functionalism controversy.

### 5.1 Hegel's Teleological Philosophy of History

Hegel's lectures on the philosophy of world history present history as the rational, necessary self-realization of Spirit as freedom, unfolding through successive epochs in which an increasing portion of humanity comes to be recognized as free; individuals pursuing their own passions and private interests unwittingly serve this larger rational end, a mechanism Hegel calls the "cunning of reason" (Hegel and Forbes 1975). Parkinson (1989) asks how much of this structure survives in Marx, arguing that Marx's account of individuals acting from private, material motives who nonetheless bring about historically necessary transformations of the mode of production reproduces a version of the cunning of reason, even though Marx does not consciously borrow the doctrine from Hegel. This suggests, on Parkinson's reading, more continuity between the two theories of history than is sometimes supposed. Yang and Yeomans (2023) supply a more technical baseline against which such continuity claims can be evaluated, reconstructing Hegel's teleology from the *Science of Logic*'s account of self-determining, internally purposive development rather than as externally imposed design. On their reading, Hegelian teleology is a structure of reciprocal interaction in which the purpose functions as an immanent governing principle realized through durable means — a specification of "teleology" considerably more precise than the loose historicist sense later deployed by Popper, and one against which subsequent charges that historical materialism is "covertly teleological" can be assessed with greater rigor.

### 5.2 The Popperian Charge and Its Rebuttal

Popper's *Open Society and Its Enemies* treats Hegel's dialectical philosophy of history and Marx's materialist theory of history as sharing a common "historicist" logical structure: both, on Popper's reading, claim to have discovered inexorable laws governing history's overall direction, and both use this claim to underwrite totalitarian political conclusions; Marx's productive-forces determinism is presented as a materialist transposition of Hegel's Spirit rather than a genuine break with idealist historicism (Popper 2012). Suchting (1972) mounts a detailed rebuttal, arguing that Popper conflates several distinct claims — holism, essentialism, unconditional prediction, large-scale utopian social engineering — that Marx's actual theory does not jointly endorse. Distinguishing two possible senses of "law of historical development" and four models of historical determinism, Suchting argues that Marx's claims about tendencies in productive-force development are considerably more modest than the target Popper's arguments are designed to hit. The Popper-Suchting exchange thus establishes, at the outset of the functionalism debate proper, that the charge of Hegelian-style historicism is itself textually contested rather than self-evidently applicable to Marx.

### 5.3 Cohen's Functionalism and the Determinism/Teleology Debate

Cohen's functionalist reconstruction of historical materialism — defended, in the citable literature, through his direct reply to Elster — holds that a given set of production relations obtains because it is suited to developing the productive forces, on the model of functional explanation in evolutionary biology, and that such explanations can be legitimate even absent a fully specified elaborating mechanism, provided there is independent evidence of the functional correlation (Cohen 2013). This reconstruction becomes the central object of dispute. Elster (1990) argues that adequate explanation in Marxist theory must ultimately be grounded in the intentional actions of individuals, and that functionalist explanations of the kind Cohen offers are explanatorily inadequate unless supplemented by an account of the individual-level mechanisms generating the alleged functional correlation. Honderich (1982) presses the teleology charge most directly against Cohen specifically — as distinct from Popper's broader historicism charge against Hegel and Marx jointly — contending that attributing to productive forces a tendency or disposition to develop imports an unwarranted, quasi-purposive structure into what should be a straightforwardly causal materialist theory; Honderich further notes that Marx's own texts may offer no positive evidence, and arguably some evidence against, a teleological reading. Wood (1986) responds from within the functionalist camp, arguing that Elster's philosophical objections to functional explanation rest on a caricature of that mode of explanation as practiced in the social sciences, just as Elster's critique of Marx's use of teleology rests on a caricature of the kind of teleological claims Marx actually makes — directly rebutting Elster's methodological-individualist position. Dickman (1990) raises independent philosophy-of-science objections, arguing that the analogy to functional explanation in evolutionary biology is disanalogous in ways that undermine its legitimacy in the social case, and that even granting the analogy, such explanations cannot do the specific work historical materialism requires of them, namely explaining epochal transitions rather than mere social stability. Ruben (1981) narrows the scrutiny to Cohen's "primacy thesis" — that relations of production are explained by their suitability for developing the productive forces rather than the reverse — questioning whether Cohen's textual and philosophical evidence establishes primacy in the strong, asymmetric sense his functionalism requires. Casal (2020) extends the critique beyond these internal explanatory-form objections to a critique of scope: Cohen's technological determinism, by privileging productive-force development as history's motor, obscures the causal and explanatory significance of reproductive labor and gender relations, and cannot plausibly explain the persistence of sexist ideologies across historically distinct modes of production — a critique bearing on whether Cohen's determinism inherits an unreflective, narrowly economistic version of the world-historical framework it claims to replace. Forty years after Cohen's original reconstruction, then, the functional-explanation controversy remains a site of live disagreement rather than a settled verdict on whether historical materialism escapes covert teleology.

### 5.4 Base/Superstructure and Structure/Agency

A related but distinct set of difficulties concerns the base/superstructure architecture itself. Lukes (1982) argues that any non-trivial version of the distinction faces a dilemma: the relations of production must be characterizable independently of the legal and political categories supposedly belonging to the superstructure, on pain of circularity, yet standard formulations either fail to achieve this independence or purchase it at the cost of historical inaccuracy — a critique aimed specifically at Cohen's attempt to identify a normatively free ("rechtsfrei") economic structure capable of explaining law and morality. Farrelly (2005) proposes an alternative that reconstructs the base/superstructure relation using the concept of supervenience, such that the superstructure supervenes on, without being reducible to, the economic base, aiming to preserve materialist explanatory priority while avoiding the circularity Lukes identifies. The structure/agency question persists into recent Political Marxism. Post (2021), responding to critics who charge that his account of social-property relations reintroduces transhistorical "laws of motion" incompatible with genuine historical agency, argues that social-property relations can structure agents' actions and generate mode-of-production-specific "developmental patterns" without positing ahistorical, quasi-teleological laws operating independently of class struggle — showing that the question of whether structural explanation smuggles in illegitimate, Hegelian-style determinism remains actively contested well beyond the 1970s–80s Cohen debates. Boveiri (2024) addresses the underlying question from a different angle, arguing that Marx's concept of social totality develops through a critical inversion of Hegelian totality: Marx retains a structurally holistic conception of society and history from Hegel's *Logic* while relocating its ground from Spirit's self-determination to the totality of material social relations and their internal contradictions. On this reading, the persistence of teleological-sounding structure in historical materialism is not simply a defect inherited unreflectively from Hegel, but a consequence of the specific way Marx inverts Hegelian totality's metaphysical ground while preserving its systematic form.

Taken together, the material surveyed in this section indicates that the relationship between historical materialism and idealist philosophy of history is not resolved at any single level. Hegel's teleology, once specified with the precision Yang and Yeomans supply, is a stronger and more determinate claim than the loose historicism Popper attributes jointly to Hegel and Marx, and Suchting's rebuttal suggests Marx's own tendential claims are more modest than Popper's target. Yet the functionalism debate shows that even sympathetic reconstructions of Marx's theory — Cohen's foremost among them — remain vulnerable to the charge that they reintroduce directionality of a broadly Hegelian kind, whether through the biological analogy (Dickman), the primacy thesis (Ruben), the base/superstructure architecture (Lukes), or the scope of what counts as material (Casal). Whether historical materialism achieves a genuine, non-teleological alternative to Hegel's philosophy of history thus remains an open technical question, sharpened rather than settled by more than forty years of controversy over functional explanation, and renewed in contemporary form by the structure/agency disputes within Political Marxism.

## Section 6: Recent Scholarship: The New Reading of Marx, MEGA2 Philology, and Contemporary Reassessments

The Marx-Engels-Gesamtausgabe (MEGA2) — including preparatory manuscripts, successive drafts, excerpt notebooks, and previously unedited correspondence — has reshaped the evidentiary basis for claims about Marx's relation to Hegel. Rather than converging on a single revised account, this philological expansion has multiplied the sites at which the relationship is contested: archival reconstruction of Marx's documented reading, internal disputes within the German "New Reading" over value-form interpretation, granular relocation of specific Hegelian logical categories within Marx's texts, and reassessments extending the Marx-Hegel relationship into ecological, global, and racial-capitalism registers.

### 6.1 MEGA2 Philology and the "Marx Revival"

Levine (2012) uses the MEGA2 edition to reconstruct which texts of Hegel's Marx actually owned, annotated, and could plausibly have read at each stage of his development, arguing that prior scholarship routinely overstated Marx's direct textual access to specific Hegelian works even while correctly identifying Marx's appropriation of Hegel's method against Hegel's system. This archival discipline — distinguishing documented engagement from inferred influence — bears on the genealogical claims examined in Section 1, including Barbour's attention to the Trendelenburg mediation and Kangal's account of Bauer as intellectual partner. Fareld and Kuch (2020) and Musto (2020) frame this MEGA2-enabled scholarship as constituting a distinct post-2010 interpretive moment, a "Marx revival" that moves away from inherited twentieth-century reception narratives — the orthodox "materialist inversion" story and the humanist-critique framework surveyed in Section 3 alike — toward treating the Hegel-Marx relationship as still philosophically open. Musto's edited volume reassesses twenty-two key Marxian concepts against what Marx actually wrote rather than against retrospectively stabilized doctrine, grounding several reassessments in newly accessible manuscripts and previewing the material of Subsection 6.4.

### 6.2 The New Reading of Marx: Heinrich and the Value-Form Debate

The German Neue Marx-Lektüre associated with Michael Heinrich reconstructs Marx's value theory as monetary and non-substantialist, holding that value and abstract labor emerge through exchange rather than existing as a substance prior to it. This reading is itself contested from within the value-theoretic literature. Lietz and Schwarz (2023) examine Heinrich's interpretation against Marx's revisions to *Capital* between the first (1867) and second (1872) editions, arguing that the manuscript evidence Heinrich cites — including passages on commodity fetishism — does not support a purely exchange-constituted account of abstract labor, which Marx instead treats as the social character of private labor presupposed by, rather than produced through, exchange. Moseley (2025), summarizing a book-length study, mounts a comparable chapter-and-verse challenge, arguing that *Capital*'s opening chapter analyzes a "representative commodity" whose value derives from exchange-value relations among all commodities in normal proportions — a reading he presents as directly contradicting Heinrich's monetary interpretation. Liu (2023) frames the dispute as also a dispute over Marx's inheritance of Hegel's abstract-to-concrete methodology, arguing that Heinrich's emphasis on value's form of manifestation reflects a merely formal grasp of dialectical method that neglects value's substance. Bonefeld (2020) represents an adjacent but distinct Anglophone strand, Open Marxism, which shares the New Reading's emphasis on money as the mystified, fetishized form of the value-relation without adopting Heinrich's specific monetary-constitution thesis — indicating that the New Reading is not a unified position even among interpreters sympathetic to its non-substantialist starting point.

### 6.3 New Philological Precision on Hegel's Logic in Marx

A cluster of recent studies locates specific segments of Hegel's *Science of Logic* within particular moments of Marx's text with a precision the systematic-dialectics program of Section 4 generally did not attempt. Schimmenti (2024) relocates Marx's first documented confrontation with Hegel's method to the 1841 doctoral dissertation on Epicurean and Democritean natural philosophy, reading its treatment of the Epicurean atom as already following a precise *Darstellung*, against Althusser's characterization of the dissertation as a merely "scholastic exercise." Ricci (2024) argues that *Capital*'s derivation of the value-form from the isolated commodity draws on Hegel's Logic of Being, producing value as a spectral, fetishized "real abstraction" that governs capitalist social praxis unconsciously, and traces this abstraction's historical genesis to the dispossession analyzed in the chapters on primitive accumulation. Ramas San Miguel (2024) instead locates the relevant resource in Hegel's Doctrine of Essence, reconstructing Marx's concept of "appearance" (*Erscheinung*) — used to treat categories such as price and wages as real yet systematically misleading forms of appearance of underlying value-relations — through the essence-appearance-actuality sequence of the *Wesenslogik*. Ninos (2024) identifies a further, previously underexamined resource: Hegel's distinction between analytic and synthetic finite cognition, which he argues supplies the methodological framework Marx uses in the *Grundrisse* to periodize classical political economy and to mount an immanent critique of Ricardo's method as a case of synthetic cognition's limitations. Taken together, these studies suggest that the question of which parts of Hegel's Logic survive in Marx, posed generally in Section 4, is being answered piecemeal — different studies locating different logical resources (Being, Essence, finite cognition) at different points in Marx's corpus rather than converging on a single governing isomorphism.

### 6.4 Ecological, Global, and Racial-Capitalism Reassessments

A further set of reassessments extends the Marx-Hegel relationship beyond the largely Eurocentric and value-theoretic terms of twentieth-century debate. Saito (2020) distinguishes three levels — universal, social, and historically specific to capitalism — at which Marx's concept of "metabolic rift" operates, reconstructing this concept from Marx's late natural-scientific notebooks made accessible through MEGA2, and argues that this reconstruction supplies a theoretical foundation for an ecological reading of the critique of political economy. Musto (2025) examines the least-studied phase of Marx's development — his late writings on non-Western societies, including the correspondence on the Russian commune and critiques of European colonialism — arguing this material challenges the characterization of Marx as a Eurocentric, economically determinist thinker and instead shows him emphasizing historical specificity and human agency over a unilinear developmental scheme, a point bearing on the determinism debates of Section 5. Zambrana (2025) extends the Marx-Hegel "speculative method" question in a different direction, responding to Gillian Rose's charge that Marx and Marxism retreat from Hegel's "severe style" of speculative thinking into a distinction between thought and actuality that forecloses a proper account of capital. Zambrana argues that Marx does pursue speculative thinking in his account of capital's self-valorizing logic, and that Stephanie Smallwood's history of the Atlantic slave trade is decisive for extending this account beyond free wage-labor to the historical genesis of the capital-relation and the money-form in the trade in enslaved persons — treating this genealogy not as a "prehistory" of capital but as constitutive of it. These reassessments do not converge on a shared thesis; each instead mobilizes MEGA2-informed or methodologically renewed access to Marx's texts to open a distinct axis — ecological, global/postcolonial, racial-capitalist — along which the inherited Marx-Hegel relationship is renegotiated.

Recent scholarship, in sum, does not resolve the questions inherited from the primary-text, continuity/rupture, humanism, and dialectical-method debates surveyed above; it multiplies the sites — archival, value-theoretic, logical, and thematic — at which those questions are renegotiated, confirming rather than closing the interpretive instability this review takes as its organizing premise.

## Conclusion

This review has traced Marx's critique of Hegelian idealism from the 1843 *Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right* through the 1873 afterword to *Capital*, and across the secondary debates that critique has generated — continuity versus rupture, Western Marxism and its anti-humanist critics, the dialectical status of *Capital*'s method, historical materialism's relation to teleology, and the most recent MEGA2-informed scholarship. At every level examined, the meaning of Marx's "materialist inversion" of Hegel has proven to be an object of dispute rather than a settled conclusion. The 1873 formula itself — standing Hegel's dialectic "on its head" to extract a "rational kernel" from its "mystical shell" — supplied this review's anchoring image, but Horn's (2013) close reading shows that Marx's own formulations of what counts as kernel and what as shell are not fully consistent across texts, so the formula names an interpretive problem rather than resolving one. Whether "inversion" designates a change of philosophical content, a transfer of formal method, a restructuring of totality's ground, or some combination of these remains open, and no position surveyed here commands consensus.

A second finding concerns the trajectory of the debate itself: positions one generation treats as settled are regularly reopened by the next, and philological advances tend to multiply rather than close off interpretive options. Confident earlier readings of the *Grundrisse*'s continuity with Hegel's *Logic* have been contested by later close readers who argue the "Notebook on Method" instead dramatizes a practical escape from dialectical derivation — a reversal internal to the systematic-dialectics literature, not merely an external objection to it. The break/continuity question shows the same dynamic: Cowling (2023) re-litigates claims about Marx's alienation theory that earlier participants had treated as adjudicated, defending a qualified discontinuity thesis against renewed continuity arguments. Musto's (2020) framing of a post-2010 "Marx revival," grounded in MEGA2 philology rather than inherited twentieth-century orthodoxies, exemplifies this pattern at the level of the field as a whole: archival access has not converged scholarship on a revised consensus but has opened new sites — philological, value-theoretic, logical, ecological, global — at which the Marx-Hegel relationship is renegotiated.

Three cross-cutting questions remain unresolved. First, whether "method" can be coherently separated from "content" in assessing what Marx retains from Hegel — the question dividing systematic-dialectics readers who locate Hegel's survival at the level of categorial presentation from critics who argue this separation empties Marx's categories of determinate content. Boveiri's (2024) argument for structural continuity between Hegelian and Marxian totality, mediated by inversion of metaphysical ground rather than outright rejection of Hegel's holism, suggests one route through this impasse without settling the narrower value-form disputes it touches. Second, whether functional or teleological-sounding explanation is eliminable from historical materialism without loss of explanatory power — sharpened rather than resolved across four decades of dispute over functionalist reconstructions of Marx and their critics. Third, whether the German New Reading's value-form emphasis is compatible with, or supersedes, Anglophone systematic dialectics — a divide this review has traced without finding grounds, internal to the literature, for treating either as having absorbed the other.

The present project takes up this landscape not by adjudicating among its positions but by treating the persistence of contestation itself as the phenomenon requiring explanation: why a formula as compact as "standing on its head" has sustained over a century and a half of divergent, still-active reconstruction, and what this durability suggests about the relation between Marx's critique of Hegel and the critique of idealist philosophy of history more broadly.
