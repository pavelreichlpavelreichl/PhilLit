## Section 3: Hegelian/Western Marxism, Alienation, and the Anti-Humanist Response

If the Althusserian break thesis treats Marx's Hegelian inheritance as ideology to be excised, the twentieth-century tradition running from Lukács through Korsch to the Frankfurt School reverses the valence entirely: on this reading, Marxism's task is not to purge Hegel but to complete him, retaining dialectical method and, for some figures in this tradition, the category of alienation as Hegelian-derived resources rather than pre-scientific residue. This section surveys that tradition's major statements and the alienation/humanism debate it generates, before noting how thinly the opposing anti-humanist pole is represented in the citable literature available here.

### 3.1 Lukács and Reification

Georg Lukács's reconstruction of reification as the central category of a Hegelian Marxism is often read as a straightforward "return to Hegel" against Second and Third International orthodoxy. Rockmore (1992) complicates this narrative by tracing Lukács's dialectic to Neo-Kantian and Fichtean sources — particularly Emil Lask's mediation of the thing-in-itself problem — arguing that Lukács's *History and Class Consciousness* solves a specifically Kantian epistemological difficulty via an appropriation of Fichte as much as via direct engagement with Hegel. On Rockmore's account, calling Lukács's Marxism simply "Hegelian" obscures this heterogeneous philosophical genealogy. The substantive content of Lukács's reification concept has itself become a site of dispute in its later reception: Chari (2010) defends the political-economic core of Lukács's category — its grounding in the commodity form and revolutionary class praxis — against Axel Honneth's recognition-theoretic reinterpretation of reification as a "forgetting" of prior intersubjective recognition, arguing that Honneth's move sacrifices the critical purchase on capitalism that made Lukács's original formulation effective. Dahbour (2017) situates this dispute within a longer genealogy, tracing how Hegelian totality and reason pass from Lukács through subsequent critical theory down to Honneth, and framing the Chari-Honneth disagreement as one instance of a recurring question about how much Hegelian inheritance critical theory can shed while remaining dialectical rather than merely normative.

### 3.2 Korsch and the Anti-Positivist Thesis

Karl Korsch's *Marxism and Philosophy* (1923) supplies a second founding statement of Western Marxism, arguing that Marxist theory must remain "philosophy" — a moment internal to revolutionary practice — rather than becoming a set of empirically verifiable propositions on the model of positive science. Negt (1975) reconstructs this thesis in terms of the "constitution" of Marxist theory out of concrete class struggle, arguing that Korsch's insistence on theory-practice unity is what commits Marxism to a dialectically self-reflective character irreducible to positivist or mechanistic readings. Browner (1977) situates this anti-positivist stance historically, showing how Korsch's argument that Marxism must remain philosophy was received as a scandal by party orthodoxy in the aftermath of 1917, and how its historicizing conception of Marxist theory anticipated central themes of subsequent Western Marxism. Devlin (2021), however, challenges this standard genealogy directly: rereading *Marxism and Philosophy* as an intervention in interwar debates over Marxist orthodoxy rather than as Marx exegesis proper, Devlin argues Korsch's work presupposed revolutionary possibility rather than the defeatist cultural retreat later associated with "Western Marxism," and that the retrospective grouping of Korsch with Lukács and the Frankfurt School under that label obscures rather than clarifies his actual argument.

### 3.3 The Frankfurt School's Divergent Hegelianisms

The Frankfurt School's relation to Hegel is not uniform across its members. Finlayson (2017) offers a differentiated account of Horkheimer, Adorno, and Marcuse: the young Horkheimer endorses only a qualified Hegelianism, treating Hegel's idealist metaphysics as superseded by sociology, psychology, and materialist historiography; Adorno remains the most committed Hegelian of the three, understanding his own dialectical method as an immanent critique continuous with Hegel's; and Marcuse offers the most charitable reading, emphasizing the critical rather than systematic moment in Hegel's conception of reason. This last claim is the specific subject of Anderson's (1993) reassessment of Marcuse's *Reason and Revolution* fifty years after its publication: Anderson argues that Marcuse's central thesis — that Hegel's dialectic of negativity anticipates and is completed by Marx's critical social theory — remains a defensible corrective to positivist social theory even where particular readings of Hegel's texts have since been revised. Taken together, Finlayson's and Anderson's accounts show that "the Frankfurt School's Hegelianism" names not a single position but a spectrum, with Adorno and Marcuse retaining more of Hegel's dialectic than Horkheimer.

### 3.4 The Alienation/Humanism Debate

The question of whether alienation is a Hegelian category Marx retains and deepens throughout his corpus, or an immature humanist residue superseded by the mature critique of political economy, organizes a distinct but related debate. Dunayevskaya (2018) states the Marxist-Humanist position directly: alienation is inseparable from Marx's debt to the Hegelian dialectic of negativity — the "negation of the negation" — and attacks on the young Marx's Hegelian-inflected humanism are, on her account, simultaneously attacks on the revolutionary core of Hegel's dialectic that Marx inherited and preserved. Ollman (1977) develops a systematic version of the continuity thesis grounded not in Dunayevskaya's dialectic of negativity but in Marx's "philosophy of internal relations," a Hegelian-derived relational ontology: alienation names the rupture of an internally related unity between the worker and his productive activity, product, species-being, and fellow workers, a unity Ollman argues is restored rather than abandoned as the organizing logic of Marx's mature economic categories. Postone (1993) rejects both the Lukácsian and the straightforwardly humanist framings of this inheritance while still affirming a Hegelian debt: he reinterprets Marx's mature critique as fundamentally Hegelian but non-humanist and non-class-subject-centered, targeting the abstract, quasi-automatic domination exercised by value and abstract labor time. On Postone's reading, both "traditional Marxism," which affirms labor as trans-historically emancipatory, and Lukács's positing of the proletariat as the Hegelian identical subject-object of history, misread this structure by locating emancipation in labor's self-realization rather than in the abolition of value-mediated domination itself. Arthur (2000) stakes out a further, distinct position: Hegel's survival in Marx belongs to the level of dialectical method, not humanist content, so that *Capital*'s categories of value-form and capital as automatic subject preserve a genuinely Hegelian dialectical logic even as Marx abandons Hegel's idealist content — a reading that, like Postone's, cuts across the polarity between the humanist-continuity and anti-Hegelian-scientistic camps.

### Section Summary

No single position dominates this literature. Lukács's reification thesis is itself contested from within the tradition it founded (Chari against Honneth's revision), Korsch's place within "Western Marxism" is disputed by revisionist intellectual history (Devlin against the standard genealogy), the Frankfurt School's Hegelianism fractures along Finlayson's Horkheimer-Adorno-Marcuse spectrum, and the alienation/humanism debate yields at least four distinguishable positions — Dunayevskaya's dialectic-of-negativity humanism, Ollman's relational-ontology humanism, Postone's Hegelian but anti-humanist and anti-Lukácsian reconstruction, and Arthur's method-only Hegelianism. Postone's and Arthur's positions in particular indicate that the humanist/anti-humanist axis, as usually posed, is not exhaustive: both affirm a substantial Hegelian debt while explicitly rejecting the humanist-alienation reading that debt is often taken to require. A significant limitation of the present bibliography should be flagged here: the anti-humanist pole proper — Lucio Colletti's argument that Hegelian dialectical logic is incompatible with Marx's materialism, and Althusser's rejection of alienation as ideological — is thinly represented in citable material, since the founding texts on this side lack retrievable abstracts under current metadata. This gap is only partially mitigated by Section 2's engagement with Balibar's, Musto's, and Cowling's characterization of the Althusserian break thesis; a fuller reconstruction of the anti-humanist case against Hegelian Marxism would require targeted bibliographic remediation.
