## Section 2: Continuity or Rupture? The Althusser Debate

Beyond disputes over what Marx's texts themselves say about Hegel, a second-order controversy asks how Marx's own intellectual development should be periodized: does the transition from the 1844 *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts* to the mature critique of political economy constitute a decisive rupture with Marx's own earlier, Hegelian-humanist thought, or an underlying continuity beneath changing vocabulary? Louis Althusser's thesis of an "epistemological break" occurring around 1845 reframes this question as internal to Marx's biography rather than external to it: on this view, the young Marx of the manuscripts is himself still enclosed within an "ideological" problematic derived from Feuerbach and Hegel, and the mature Marx of *Capital* achieves a scientific rupture with that problematic rather than a mere refinement of it.

A significant evidentiary limitation must be flagged at the outset of this section. Althusser's own founding statements of the break thesis—*For Marx* and, with Étienne Balibar, *Reading Capital*—lack retrievable abstracts in the current bibliography and are excluded from direct citation per project convention, as are the principal continuity-side rejoinders: E. P. Thompson's *The Poverty of Theory*, Norman Geras's *Marx and Human Nature*, István Mészáros's *Marx's Theory of Alienation*, and Bertell Ollman's 1971 *Alienation*. These are not minor omissions; they are the debate's namesake texts on both sides. The present section therefore characterizes the break thesis and its reception primarily through three works that discuss and evaluate that debate rather than through the primary combatants themselves. Readers seeking the substantive continuity-side case should consult Section 3's discussion of alienation, where Ollman's 1977 restatement of his relational-ontology argument and Dunayevskaya's Marxist-Humanist account of alienation as a permanent, Hegel-derived category are cited directly, since both carry retrievable abstracts.

Within the citable literature, Balibar (1978)—himself Althusser's collaborator on *Reading Capital*—traces the "epistemological break" concept's genealogy from Bachelard's philosophy of science through Canguilhem before its appropriation for reading Marx, scrutinizing how the concept's meaning shifted as it migrated from the history of the natural sciences into historical materialism. This internal genealogical audit suggests that the break concept's applicability to Marx was, from within Althusser's own circle, treated as requiring justification rather than as self-evidently transferable. Musto (2015) approaches the question philologically rather than conceptually, examining the international reception history of the 1844 Manuscripts and arguing that both continuity readings (which treat the manuscripts as a finished statement of Marx's whole philosophy) and Althusserian discontinuity readings (which treat them as superseded ideology) rest on an indefensible treatment of what was, in fact, an unfinished, exploratory text. On Musto's account, "the young Marx" invoked by both sides of the debate is substantially a retrospective construction rather than a textually grounded unity. Cowling (2023) most recently re-weighs the accumulated arguments for and against continuity, explicitly revisiting Althusser's position alongside Meszaros's and Geras's, and defends a qualified discontinuity thesis: that alienation theory, while recurring occasionally in vocabulary after 1847, does not perform load-bearing theoretical work in Marx's mature political economy.

Taken together, these three sources indicate that the break/continuity question has not converged toward resolution; if anything, Musto's and Cowling's more recent interventions suggest the debate increasingly turns on contested premises about what kind of textual unity "the young Marx" ever possessed, rather than on a settled reading of either side's primary texts.
