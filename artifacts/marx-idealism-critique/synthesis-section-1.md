## Introduction

Marx's relationship to Hegel is conventionally summarized by a single image: dialectic "standing on its head," to be "turned right side up again" so as to discover "the rational kernel within the mystical shell," as Marx put it in the 1873 afterword to the second German edition of *Capital*. This review pursues two connected aims. The first is to reconstruct Marx's own critique of Hegelian idealism and of the idealist philosophy of history across his career, from the 1843 *Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right* through the 1873 afterword's retrospective formula. The second is to survey the extensive secondary literature this critique has generated — debates over continuity and rupture in Marx's development, the status of dialectical method in *Capital*, the relation of Hegelian and Western Marxism to anti-humanist readings, the coherence of historical materialism as an alternative to idealist philosophy of history, and recent New Reading and MEGA2-based scholarship.

The 1873 formula serves as this review's anchor image, but not as a settled point of departure. Horn (2013) treats the "rational kernel"/"mystical shell" metaphor itself as a textual problem: Marx's scattered formulations of what is to be extracted from Hegel's dialectic, and what discarded, are not fully consistent with one another, so that the metaphor names an interpretive task rather than announcing its own solution. This review's organizing premise follows from Horn's diagnosis — the "materialist inversion" is not a fixed doctrine that scholarship merely applies to particular texts, but is itself the object of sustained and, at every level, unresolved dispute. Fine (2001) frames the resulting field in terms of a historiographical division between an older narrative of a clean, total break between Hegel and Marx and a body of "revisionist" scholarship that instead finds *Capital* retaining substantive methodological debts to Hegelian dialectic. Where a text like Fine's positions itself along this axis structures how later, more specialized interventions in the debate should be read.

This contestation is not confined to method. Avineri (1968) supplies an early and influential account of Marx's mature categories — civil society, alienation, the state — as transformed rather than simply rejected Hegelian categories, establishing a chronological and conceptual scaffolding against which later, more skeptical readings can be measured. Boveiri (2024) extends this question of inheritance to the level of totality, arguing that Marx retains a structurally holistic conception of society and history from Hegel's *Science of Logic* even while relocating its ground from Spirit's self-determination to the material relations and contradictions of social life. Musto (2020), reassessing Marx's key concepts in light of the MEGA2 edition and previously neglected manuscripts, shows that even the most recent philological turn in Marx scholarship multiplies rather than resolves these questions of inheritance and inversion.

The review proceeds from this premise through six stages: the primary-text development of Marx's critique of Hegel and the Young Hegelian mediation; the continuity/rupture debate inaugurated by Althusser; Hegelian and Western Marxism's alienation-centered readings against anti-humanist responses; the status of dialectical method in *Capital*, including the analytical-Marxist rejection of dialectic; historical materialism's contested relation to idealist philosophy of history; and recent New Reading and MEGA2 scholarship. At each stage, the throughline is the same: what counts as the "rational kernel" of Hegel's philosophy, and what counts as its discardable "mystical shell," remains a live question rather than a settled inheritance.
