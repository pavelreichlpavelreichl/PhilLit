## Section 6: Recent Scholarship — The New Reading of Marx, MEGA2 Philology, and Contemporary Reassessments

The Marx-Engels-Gesamtausgabe (MEGA2) — including preparatory manuscripts, successive drafts, excerpt notebooks, and previously unedited correspondence — has reshaped the evidentiary basis for claims about Marx's relation to Hegel. Rather than converging on a single revised account, this philological expansion has multiplied the sites at which the relationship is contested: archival reconstruction of Marx's documented reading, internal disputes within the German "New Reading" over value-form interpretation, granular relocation of specific Hegelian logical categories within Marx's texts, and reassessments extending the Marx-Hegel relationship into ecological, global, and racial-capitalism registers.

### Subsection 6.1: MEGA2 Philology and the "Marx Revival"

Levine (2012) uses the MEGA2 edition to reconstruct which texts of Hegel's Marx actually owned, annotated, and could plausibly have read at each stage of his development, arguing that prior scholarship routinely overstated Marx's direct textual access to specific Hegelian works even while correctly identifying Marx's appropriation of Hegel's method against Hegel's system. This archival discipline — distinguishing documented engagement from inferred influence — bears on the genealogical claims examined in Section 1, including Barbour's attention to the Trendelenburg mediation and Kangal's account of Bauer as intellectual partner. Fareld and Kuch (2020) and Musto (2020) frame this MEGA2-enabled scholarship as constituting a distinct post-2010 interpretive moment, a "Marx revival" that moves away from inherited twentieth-century reception narratives — the orthodox "materialist inversion" story and the humanist-critique framework surveyed in Section 3 alike — toward treating the Hegel-Marx relationship as still philosophically open. Musto's edited volume reassesses twenty-two key Marxian concepts against what Marx actually wrote rather than against retrospectively stabilized doctrine, grounding several reassessments in newly accessible manuscripts and previewing the material of Subsection 6.4.

### Subsection 6.2: The New Reading of Marx — Heinrich and the Value-Form Debate

The German Neue Marx-Lektüre associated with Michael Heinrich reconstructs Marx's value theory as monetary and non-substantialist, holding that value and abstract labor emerge through exchange rather than existing as a substance prior to it. This reading is itself contested from within the value-theoretic literature. Lietz and Schwarz (2023) examine Heinrich's interpretation against Marx's revisions to *Capital* between the first (1867) and second (1872) editions, arguing that the manuscript evidence Heinrich cites — including passages on commodity fetishism — does not support a purely exchange-constituted account of abstract labor, which Marx instead treats as the social character of private labor presupposed by, rather than produced through, exchange. Moseley (2025), summarizing a book-length study, mounts a comparable chapter-and-verse challenge, arguing that *Capital*'s opening chapter analyzes a "representative commodity" whose value derives from exchange-value relations among all commodities in normal proportions — a reading he presents as directly contradicting Heinrich's monetary interpretation. Liu (2023) frames the dispute as also a dispute over Marx's inheritance of Hegel's abstract-to-concrete methodology, arguing that Heinrich's emphasis on value's form of manifestation reflects a merely formal grasp of dialectical method that neglects value's substance. Bonefeld (2020) represents an adjacent but distinct Anglophone strand, Open Marxism, which shares the New Reading's emphasis on money as the mystified, fetishized form of the value-relation without adopting Heinrich's specific monetary-constitution thesis — indicating that the New Reading is not a unified position even among interpreters sympathetic to its non-substantialist starting point.

### Subsection 6.3: New Philological Precision on Hegel's Logic in Marx

A cluster of recent studies locates specific segments of Hegel's *Science of Logic* within particular moments of Marx's text with a precision the systematic-dialectics program of Section 4 generally did not attempt. Schimmenti (2024) relocates Marx's first documented confrontation with Hegel's method to the 1841 doctoral dissertation on Epicurean and Democritean natural philosophy, reading its treatment of the Epicurean atom as already following a precise *Darstellung*, against Althusser's characterization of the dissertation as a merely "scholastic exercise." Ricci (2024) argues that *Capital*'s derivation of the value-form from the isolated commodity draws on Hegel's Logic of Being, producing value as a spectral, fetishized "real abstraction" that governs capitalist social praxis unconsciously, and traces this abstraction's historical genesis to the dispossession analyzed in the chapters on primitive accumulation. Ramas San Miguel (2024) instead locates the relevant resource in Hegel's Doctrine of Essence, reconstructing Marx's concept of "appearance" (*Erscheinung*) — used to treat categories such as price and wages as real yet systematically misleading forms of appearance of underlying value-relations — through the essence-appearance-actuality sequence of the *Wesenslogik*. Ninos (2024) identifies a further, previously underexamined resource: Hegel's distinction between analytic and synthetic finite cognition, which he argues supplies the methodological framework Marx uses in the *Grundrisse* to periodize classical political economy and to mount an immanent critique of Ricardo's method as a case of synthetic cognition's limitations. Taken together, these studies suggest that the question of which parts of Hegel's Logic survive in Marx, posed generally in Section 4, is being answered piecemeal — different studies locating different logical resources (Being, Essence, finite cognition) at different points in Marx's corpus rather than converging on a single governing isomorphism.

### Subsection 6.4: Ecological, Global, and Racial-Capitalism Reassessments

A further set of reassessments extends the Marx-Hegel relationship beyond the largely Eurocentric and value-theoretic terms of twentieth-century debate. Saito (2020) distinguishes three levels — universal, social, and historically specific to capitalism — at which Marx's concept of "metabolic rift" operates, reconstructing this concept from Marx's late natural-scientific notebooks made accessible through MEGA2, and argues that this reconstruction supplies a theoretical foundation for an ecological reading of the critique of political economy. Musto (2025) examines the least-studied phase of Marx's development — his late writings on non-Western societies, including the correspondence on the Russian commune and critiques of European colonialism — arguing this material challenges the characterization of Marx as a Eurocentric, economically determinist thinker and instead shows him emphasizing historical specificity and human agency over a unilinear developmental scheme, a point bearing on the determinism debates of Section 5. Zambrana (2025) extends the Marx-Hegel "speculative method" question in a different direction, responding to Gillian Rose's charge that Marx and Marxism retreat from Hegel's "severe style" of speculative thinking into a distinction between thought and actuality that forecloses a proper account of capital. Zambrana argues that Marx does pursue speculative thinking in his account of capital's self-valorizing logic, and that Stephanie Smallwood's history of the Atlantic slave trade is decisive for extending this account beyond free wage-labor to the historical genesis of the capital-relation and the money-form in the trade in enslaved persons — treating this genealogy not as a "prehistory" of capital but as constitutive of it. These reassessments do not converge on a shared thesis; each instead mobilizes MEGA2-informed or methodologically renewed access to Marx's texts to open a distinct axis — ecological, global/postcolonial, racial-capitalist — along which the inherited Marx-Hegel relationship is renegotiated.

Recent scholarship, in sum, does not resolve the questions inherited from the primary-text, continuity/rupture, humanism, and dialectical-method debates surveyed above; it multiplies the sites — archival, value-theoretic, logical, and thematic — at which those questions are renegotiated, confirming rather than closing the interpretive instability this review takes as its organizing premise.
