## Section 5: Historical Materialism vs. Idealist Philosophy of History — Teleology, Determinism, Structure/Agency

If Sections 1 through 4 examine whether Marx's *method* remains Hegelian, this section addresses the question posed at the level of *content*: what, precisely, does historical materialism reject and retain from Hegel's philosophy of history, and does it succeed in replacing providential teleology with a genuinely non-teleological account of historical change? The debate proceeds in layers. Hegel's own philosophy of history must first be specified with some precision, since looser characterizations of "teleology" make the subsequent charge against Marx either too easy or too vague to assess. Popper's historicism charge then treats Hegel and Marx as instances of a single logical structure, a claim contested on textual grounds. Cohen's functionalist reconstruction of historical materialism becomes the central flashpoint of the secondary literature, generating four decades of dispute over whether functional explanation of relations of production by productive-force development smuggles back a covert, quasi-Hegelian directionality. Finally, the base/superstructure architecture and the structure/agency question face difficulties that persist independently of, and beyond, the functionalism controversy.

### Subsection 5.1: Hegel's Teleological Philosophy of History

Hegel's lectures on the philosophy of world history present history as the rational, necessary self-realization of Spirit as freedom, unfolding through successive epochs in which an increasing portion of humanity comes to be recognized as free; individuals pursuing their own passions and private interests unwittingly serve this larger rational end, a mechanism Hegel calls the "cunning of reason" (Hegel and Forbes 1975). Parkinson (1989) asks how much of this structure survives in Marx, arguing that Marx's account of individuals acting from private, material motives who nonetheless bring about historically necessary transformations of the mode of production reproduces a version of the cunning of reason, even though Marx does not consciously borrow the doctrine from Hegel. This suggests, on Parkinson's reading, more continuity between the two theories of history than is sometimes supposed. Yang and Yeomans (2023) supply a more technical baseline against which such continuity claims can be evaluated, reconstructing Hegel's teleology from the *Science of Logic*'s account of self-determining, internally purposive development rather than as externally imposed design. On their reading, Hegelian teleology is a structure of reciprocal interaction in which the purpose functions as an immanent governing principle realized through durable means — a specification of "teleology" considerably more precise than the loose historicist sense later deployed by Popper, and one against which subsequent charges that historical materialism is "covertly teleological" can be assessed with greater rigor.

### Subsection 5.2: The Popperian Charge and Its Rebuttal

Popper's *Open Society and Its Enemies* treats Hegel's dialectical philosophy of history and Marx's materialist theory of history as sharing a common "historicist" logical structure: both, on Popper's reading, claim to have discovered inexorable laws governing history's overall direction, and both use this claim to underwrite totalitarian political conclusions; Marx's productive-forces determinism is presented as a materialist transposition of Hegel's Spirit rather than a genuine break with idealist historicism (Popper 2012). Suchting (1972) mounts a detailed rebuttal, arguing that Popper conflates several distinct claims — holism, essentialism, unconditional prediction, large-scale utopian social engineering — that Marx's actual theory does not jointly endorse. Distinguishing two possible senses of "law of historical development" and four models of historical determinism, Suchting argues that Marx's claims about tendencies in productive-force development are considerably more modest than the target Popper's arguments are designed to hit. The Popper-Suchting exchange thus establishes, at the outset of the functionalism debate proper, that the charge of Hegelian-style historicism is itself textually contested rather than self-evidently applicable to Marx.

### Subsection 5.3: Cohen's Functionalism and the Determinism/Teleology Debate

Cohen's functionalist reconstruction of historical materialism — defended, in the citable literature, through his direct reply to Elster — holds that a given set of production relations obtains because it is suited to developing the productive forces, on the model of functional explanation in evolutionary biology, and that such explanations can be legitimate even absent a fully specified elaborating mechanism, provided there is independent evidence of the functional correlation (Cohen 2013). This reconstruction becomes the central object of dispute. Elster (1990) argues that adequate explanation in Marxist theory must ultimately be grounded in the intentional actions of individuals, and that functionalist explanations of the kind Cohen offers are explanatorily inadequate unless supplemented by an account of the individual-level mechanisms generating the alleged functional correlation. Honderich (1982) presses the teleology charge most directly against Cohen specifically — as distinct from Popper's broader historicism charge against Hegel and Marx jointly — contending that attributing to productive forces a tendency or disposition to develop imports an unwarranted, quasi-purposive structure into what should be a straightforwardly causal materialist theory; Honderich further notes that Marx's own texts may offer no positive evidence, and arguably some evidence against, a teleological reading. Wood (1986) responds from within the functionalist camp, arguing that Elster's philosophical objections to functional explanation rest on a caricature of that mode of explanation as practiced in the social sciences, just as Elster's critique of Marx's use of teleology rests on a caricature of the kind of teleological claims Marx actually makes — directly rebutting Elster's methodological-individualist position. Dickman (1990) raises independent philosophy-of-science objections, arguing that the analogy to functional explanation in evolutionary biology is disanalogous in ways that undermine its legitimacy in the social case, and that even granting the analogy, such explanations cannot do the specific work historical materialism requires of them, namely explaining epochal transitions rather than mere social stability. Ruben (1981) narrows the scrutiny to Cohen's "primacy thesis" — that relations of production are explained by their suitability for developing the productive forces rather than the reverse — questioning whether Cohen's textual and philosophical evidence establishes primacy in the strong, asymmetric sense his functionalism requires. Casal (2020) extends the critique beyond these internal explanatory-form objections to a critique of scope: Cohen's technological determinism, by privileging productive-force development as history's motor, obscures the causal and explanatory significance of reproductive labor and gender relations, and cannot plausibly explain the persistence of sexist ideologies across historically distinct modes of production — a critique bearing on whether Cohen's determinism inherits an unreflective, narrowly economistic version of the world-historical framework it claims to replace. Forty years after Cohen's original reconstruction, then, the functional-explanation controversy remains a site of live disagreement rather than a settled verdict on whether historical materialism escapes covert teleology.

### Subsection 5.4: Base/Superstructure and Structure/Agency

A related but distinct set of difficulties concerns the base/superstructure architecture itself. Lukes (1982) argues that any non-trivial version of the distinction faces a dilemma: the relations of production must be characterizable independently of the legal and political categories supposedly belonging to the superstructure, on pain of circularity, yet standard formulations either fail to achieve this independence or purchase it at the cost of historical inaccuracy — a critique aimed specifically at Cohen's attempt to identify a normatively free ("rechtsfrei") economic structure capable of explaining law and morality. Farrelly (2005) proposes an alternative that reconstructs the base/superstructure relation using the concept of supervenience, such that the superstructure supervenes on, without being reducible to, the economic base, aiming to preserve materialist explanatory priority while avoiding the circularity Lukes identifies. The structure/agency question persists into recent Political Marxism. Post (2021), responding to critics who charge that his account of social-property relations reintroduces transhistorical "laws of motion" incompatible with genuine historical agency, argues that social-property relations can structure agents' actions and generate mode-of-production-specific "developmental patterns" without positing ahistorical, quasi-teleological laws operating independently of class struggle — showing that the question of whether structural explanation smuggles in illegitimate, Hegelian-style determinism remains actively contested well beyond the 1970s–80s Cohen debates. Boveiri (2024) addresses the underlying question from a different angle, arguing that Marx's concept of social totality develops through a critical inversion of Hegelian totality: Marx retains a structurally holistic conception of society and history from Hegel's *Logic* while relocating its ground from Spirit's self-determination to the totality of material social relations and their internal contradictions. On this reading, the persistence of teleological-sounding structure in historical materialism is not simply a defect inherited unreflectively from Hegel, but a consequence of the specific way Marx inverts Hegelian totality's metaphysical ground while preserving its systematic form.

Taken together, the material surveyed in this section indicates that the relationship between historical materialism and idealist philosophy of history is not resolved at any single level. Hegel's teleology, once specified with the precision Yang and Yeomans supply, is a stronger and more determinate claim than the loose historicism Popper attributes jointly to Hegel and Marx, and Suchting's rebuttal suggests Marx's own tendential claims are more modest than Popper's target. Yet the functionalism debate shows that even sympathetic reconstructions of Marx's theory — Cohen's foremost among them — remain vulnerable to the charge that they reintroduce directionality of a broadly Hegelian kind, whether through the biological analogy (Dickman), the primacy thesis (Ruben), the base/superstructure architecture (Lukes), or the scope of what counts as material (Casal). Whether historical materialism achieves a genuine, non-teleological alternative to Hegel's philosophy of history thus remains an open technical question, sharpened rather than settled by more than forty years of controversy over functional explanation, and renewed in contemporary form by the structure/agency disputes within Political Marxism.
