## Introduction

This document is a chronological catalogue of English-language scholarship on Marx published since 1990. It is not a thematic or debate-driven review. It lists articles, monographs and edited volumes published between 1990 and 2026 in which Marx (his texts, concepts or legacy) appears in the title or is the primary subject. The catalogue is ordered by decade, then by year, with entries in alphabetical order by author within each year. Each entry receives a one- or two-sentence overview drawn from the note and abstract in its BibTeX record. Its purpose is to let readers see what was published on Marx, and when, rather than to argue for a reading of Marx.

### Method

The sample was assembled through six search partitions. These were working divisions of the search, not sections of the review: (1) philosophy, alienation and ethics; (2) *Capital*, value theory and political economy; (3) historical materialism, politics, class and the state; (4) ecology, gender, colonialism and technology; (5) biography, reception and philosophical relations; and (6) a title-based sweep of each decade to close gaps. The Stanford and Internet Encyclopedias of Philosophy were used for orientation. Candidate works were located through Semantic Scholar, OpenAlex, CORE and PhilPapers, and bibliographic details were checked against CrossRef. After removal of one cross-file duplicate, the catalogue contains 446 entries. Some titles are well known, for example Postone's (1993) *Time, Labor, and Social Domination*, a reinterpretation of Marx's critical theory as a theory of historically specific forms of social domination; Derrida's (1994) "Spectres of Marx"; and Stedman Jones's (2016) biography *Karl Marx*. They are listed in the same format as every other entry.

### Limitations

The list is a sample, not a census, and readers should take the following into account.

- Only English-language works are included. Marx scholarship in German, French, Chinese, Japanese, Spanish and other languages is under-represented.
- Coverage in OpenAlex, CORE and PhilPapers has gaps. The per-year counts (roughly 10 to 17 entries) reflect search results, not publication volume, and should not be read as measures of scholarly activity.
- Entries flagged "[unverified]" are books without a DOI whose bibliographic details CrossRef could not confirm.
- Entries marked "(title-based description)" have no retrieved abstract. Their overviews are hedged and say only what the title and available notes indicate.
- Where CrossRef and Semantic Scholar disagreed on a publication date, the BibTeX `year` field is used for ordering. Some keys therefore carry a year that differs from the year in which the item is placed; for example, buchwalter1991hegel appears under 2008 and leopold2017greatness under 2019.
- Derrida's book *Specters of Marx* and his 1994 *New Left Review* essay "Spectres of Marx" are distinct works and are described separately.
- Some entries are book reviews, review essays or encyclopedia-style articles, and some treat Marx as one of several subjects. These are flagged in the entry line, as are works that are critical of or skeptical about Marx or Marxism.

### How to use the catalogue

The catalogue has four decade sections: the 1990s, the 2000s, the 2010s, and 2020-2026 (a partial decade, with 2026 covered only up to the date of the search). Each is divided into year ranges, and individual years appear as short headings. A brief conclusion then reports patterns visible in the sample, without evaluating the works themselves.

## Section 1: The 1990s (1990-1999)

### 1.1 1990-1994

Several entries in these years respond to the collapse of communist states (Krygier 1990; Samuels 1993; Hudelson 1993), while others continue the justice, rights and Hegel-Marx discussions.

#### 1990

- Adler (1990), *Marx, Machines, and Skill*: argues against the assumption that Marx predicted machinery-driven deskilling, distinguishing the different effects of technology on skill requirements.
- Berki (1990), *Through and Through Hegel: Marx's Road to Communism*: argues that ambiguities in Marx's attitude to communism reveal the Hegelian foundations of his concept of communism, tracing his move from radical liberalism grounded in the Hegelian rational state.
- Bimber (1990), *Karl Marx and the Three Faces of Technological Determinism*: distinguishes norm-based, unintended-consequences and nomological accounts of technological determinism and uses them to sort conflicting readings of Marx on technology and social change.
- Bryan (1990), *"Natural" and "Improved" Land in Marx's Theory of Rent*: examines how Marx's value theory treats land and natural resources, arguing that the natural/improved distinction in his theory of rent is hard to reconcile with a labour-based account of value.
- Jackson (1990), *Marx's 'Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right'*: examines Marx's 1843 critique, the early text in which he works out his break with Hegelian political philosophy.
- Katz (1990), *The Problems of Europocentrism and Evolutionism in Marx's Writings on Colonialism*: examines whether Marx's colonial writings, including his newspaper articles, were Eurocentric and evolutionist, separating Marx's own ideas from his critics' claims.
- Krygier (1990), *Marxism and the Rule of Law: Reflections After the Collapse of Communism*: a critical-skeptical assessment of the relation between Marx's thought and the fate of law in the communist states of the Soviet Union and Eastern and Central Europe after 1989; concerns Marxist thought more than Marx's texts.
- Panichas and Hobart (1990), *Marx's Theory of Revolutionary Change*: discusses Marx's account of revolutionary change within the reconsideration of historical materialism prompted by G. A. Cohen's *Karl Marx's Theory of History*.
- Peffer (1990), *Marxism, Morality, and Social Justice*: a book on how to reconcile Marx's explicit criticisms of morality with the moral commitments evident in his outlook, within the debate over Marx and justice.
- Rothbard (1990), *Karl Marx: Communist as Religious Eschatologist*: an Austrian-school critique presenting Marx's communism as a secularized religious eschatology; critical-skeptical (title-based description).
- Stallybrass (1990), *Marx and Heterogeneity: Thinking the Lumpenproletariat*: the title indicates a reading of Marx's treatment of the lumpenproletariat, especially in the *Eighteenth Brumaire*, as a problem of class heterogeneity and representation (title-based description).

#### 1991

- Barbalet (1991), *Class and Rationality: Olson's Critique of Marx*: examines Mancur Olson's rational-choice critique of Marxian class theory and its implications for Marx's account of class action (title-based description).
- Cartelier (1991), *Marx's Theory of Value, Exchange and Surplus Value: A Suggested Reformulation*: the title indicates a proposed reformulation of Marx's theory of value, exchange and surplus value (title-based description).
- Fracchia (1991), *Marx's Aufhebung of Philosophy and the Foundations of a Materialist Science of History*: discusses how Marx's Aufhebung of philosophy grounds a materialist science of history, addressing Marx's relation to philosophy as such.
- Langman (1991), *Alienation and Everyday Life: Goffman Meets Marx at the Shopping Mall*: combines Goffman's dramaturgical sociology with the Marxian concept of alienation to analyse everyday life in the consumer setting of the mall (Marx is one of two frameworks).
- Larrain (1991), *Classical Political Economists and Marx on Colonialism and "Backward" Nations*: the title indicates a comparison of classical political economists and Marx on colonialism and "backward" nations (title-based description).
- Lynch and Fuhrman (1991), *Recovering and Expanding the Normative: Marx and the New Sociology of Scientific Knowledge*: argues that the strong programme in the sociology of scientific knowledge can recover a normative dimension through Marx's account of the social determination of ideas (Marx is one of two named frameworks).
- Murray (1991), *Respecifying Marx's Value Categories*: reconsiders theoretically and empirically Marx's law of the falling rate of profit, proposing a respecification of his value categories for measuring profitability in North American capitalism.
- Parker (1991), *Unthinking Sex: Marx, Engels and the Scene of Writing*: argues that Western Marxism's dependence on the category of production stems partly from an antitheatricalism that keeps sexuality from acquiring the political weight of class, reading scenes from Marx's and Engels's private life (Marx and Engels are joint objects).
- Shuklian (1991), *Marx on Credit, Interest and Financial Instability*: the title indicates a reconstruction of Marx's treatment of credit, interest and financial instability (title-based description).
- Weisskopf (1991), *Marxian Crisis Theory and the Contradictions of Late Twentieth-Century Capitalism*: the title indicates an application of Marxian crisis theory to late twentieth-century capitalism; Marx supplies the framework rather than being the main topic (title-based description).

#### 1992

- Baxter (1992), *Marx, Class Consciousness, and the Transition to Socialism*: traces Marx's argument, in *Capital* I and the *Manifesto*, that capitalist development produces a revolutionary class consciousness, and examines its role in the transition to socialism.
- Chattopadhyay (1992), *The Economic Content of Socialism: Marx vs. Lenin*: contrasts Marx's socialism of free producers, without private property, commodity relations, wage labour or state, with Lenin's state-owned, wage-labour-based socialism; critical of the Leninist model.
- Geras (1992), *Bringing Marx to Justice: An Addendum and Rejoinder*: a rejoinder in the justice debate defending the claim that Marx's condemnation of capitalism rests on an implicit conception of justice despite his explicit disavowals.
- Kain (1992), *Modern Feminism and Marx*: the title indicates an examination of the relation of modern feminism to Marx (title-based description).
- Katz (1992), *Marx on the Peasantry: Class in Itself or Class in Struggle?*: examines Marx's historical writings on the peasantry to clarify his concept of class, in particular objective versus subjective conditions of class formation.
- Khalil (1992), *Marx's Understanding of the Essence of Capitalism*: the title indicates an interpretation of what Marx took to be the essence of capitalism (title-based description).
- Kluchert (1992), *The Paradigm and the Parody: Karl Marx and the French Revolution in the Class Struggles from 1848-1851*: reads Marx's writings on the French Revolution and the class struggles of 1848-1851 through the model of paradigm and parody (title-based description).
- Lapides (1992), *Henryk Grossmann and the Debate on the Theoretical Status of Marx's Capital*: reconstructs Grossmann's argument on whether *Capital* is Marx's complete economics or a fragment of the larger six-book plan, notably the alleged missing book on wage labour.
- Lianos and Droucopoulos (1992), *Price Determination in Chapter X of Volume III of Marx's Capital*: argues that Marx's concepts of individual value, market value, market price and price of production in *Capital* III, chapter 10 can be assembled into a unified theory of price determination under competition.
- Likitkijsomboon (1992), *The Hegelian Dialectic and Marx's Capital*: discusses the role of the Hegelian dialectic in *Capital*, contributing to the Hegel-Marx methodological debate in economics.

#### 1993

- Fluxman (1993), *Marx, Socialism, and Rights*: examines Marx's claim that socialism need not rely on a framework of rights and argues that this strategy fails because rights are necessary for individual autonomy.
- Hayes (1993), *Marx's Analysis of the French Class Structure*: the title indicates an examination of how Marx analysed the class structure of France in his political-historical writings (title-based description).
- Hazelrigg (1993), *Marx and the Meter of Nature*: the title indicates a discussion of Marx and the "meter of nature" (title-based description).
- Hudelson (1993), *Has History Refuted Marxism?*: asks whether the fall of communism refutes Marxism, arguing that Stalinism partly rests on non-Marxist elements while Marxism still bears on the verdict; a critical-skeptical engagement with Marxism rather than Marx's texts.
- Kain (1993), *Marx, Housework, and Alienation*: applies Marx's theory of alienation to housework, asking whether unpaid domestic labour is alienated in the Marxian sense.
- Katz (1993), *Karl Marx on the Transition from Feudalism to Capitalism*: the title indicates an examination of Marx's account of the transition from feudalism to capitalism within his theory of history (title-based description).
- Keen (1993), *Use-Value, Exchange Value, and the Demise of Marx's Labor Theory of Value*: a critical assessment arguing that logical problems in Marx's treatment of use-value and exchange value lead to the demise of the labour theory of value as a theory of price.
- Postone (1993), *Time, Labor, and Social Domination: A Reinterpretation of Marx's Critical Theory*: reinterprets Marx's mature critique as a theory of historically specific forms of social domination mediated by labour, rather than a labour-centred critique of market distribution.
- Saad-Filho (1993), *A Note on Marx's Analysis of the Composition of Capital*: distinguishes technical, value and organic compositions of capital in Marx and traces the distinctions to the relation between production and circulation, arguing this matters for accumulation and technical change.
- Samuels (1993), *The Status of Marx after the Disintegration of the USSR*: the title indicates a consideration of Marx's standing after the Soviet collapse, separating Marx from Soviet practice (title-based description).
- Vincent (1993), *Marx and Law*: surveys Marx's scattered observations on law, which interweave philosophical, political, economic and legal strands without amounting to a systematic jurisprudence.

#### 1994

- Bryer (1994), *Why Marx's Labour Theory is Superior to the Marginalist Theory of Value: The Case from Modern Financial Reporting*: the title indicates an argument from financial accounting that Marx's labour theory of value is superior to marginalist theory (title-based description).
- Cahan (1994), *The Concept of Property in Marx's Theory of History: A Defense of the Autonomy of the Socioeconomic Base*: argues that the concept of property, which appears in both base and superstructure, can defend the autonomy of the socioeconomic base in Marx's theory of history.
- Chakrabarty (1994), *Marx After Marxism: History, Subalternity, and Difference*: discusses history, subalternity and difference as a postcolonial rereading of Marx after Marxism.
- Corlett (1994), *Marx and Rights*: asks whether there is room for rights in Marx's social philosophy, against the view that Marxism, unlike liberalism, cannot respect rights.
- Derrida (1994), *Specters of Marx: The State of the Debt, the Work of Mourning and the New International*: a book-length reading of Marx's spectral language and the "new International," responding to post-1989 proclamations of the death of Marx (French original 1993; English edition 1994) (title-based and note-based description). [unverified]
- Derrida (1994), *Spectres of Marx* (the essay, distinct from the book): an essay on Marx's legacy; the note describes it via the title and the parallel book's themes of "spectropoetics" and the "new International" (title-based description).
- Lapavitsas (1994), *The Banking School and the Monetary Thought of Karl Marx*: reconstructs Marx's engagement with the Banking School and the Bullion and Currency controversies to place his monetary thought in its historical context.
- McCarthy (1994), *The Uncertain Future of Ideology: Rereading Marx*: rereads Marx's theory of ideology to assess the future of ideology as a concept in the social sciences.
- Pancaldi (1994), *The Technology of Nature: Marx's Thoughts on Darwin*: a chapter whose title indicates a treatment of Marx's remarks on Darwin and the technology of nature (title-based description).
- Smith (1994), *Alienation, Exploitation and Abstract Labor: A Humanist Defense of Marx's Theory of Value*: offers a humanist defence of Marx's theory of value, linking alienation, exploitation and abstract labour.

### 1.2 1995-1999

Several entries in these years respond to Derrida's *Specters of Marx* (Critchley 1995; Lewis 1996; Postone 1998), and a run of items by Burkett and Foster develops ecological readings of Marx's value theory (Burkett 1996; Burkett 1997; Foster 1999).

#### 1995

- Balibar (1995), *The Philosophy of Marx*: a book-length study of Marx's philosophical position; the entry gives no fuller statement of its thesis, and CrossRef has no record of it.
- Brewer (1995), *A Minor Post-Ricardian? Marx as an Economist*: the title indicates an assessment of Marx as an economist, asking how far his economics modifies Ricardian classical political economy (title-based description).
- Cockshott, Cottrell, and Michaelson (1995), *Testing Marx: Some New Results from UK Data*: tests Marxian theses and categories against UK economic data from the mid-nineteenth to the late twentieth century, arguing that Marxian economics has nothing to fear from empirical confrontation.
- Critchley (1995), *On Derrida's Specters of Marx*: examines the hypotheses and secondary themes of Derrida's book, whose central theme is the messianic question, including the democracy to come, justice, hegemony and the New International; Marx figures as an inherited spectre.
- Likitkijsomboon (1995), *Marxian Theories of Value-Form*: surveys modern value-form theories that reconstruct Marx's derivation of money from the commodity, and assesses how far they preserve his integration of the social and quantitative aspects of value.
- Long (1995), *Marx, Fanon, Nkrumah, and the Intersection of Socialism and Radical Feminism*: the title indicates an exploration of socialism and radical feminism through Marx, Fanon and Nkrumah (title-based description; Marx is one of three thinkers).
- Roberts (1995), *Rereading Marx and Nietzsche*: the title indicates a dialogue between Marx and Nietzsche through a rereading of both (title-based description).
- Schwartz (1995), *The Origins of Marx's Hostility to Politics: The Devaluation of Rights and Justice*: the title indicates an argument that Marx's hostility to politics originates in his devaluation of rights and justice (title-based description; book chapter).
- Stabile (1995), *Postmodernism, Feminism, and Marx: Notes from the Abyss*: argues from a Marxist perspective on postmodernism and feminism; Marx is one of three named terms, and his role is inferred from the title (title-based description).
- Wood (1995), *Democracy against Capitalism*: argues that after the collapse of communism the Marxist critique of capitalism is more timely, and renews historical materialism against postmodern fragmentation and teleological readings.

#### 1996

- Burkett (1996), *Value, Capital and Nature: Some Ecological Implications of Marx's Critique of Political Economy*: reads Marx's critique as turning on a contradiction between nature and labour as sources of use value and value's representation of wealth as abstract labour time, tracing capitalism's despoliation of nature to this form.
- Castree (1996), *Invisible Leviathan: Speculations on Marx, Spivak, and the Question of Value*: argues that Marxism must respond creatively to contemporary problems, reading Marx's theory of value through Spivak's work on the question of value.
- Davis (1996), *Morris, Wilde, and Marx on the Social Preconditions of Individual Development*: compares Morris, Wilde and Marx on the social preconditions for individual development under socialism (Marx is one of three subjects).
- De Angelis (1996), *Social Relations, Commodity-Fetishism and Marx's Critique of Political Economy*: links the fetishised categories of bourgeois economics to the capitalist class relation, arguing that Marx's critique turns on the confusion of substance with form.
- Heinrich (1996), *Engels' Edition of the Third Volume of Capital and Marx's Original Manuscript*: compares Marx's 1864-65 manuscript, published in MEGA in 1993, with Engels's edition of *Capital* Volume III, finding significant editorial changes despite Engels's claim of fidelity.
- Jarvikoski (1996), *The Relation of Nature and Society in Marx and Durkheim*: compares how Marx and Durkheim conceive the relation between nature and society, evaluating both from the standpoint of environmental sociology (Marx is one of two subjects).
- Lewis (1996), *The Politics of "Hauntology" in Derrida's Specters of Marx*: the title indicates an examination of the politics of hauntology in Derrida's book, with Marx as inherited figure (title-based description).
- Lysandrou (1996), *Methodological Dualism and the Microfoundations of Marx's Economic Theory*: argues that Marx's economics uses an unconventional analytical reductionism starting from the single commodity rather than the individual agent, so that value and money yield a unified account of structure and behaviour.
- Van der Linden (1996), *Marx's Political Universalism*: the title indicates an examination of the political universalism in Marx's thought (title-based description).
- White (1996), *Needs, Labour, and Marx's Conception of Justice*: examines Marx's conception of justice in relation to needs and labour, contributing to the debate on whether Marx had a theory of justice.

#### 1997

- Burkett (1997), *Nature in Marx Reconsidered*: reassesses Alfred Schmidt's ecological interpretation of Marx from the standpoint of Marx's value-form analysis and his materialist view of nature under communism, arguing that Schmidt lapses into determinism.
- Evans (1997), *Marxian Theories of Credit Money and Capital*: the title indicates a discussion of Marxian theories of credit money and capital, with Marx and later Marxian writers as subjects (title-based description).
- Fraser (1997), *Two of a Kind: Hegel, Marx, Dialectic and Form*: argues that Hegel's and Marx's dialectics are the same, that the mysticism imputed to Hegel's dialectic is mistaken, and that both analyse social forms through general and determinate abstractions.
- Freudenthal (1997), *Marx's Critique of Economic Reason*: argues that Marx's critique of political economy uses "critique" in the Kantian sense of determining the legitimate domain of the categories of political economy.
- Meiksins Wood (1997), *Back to Marx*: argues that the post-1989 moment is the most appropriate time to bring Marx back because capitalism has become a global system (the bibliography tags it critical-skeptical, but the note reports a case for returning to Marx).
- O'Hara (1997), *Veblen's Critique of Marx's Philosophical Preconceptions of Political Economy*: a critical treatment of Marx through Veblen's criticisms that Marx relied on a natural right of labour to the full product and on teleological conscious agency.
- Roseberry (1997), *Marx and Anthropology*: examines the continuing relevance of Marx to anthropological theory through *The German Ideology*, *Capital* Volume 1 and *The Eighteenth Brumaire*.
- Saad-Filho (1997), *Concrete and Abstract Labour in Marx's Theory of Value*: reads Marx's value theory as overcoming the production/circulation dichotomy, treating abstract labour through the normalisation, synchronisation and homogenisation of labour in capitalist exchange.
- Smith (1997), *Friedrich Engels and Marx's Critique of Political Economy*: argues that Engels's 1843 *Outlines* started Marx's later work but that Engels's later writings never grasp what Marx took to be essential, so Engels's influence on readings of *Capital* needs correcting.
- Thomas (1997), *Enigmatic Writings: Karl Marx's The Civil War in France and the Paris Commune of 1871*: the title indicates an examination of Marx's *The Civil War in France* as a difficult, ambiguous text on the Paris Commune (title-based description). [unverified]

#### 1998

- Breckman (1998), *Marx, the Young Hegelians, and the Origins of Radical Social Theory*: traces the Young Hegelian origins of radical social theory, including Marx's move from social republicanism to communism.
- Brudney (1998), *Marx's Attempt to Leave Philosophy*: a monograph on Marx's early writings and his attempt to move beyond philosophy toward a critique of social life.
- Burkett (1998), *Labour, Eco-Regulation, and Value: A Response to Benton's Ecological Critique of Marx*: responds to Ted Benton's charge that Marx and Engels retreated from materialism to social constructionism about production, arguing that Benton dichotomises material and social elements of historical materialism.
- Cockshott and Cottrell (1998), *Does Marx Need to Transform?*: argues that Marx did not need to transform values into prices of production, challenging the assumption in the transformation-problem literature that equalised profit rates were the target of *Capital* III part 2 (book chapter).
- Foster and Magdoff (1998), *Liebig, Marx and the Depletion of Soil Fertility: Relevance for Today's Agriculture*: argues that Liebig's critique of soil depletion shaped Marx's analysis of capitalist agriculture and remains relevant for a critique of agriculture today (Marx is one of two subjects).
- Harding (1998), *Marx, Engels and the Manifesto: Working Class, Party, and Proletariat*: explores how Marx and Engels arrived at their conception of the proletariat as universal world-historical actor, distinguished from the empirical working class, as set out in the *Manifesto*.
- Marsden (1998), *The Unknown Masterpiece: Marx's Model of Capital*: offers a realist reading of Marx centred on the *Grundrisse* notebooks as an alternative to interpretations rooted in the 1859 Preface.
- Postone (1998), *Deconstruction as Social Critique: Derrida on Marx and the New World Order*: the title indicates an assessment of Derrida's *Specters of Marx* as social critique in the post-1989 context (title-based description).
- Stallybrass (1998), *Well Grubbed, Old Mole: Marx, Hamlet, and the (Un)fixing of Representation*: reads the double strategy of *The Eighteenth Brumaire* (history as catastrophic decline and as unsettling repetition) through Hamlet's ghost and the figure of the old mole.
- Williams (1998), *Money and Labour-Power: Marx after Hegel, or Smith plus Sraffa?*: argues that reinstating the dialectic, and purging any autonomous labour-value substructure beneath prices and money, keeps Marx's account of capitalism coherent after the Sraffian critique.
- Wood (1998), *The Communist Manifesto After 150 Years*: a short essay treating the *Manifesto* as a political declaration written on the eve of 1848 rather than a comprehensive scholarly study, marking its 150th anniversary.

#### 1999

- Bryer (1999), *Marx and Accounting*: the title indicates a treatment relating Marx's political economy to accounting (title-based description).
- Burkett (1999), *Marx and Nature*: a book reconstructing Marx's approach to nature, society and environmental crisis from his value theory, defending a "red and green" reading against charges of ecological blindness.
- Castree (1999), *Envisioning Capitalism: Geography and the Renewal of Marxian Political Economy*: reinterprets what Postone calls the fundamental core of capitalism to lay theoretical foundations for a renewed Marxian political economy within geography.
- Chong (1999), *Combining Marx with Kant: The Philosophical Anthropology of Li Zehou*: presents Li Zehou's philosophical anthropology as a combination of Marx and Kant (Marx is one of two sources; the main topic is Li).
- Cohen (1999), *Marxism after the Collapse of the Soviet Union*: the title indicates G. A. Cohen's consideration of the fate of Marxism after the Soviet collapse (title-based description).
- Foster (1999), *Marx's Theory of Metabolic Rift: Classical Foundations for Environmental Sociology*: recovers Marx's concept of a metabolic rift between society and nature, arguing that it shows classical sociology, and Marx in particular, contained systematic environmental insight.
- Kliman and McGlone (1999), *A Temporal Single-System Interpretation of Marx's Value Theory*: presents the temporal single-system interpretation, in which values and prices are determined interdependently over time, arguing that it refutes charges of internal inconsistency such as the falling rate of profit and redundancy of value.
- Nimtz (1999), *Marx and Engels: The Unsung Heroes of the Democratic Breakthrough*: argues that Marx and Engels contributed decisively to the political self-organisation of the European working class and thus to the democratic breakthrough.
- Perelman (1999), *Marx, Devalorisation, and the Theory of Value*: investigates devalorisation of fixed capital in Marx's value theory, arguing that it precludes an exact algebraic value theory while grounding an understanding of crises.
- Wheen (1999), *Karl Marx: A Life*: a popular biography portraying Marx as impoverished emigre, scholar in the British Museum and convivial friend who quarrelled with most associates (title-based description; note-based summary). [unverified]
- Wolff (1999), *Marx and Exploitation*: the title indicates an analysis of Marx's theory of exploitation and its relation to justice and to the labour theory of value (title-based description).

## Section 2: The 2000s (2000-2009)

### 2.1 2000-2004

Several entries in these years return to Hegelian and systematic-dialectical readings of *Capital* (Arthur 2002; Campbell and Reuten 2002; Bell 2003; Reuten 2004), while others continue the post-1989 reassessment (Burawoy 2000; Godelier 2000; Wolff 2002) and the debate over the temporal single-system interpretation (Mongiovi 2002; Veneziani 2004).

#### 2000

- Barrow (2000), *The Marx Problem in Marxian State Theory*: argues that Marxist state theory (instrumentalist, structuralist, derivationist) has diverged from Marx's own texts, raising a "Marx problem" about what his writings actually say on the state.
- Burawoy (2000), *Marxism after Communism*: assesses the prospects and reconstruction of Marxism after the fall of communism; the note reports only this topic (title-based description; concerns Marxism more than Marx's texts).
- Burns (2000), *Materialism in Ancient Greek Philosophy and in the Writings of the Young Marx*: asks about the young Marx's view of mind and body, tracing his and Feuerbach's position to Aristotle's middle path between idealism and materialism.
- Cohen (2000), *Karl Marx's Theory of History: A Defence*: expanded edition of the 1978 book, keeping the original defence of historical materialism and adding an introduction, "Reflections on Analytical Marxism", with reservations about traditional historical materialism (title-based description). [unverified]
- Daly (2000), *Marx and Justice*: takes up the question of Marx and justice, reviewing whether Marx's critique of capitalism can be understood in terms of justice.
- Foster (2000), *Marx's Ecology: Materialism and Nature*: argues that Marx's critique of capitalism sits within a materialist conception of nature and history, drawing on his writings on soil ecology, Epicurus and Darwin (title-based description). [unverified]
- Godelier (2000), *The Disappearance of the 'Socialist System': Failure or Confirmation of Marx's Views on the Transition from One Form of Production and Society to Another?* (book chapter): asks whether the collapse of the socialist system fails or confirms Marx's views on periods of transition; tagged critical-skeptical, though the note reports only the question.
- Hudis (2000), *The Dialectical Structure of Marx's Concept of 'Revolution in Permanence'*: analyses the dialectical structure of Marx's concept of revolution in permanence.
- Lapavitsas (2000), *On Marx's Analysis of Money Hoarding in the Turnover of Capital*: shows that Marx treats money hoarding as a necessary feature of capitalist production and circulation rather than an individual motive, and derives from it a monetary account of the turnover of capital.
- Mawhinney (2000), *Marx, Nature, and the Ethics of Nonidentity*: the title indicates a treatment of Marx's view of nature and an ethics of nonidentity (title-based description).
- McLaren and Farahmandpur (2000), *Research News and Comment: Reconsidering Marx in Post-Marxist Times: A Requiem for Postmodernism?*: reconsiders Marx for critical education in post-Marxist times, addressing postmodern critiques (title-based description).
- Murray (2000), *Marx's "Truly Social" Labour Theory of Value: Part I, Abstract Labour in Marxian Value Theory*: argues that abstract labour in Marx is "truly social" labour, constituted only under capitalist social relations (Part I of a two-part study).
- Tabak (2000), *Marx's Theory of Proletarian Dictatorship Revisited*: reads the Paris Commune, in Marx's view, as a form of proletarian dictatorship based on direct working-class rule with coercive and constructive functions.

#### 2001

- Burnham (2001), *Marx, International Political Economy and Globalisation*: notes that until 1989 Marxism in international relations was largely restricted to Marxism-Leninism, and considers Marx's relevance for international political economy and globalisation.
- Davidson (2001), *Marx and Engels on the Scottish Highlands*: treats Marx's and Engels's writings on the Scottish Highlands as an index of their changing positions on progress in societies facing capitalist development (Marx and Engels are joint subjects).
- Dussel (2001), *The Four Drafts of Capital: Toward a New Interpretation of the Dialectical Thought of Marx*: the title indicates a new interpretation of Marx's dialectical thought through the four successive drafts of *Capital* (title-based description).
- Filho (2001), *The Value of Marx*: argues for a reading of Marx's value theory that engages new-dialectic and value-form approaches, and assesses embodied-labour, Sraffian and Rubin-tradition interpretations for contemporary capitalism.
- Fine (2001), *The Marx-Hegel Relationship: Revisionist Interpretations*: discusses revisionist interpretations of the Marx-Hegel relationship.
- Fleetwood (2001), *What Kind of Theory Is Marx's Labour Theory of Value? A Critical Realist Inquiry*: uses critical realism to argue that Marx's labour theory of value is a causal-explanatory rather than deductivist theory, so that quantitative reconstructions lose explanatory power.
- Foster and Burkett (2001), *Marx and the Dialectic of Organic/Inorganic Relations*: the title indicates a discussion of Marx's dialectic of organic and inorganic relations, with metabolism as the topic (title-based description).
- Haug (2001), *From Marx to Gramsci, from Gramsci to Marx: Historical Materialism and the Philosophy of Praxis*: compares Marx and Gramsci on historical materialism and the philosophy of praxis (Marx and Gramsci are joint subjects).

- Archibald (2009), *Marx, Globalization and Alienation: Received and Underappreciated Wisdoms*: argues that Marx's theory of alienation still applies to a global context in which factory work is said to have been humanised or exported.

## Section 3: The 2010s (2010-2019)

### 3.1 2010-2012

Several entries in these years connect Marx's value theory, crisis theory and law of the falling profit rate to the 2007-8 crisis (Resnick and Wolff 2010; Tabb 2010; Carchedi 2011; Giacché 2011; Micocci 2011; Smith and Butovsky 2012), while others treat human nature, alienation and species-being (Musto 2010; Sayers 2011; Czank 2012; Tabak 2012; Weyher 2012).

- Smith (2013), *The "General Intellect" in the Grundrisse and Beyond*: examines Marx's "general intellect" in the Grundrisse against its use by Virno and Vercellone to describe post-Fordist capitalism.
- Sperber (2013), *Karl Marx the German*: places Marx in the intellectual and political world of nineteenth-century Germany, arguing that his theory paralleled other Young Hegelians but that his endorsement of English political economy set him apart (abstract-based).
- Sperber (2013), *Karl Marx: A Nineteenth-Century Life*: a biography placing Marx in the context of his own century and drawing on *MEGA* materials (title-based description) [unverified].
- Wendling (2013), *Second Nature: Gender in Marx's Grundrisse*: explores what the Grundrisse offers on gender, women's history and the family, and relates it to Marxist-feminist debates (book chapter).

#### 2014

- Bowring (2014), *Arendt after Marx: Rethinking the Dualism of Nature and World*: challenges the view that Arendt is unsympathetic to ecology, showing that she and Marx share more on nature and world than she presumed (Marx is one of two subjects).
- Brown (2014), *Marx on Gender and the Family: A Summary*: summarises Marx's writings on gender and the family, arguing they were once widely debated and were viewed as gender-blind by earlier feminist scholars.
- Chae (2014), *Marx on the Family and Class Consciousness*: examines Marx's account of the family in relation to class consciousness.
- Corell and Herzog (2014), *Subprime Crisis and Marx's Theory on Ground Rent*: applies Marx's theory of ground rent to the 2008-10 subprime crisis, treating mortgage-financed land and housing as a rent-bearing source of crisis.
- Crook and Short (2014), *Marx, Lemkin and the genocide-ecocide nexus*: links ecocide and genocide in extractive-industry contexts, taking Marx and Lemkin as theoretical starting points (Marx is one of two subjects; summary from title and opening of abstract).
- Foster and Holleman (2014), *The theory of unequal ecological exchange: a Marx-Odum dialectic*: reviews theories of unequal exchange from Ricardo and Marx onward and offers a Marx-Odum approach to unequal ecological exchange between centre and periphery (Marx is one of two foundations).
- Fuchs (2014), *Digital Labour and Karl Marx*: a book developing a critical theory of digital labour in the ICT industry using Marx's categories (year disputed between CrossRef and other indexes, which list 2013).
- Henning and Henninger (2014), *Philosophy after Marx*: gives a concise history of twentieth-century misreadings of Marx in German philosophy from Heidegger to Habermas, including the influence of Rawls and neopragmatism.
- Lange (2014), *Failed Abstraction: The Problem of Uno Kozo's Reading of Marx's Theory of the Value Form*: criticises Uno's rewriting of *Capital*, in particular his rejection of the early introduction of the value form in Volume I, and defends Marx's presentation.

- Pradella (2014), *Globalization and the Critique of Political Economy*: reconstructs Marx's critique of political economy within the history of political economy and reads it as a basis for analysing globalisation and the current crisis.

### 3.3 2015-2016

Several entries in these years take up Marx on human nature (Fox 2015; Byron 2016; Jaffe 2016; Struhl 2016) and on digital labour and media (Fuchs 2015; Koloğlugil 2015; Mosco 2016), while others apply Marxian value and money theory to finance and crisis (Jessop 2015; Kliman 2015; Toporowski 2016).

#### 2015

- Azeri (2015), *Marx's Concept of Class: A Reconsideration*: reconstructs a Marxian concept of class from the unfinished discussion in *Capital* III, chapter 52, arguing that class is a continuously constituted, politically mediated social relation rather than a sociological group.
- Casal (2015), *Marx, Rawls, Cohen, and Feminism*: argues that G. A. Cohen's work on Marx lacked gender-awareness while his work on Rawls drew on feminist inspiration, and defends Cohen's egalitarian ethos against a publicity objection (Marx is one of several named subjects).
- Fox (2015), *Marx, the Body, and Human Nature*: a monograph arguing that the body and the material world played a far more significant role in Marx's theory than previously recognised (the abstract is truncated in the record).
- Fuchs (2015), *Reading Marx in the Information Age*: a chapter-by-chapter introduction to *Capital* Volume 1 through media and communication studies, using the Internet, digital labour and social media as examples.
- Jessop (2015), *Hard cash, easy credit, fictitious capital: Critical reflections on money as a fetishised social relation*: draws on Marx's distinction between money as money and money as capital, and on Minsky, to analyse credit, financialisation and the post-crisis survival of neoliberalism.
- Kliman (2015), *The Great Recession and Marx's Crisis Theory*: uses Marx's crisis theory to argue that a half-century fall in US corporate profit rates accounts for the decline in accumulation before the Great Recession.
- Koloğlugil (2015), *Digitizing Karl Marx: The New Political Economy of General Intellect and Immaterial Labor*: describes digital immaterial labour as an online general intellect that resists reification in capital's means of production, while capital develops strategies to profit from it.
- Malott and Ford (2015), *Marx, Capital, and Education*: the title indicates a book on Marx's *Capital* and education (title-based description).
- Musto (2015), *The 'Young Marx' Myth in Interpretations of the Economic-Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844*: reviews the reception of the 1844 Manuscripts and, with the Paris notebooks, challenges readings that treat them as a finished work prefiguring or opposing Marx's mature thought.
- Ng (2015), *Ideology Critique from Hegel and Marx to Critical Theory*: the title indicates a treatment of the ideology-critique tradition from Hegel and Marx to critical theory (title-based description; Marx is one of several subjects).
- Piterberg and Veracini (2015), *Wakefield, Marx, and the world turned inside out*: examines Wakefield's theory of colonisation and Marx's rejoinder, linking Marx's primitive accumulation to settler colonialism (Marx is one of three named subjects).
- Pitts (2015), *Creative Industries, Value Theory and Michael Heinrich's New Reading of Marx*: applies Heinrich's "social validation" to argue that circulation-sphere labour in advertising, design and branding participates in value production, against autonomist claims about immaterial labour.
- Pivetti (2015), *Marx and the Development of Critical Political Economy*: argues that critical economics has been hampered by the failed integration of Marx's analysis with Keynes, and that Marx's treatment of distribution and investment yields a critical theory of effective demand.
- Starosta (2015), *Marx's Capital, Method and Revolutionary Subjectivity*: a book reconstructing the Marxian critique of political economy from the early writings to *Capital*, arguing that capital's development constitutes the working class as a revolutionary subject (year disputed between CrossRef and Semantic Scholar).

#### 2016

- Anderson (2016), *Marx at the Margins*: the note describes a book on Marx's writings on nationalism, ethnicity and non-Western societies, arguing for a multilinear, non-Eurocentric late Marx (title-based description; year disputed between CrossRef and other databases, and it may be a later edition).
- Byron (2016), *Essence and Alienation: Marx's Theory of Human Nature*: argues that Marx's theory of human nature and his theory of alienation depend on each other, and that Marx is an essentialist who sees human essence as an ensemble of socio-historical relations.
- Horn (2016), *Abstract Labour & Value in Marx's System (A belated reply to I.I. Rubin)*: offers an account of abstract labour as the source and substance of value and contrasts it with Rubin's account.
- Ince (2016), *Bringing the Economy Back In: Hannah Arendt, Karl Marx, and the Politics of Capitalism*: puts Arendt and Marx in conversation, using primitive accumulation and Arendtian power to argue that the politics of capitalism lies in expropriation as much as exploitation (Marx is one of two principal subjects).
- Jaffe (2016), *From Aristotle to Marx: A Critical Philosophical Anthropology*: argues that Marx's species-essence inverts Aristotle's priority of actuality over potentiality, so that the human is a set of historically developing potentialities.
- Moseley (2016), *Money and Totality*: a book presenting a "macro-monetary" interpretation of the logical method of *Capital*, on which Marx's theory is a monetary theory of total surplus-value and there is no transformation problem.
- Mosco (2016), *Marx in the Cloud*: the title indicates a chapter on Marx and cloud computing (title-based description; book chapter).
- Pradella (2016), *Crisis, Revolution and Hegemonic Transition: The American Civil War and Emancipation in Marx's Capital*: the title and note indicate a reading of *Capital* on the American Civil War and emancipation in terms of crisis, revolution and hegemonic transition (title-based description).
- Roberts (2016), *Twilight of Work: The Labor Question in Nietzsche and Marx*: intervenes in the debate on articulating Nietzsche with Marx through their shared critique of the capitalist work ethic and the separation of work from leisure (Marx is one of two principal subjects).
- Saito (2016), *Why Ecosocialism Needs Marx*: a short piece drawing on the post-1990s recovery of Marx's ecology by Foster and Burkett, with reference to their book *Marx and the Earth*, and previewing Saito's own reading.
- Stedman Jones (2016), *Karl Marx: Greatness and Illusion*: a full biography of Marx (title-based description; the record has no abstract and lists CrossRef's title as *Karl Marx*).
- Struhl (2016), *Marx and Human Nature: The Historical, the Trans-Historical, and Human Flourishing*: argues that Marx held a trans-historical as well as a historical concept of human nature, the former grounding his ideal of human flourishing in communist society.
- Toporowski (2016), *The Crisis of Finance in Marxian Political Economy*: the title indicates a discussion of financial crisis in Marxian political economy (title-based description).

### 3.4 2017-2018

Several entries in these years are tied to anniversaries: the year 1867, when *Capital* I appeared (Stedman Jones 2017), the 45 years since the "Notes on machines" was first published in English (Pitts 2017), and the bicentenary of Marx's birth (Fine and Saad-Filho 2018).

#### 2017

- Anderson (2017), *Marx's intertwining of race and class during the Civil War in the United States*: examines Marx's Civil War writings, which argue that capitalism was grounded in slavery and that racism attenuated class-consciousness among workers, while the war also opened new cross-racial democratic solidarities.
- Stedman Jones (2017), *History and Nature in Karl Marx: Marx's Debt to German Idealism*: the note reports an argument that Marx's account of history and nature draws on German idealism (title-based description; no abstract in the record).
- Löwy (2017), *Marx, Engels, and Ecology*: a brief survey of Marx's and Engels's ecological views for 21st-century ecosocialism, arguing that they are incomplete and dated yet still relevant, notably on capitalism's destructive effects and the metabolic rift (Marx and Engels are joint subjects).
- Moseley (2017), *Money and Totality: A Macro-Monetary Interpretation of Marx's Logic in Capital and the End of the "Transformation Problem"*: introduces the argument of his book of the same title that Marx's logical method is macroeconomic and monetary, so that no transformation problem arises in Marx's theory.
- Pitts (2017), *Beyond the Fragment: postoperaismo, postcapitalism and Marx's 'Notes on machines', 45 years on*: critiques postoperaist and Paul Mason-style uses of Marx's "Fragment on machines," drawing on the New Reading of Marx to argue that value is a social form resting on antagonistic social relations.
- Pradella (2017), *Marx and the Global South: Connecting History and Value Theory*: reads the critique of political economy through southern epistemologies, arguing that Marx incorporated colonialism and imperialism into his account of accumulation, including through his support for the Taiping revolution.
- Raekstad (2017), *The Democratic Theory of the Early Marx*: presents a reading of Marx's pre-1844 democratic theory and its link to his early views on alienation, arguing against established readings that a developed theory of alienation predates 1844 (the abstract is truncated in the record).
- Saito (2017), *Karl Marx's Ecosocialism*: the note describes a book arguing that the metabolism between humans and nature is central to Marx's critique of political economy, drawing on his notebooks (title-based description; no abstract in the record).
- Sinn (2017), *What Marx Means Today*: a critical assessment from a mainstream economist, arguing that Marx anticipated Keynesian aggregate demand and Schumpeterian creative destruction while criticising other parts of his system, and linking the falling rate of profit to secular stagnation.
- Smith (2017), *The discovery of surplus value: Karl Marx and Senior's 'last hour'*: claims that Marx worked out surplus value between 1857 and 1867 after reading Nassau Senior's "last hour" pamphlet, and offers two letters of 1862 and 1863 to help date the discovery.
- Starosta (2017), *The Role and Place of 'Commodity Fetishism' in Marx's Systematic-dialectical Exposition in Capital*: examines the section on commodity fetishism within the systematic sequence of form-determinations in *Capital* I, concluding that its significance is visible only through a grasp of the dialectical method.
- Stedman Jones (2017), *'Pressure from Without': Karl Marx and 1867*: treats 1864-67, when Marx wrote *Capital* and worked in the International Working Men's Association, as a case study of his political thought through his engagement with British parliamentary reform.
- Vrousalis (2017), *Capital without Wage-Labour: Marx's Modes of Subsumption Revisited*: argues that capitalist relations of production do not presuppose wage-labour, and distinguishes at least four modes of subsumption of labour by capital, one transitional to and one from the capitalist mode.
- Wood (2017), *Marx and Kant on Capitalist Exploitation*: relates Marx's view that capitalism is exploitative but not unjust to Kant's theory of right, using Fichte and Hegel to clarify how the two thinkers see workers' freedom being undermined (Marx is one of two subjects).
- Ypi (2017), *From Revelation to Revolution: The Critique of Religion in Kant and Marx*: examines Kant's and Marx's critiques of religion in relation to human emancipation, noting affinities on human nature, revealed religion and ethical community (Marx is one of two subjects).

#### 2018

- Araujo (2018), *On the Misappropriation of Marx's Late Writings on Russia: A Critique of Marx at the Margins*: criticises Kevin Anderson's book for extending Marx's remarks on the Russian commune bypassing capitalist stages to all precapitalist societies, arguing this "multilinearity" ignores the commune's specific dualism.
- Cowling (2018), *Marx and Human Nature*: the note indicates a chapter on Marx and human nature in a volume on Norman Geras's political thought (title-based description; book chapter).
- Federici (2018), *Marx and Feminism*: argues that Marx never developed a theory of gender but that his method allows gender and class to be thought together once reproductive, slave and migrant labour are included in the analysis of capitalism.
- Fine and Saad-Filho (2018), *Marx 200: The Abiding Relevance of the Labour Theory of Value*: argues that Marxist political economy, unified by Marx's theory of value, still explains social and economic reproduction, illustrated through the Sraffian critique, the value of labour power and financialisation.
- Hassan (2018), *"How to Liberate Marx from his Eurocentrism?"*: argues that Marx was bound by the evolutionary thinking and colonial framework of his time, and reads the Sudanese Marxist Abdel Khaliq Mahgoub's autobiographical text to discuss Marxism in African and Black contexts.
- Herzog (2018), *Marx's critique of ideology for discourse analysis: from analysis of ideologies to social critique*: draws on Marx's early and mature writings to argue that a Marxist notion of ideology as falsity can be adapted for discourse analysis and turns ideology critique into social critique.
- Holborow (2018), *Language, commodification and labour: the relevance of Marx*: uses Marx's concepts of commodification and labour power to analyse the commodification of workers' language skills in sociolinguistics, including workers' resistance to it.
- McCarthy (2018), *Marx and Social Justice*: a monograph presenting Marx's theory of social justice, grounded in Aristotelian ethics and politics and political economy, and contrasting it with liberal and reform-socialist views based on legal rights and fair distribution.
- Musto (2018), *The Writing of Capital: Genesis and Structure of Marx's Critique of Political Economy*: reconstructs the genesis of *Capital* from Marx's 1860s biography and the MEGA2 manuscripts, addressing why the initial six-book plan changed.
- Roberts (2018), *Marx's Inferno*: a book arguing that *Capital* is a work of political theory addressed to the workers' movement, modelled on Dante's *Inferno* and continuous with republican ideas of freedom (BibTeX year 2018; the key reads 2017).
- Stedman Jones (2018), *Karl Marx's changing picture of the end of capitalism*: traces three attempts, in the 1840s, the 1850s (*Grundrisse*) and *Capital* (1867), to theorise the value form or capitalist mode of production, and how Marx's conception of revolution and capitalism's end changed.
- Ware (2018), *Marx on Some Phases of Communism*: the note indicates a discussion of Marx's remarks on phases of communism, including the *Critique of the Gotha Programme* (title-based description; chapter in *Marx, Engels, and Marxisms*).
- Wright (2018), *Marx's transformation problem and Pasinetti's vertically integrated subsystems*: argues that the transformation problem dissolves once vertically super-integrated subsystems are considered, so the labour theory of value, suitably generalised, applies at the institutional stage of analysis.

### 3.5 2019

Several entries in this year treat Capital and its anniversary (Musto 2019; van der Linden and Hubmann 2019), and several take up Marx's political thought in republican and liberal terms (Roberts 2019; Thompson 2019; Shoikhedbrod 2019).

#### 2019

- Beatty (2019), *Marx and Engels, Ireland, and the Racial History of Capitalism*: examines Marx's and Engels's writings on Ireland within the racial history of capitalism (Marx and Engels are joint subjects; the abstract in the record is a quotation from Engels, so this line rests on the note).
- de Vivo (2019), *Marx and Sraffa: A Comment on Gehrke and Kurz*: a comment contending that the equations of Sraffa's *Production of Commodities* derive from Marx's reproduction schemes, defending Gilibert and de Vivo against Gehrke and Kurz, who deny any such Marxian origin.
- Fine (2019), *Marx's rent theory revisited? Landed property, nature and value*: revisits a debate of forty years earlier on Marx's theory of rent in light of newly published Marx papers, reassessing it and considering its relevance for contemporary issues.
- Giménez (2019), *Marx, Women, and Capitalist Social Reproduction*: collected essays holding that relations of production determine relations of social reproduction, and arguing on Marxian grounds that feminist politics is tied to class politics (collected essays).
- Johnson (2019), *The Early Life of Marx's "Mode of Production"*: re-reads the 1845-46 *German Ideology* manuscripts, arguing that the productive-forces theory of history is an artifact of later editing and that "mode of production" served as a historical lens on individual epochs.
- Leopold (2019), *More Greatness than Illusion: Stedman Jones on Marx*: a critical discussion of Stedman Jones's biography, which it says focuses on political context more than intellectual content and underestimates the complexity and relevance of parts of Marx's thought (article-length engagement with a biography).
- Musto (2019), ed., *Marx's Capital after 150 Years*: an edited volume of proceedings from an international conference on *Capital*'s 150th anniversary, in three parts on capitalism past and present, extending the critique, and the politics of *Capital* (edited volume).
- Psillos (2019), *Value and abstraction in Marx's Capital*: argues that Marx introduces value through two distinct kinds of abstraction, an "Aristotelian" one and a second based on an equivalence relation among commodities containing equal abstract general labour.
- Roberts (2019), *Marx's Social Republic: Political not Metaphysical*: argues that Marx's political project was a social republic freeing people from alien power, rejects a positive-freedom reading, and holds that this goal orients his critique of political economy.
- Shoikhedbrod (2019), *Revisiting Marx's Critique of Liberalism*: the note reports an argument that Marx's critique of liberalism targets bourgeois rights, legality and justice, and defends its continuing relevance (title-based description; no abstract in the record).
- Smith (2019), *Marx's Hegelian Critique of Hegel*: examines Marx's critique of Hegel as itself Hegelian, discussing Hegel's system of needs and arguing that Hegel missed capital as a dominant subject, so that reconciliation requires a socialism with deliberative democracy.
- Sorentino (2019), *The Abstract Slave: Anti-Blackness and Marx's Method*: an immanent critique of Marx's value-form argument, claiming that it cannot think the conjuncture of slavery and capitalism, and proposing to substitute slavery for labour as the abstraction.
- Swain (2019), *None so Fit to Break the Chains: Marx's Ethics of Self-Emancipation*: a monograph that reads Marx's ethics through his commitment to working-class self-emancipation, offering new perspectives on alienation, exploitation and utopianism (monograph).
- Thompson (2019), *The Radical Republican Structure of Marx's Critique of Capitalist Society*: argues that Marx's theory is "radical republican," resting on the social nature of humans, free individuality and a common good, with exploitation, alienation and fetishism as negations of these values.
- van der Linden and Hubmann (2019), *Marx's Capital: An Unfinishable Project?*: a volume of essays using the *MEGA* edition of Marx's economic manuscripts to trace the development of *Capital* and Engels's editing of volumes II and III (essay volume; year disputed between CrossRef and Semantic Scholar).

## Section 4: The 2020s (2020-2026)

### 4.1 2020-2022

Several entries in these years return to alienation and fetishism (Kandiyali 2020; Pimenta 2020; Øversveen 2021; Raekstad 2022) and to social reproduction (Cammack 2020; Yaman 2022), while others take Marx's *Capital* to contemporary platform labour (Pires 2021) and to the state (Das 2022).

#### 2020

- Bryer (2020), *Accounting for History in Marx's Capital*: uses an accounting interpretation of Marx's theories of history and value to defend the prediction that capitalism leads to socialism, arguing that demystified accounting is integral to Marx's concept of socialism (monograph; year disputed between CrossRef and Semantic Scholar).
- Cammack (2020), *Marx on Social Reproduction*: argues against the view that Marx said little on social reproduction, holding that the approach he developed with Engels and in *Capital* volume 1 provides a framework for analysing it.
- Cuyvers (2020), *Why Did Marx's Capital Remain Unfinished? On Some Old And New Arguments*: reviews explanations for the unfinished state of *Capital*, weighing Marx's health, commitments, other studies and alleged theoretical problems, and finds most of the theoretical explanations unconvincing.
- De Grandi and Tutin (2020), *Marx and the "Minsky moment" liquidity crises and reproduction crises in Das Kapital*: reconstructs a financial-instability theory in *Capital* III resembling Minsky's, linking the reproduction schemes of Book II to financial instability through Hilferding.
- Jal (2020), *Naxalbari and the Specters of Marx: A Contemporary Reflection on the Maoist Movement in India*: a review essay on D'Mello's *India After Naxalbari* that reflects on Indian Maoism and locates it in an epistemological break from Marx's original repertoire (Marx is not the main topic).
- Kandiyali (2020), *The Importance of Others: Marx on Unalienated Production*: argues that objections to Marx's vision of unalienated production rest on misinterpretation, and reads it as realising oneself by providing others with the goods and services they need.
- Karakilic (2020), *Why Do Humans Remain Central to the Knowledge Work in the Age of Robots? Marx's Fragment on Machines and Beyond*: revisits the *Fragment on Machines* through autonomist Marxism to challenge "world-without-work" claims, locating value in the general intellect embodied in living labour.
- Magness and Makovi (2020), *The Mainstreaming of Marx: Measuring the Effect of the Russian Revolution on Karl Marx's Influence*: a skeptical, empirical study using synthetic control on Google Ngram data, concluding that Marx's academic stature owes a debt to the 1917 revolution (CrossRef gives 2023; Semantic Scholar 2020).
- Monahan (2020), *The American Workingmen's Parties, Universal Suffrage, and Marx's Democratic Communism*: argues that the American Workingmen's Parties of 1828-32 anticipated Marx's democratic communism and its strategy of seizing the democratic state, which Marx knew through Hamilton and Cooper.
- Moseley (2020), *A critique of Shaikh's two interpretations of Marx's 'transformation problem'*: criticises Shaikh's iterative and Department 3 interpretations, finds no textual support for the second, and contrasts them with Moseley's macro-monetary reading (flagged INCOMPLETE in the record, which nonetheless holds an abstract).
- Musto (2020), *The Last Years of Karl Marx*: an intellectual biography of 1881-83 drawing on late manuscripts, arguing against the views that Marx stopped writing late in life and that he was a Eurocentric, class-only thinker (monograph).
- Musto (2020), *New Profiles of Marx after the Marx-Engels-Gesamtausgabe (MEGA 2)*: argues that the *MEGA* edition supports new profiles of Marx, set against the recent Marx revival (published in a journal of reviews).
- Nail (2020), *Marx in Motion*: introduces Marx's critique of capitalism to readers returning after the 2008 crash, proposing a "kinetic" process-materialist reading and treating primitive accumulation, metabolism, value, fetishism and dialectics (monograph).
- Pimenta (2020), *Alienation and fetishism in Karl Marx's critique of political economy*: argues that the early types of alienation are subsumed by the mature theory of commodity fetishism, which it treats as a radical criticism of modernity.
- Saito (2020), *Marx's Theory of Metabolism in the Age of Global Ecological Crisis*: revisits Marx's own theory of metabolism alongside the metabolic-rift school, distinguishing three levels of "metabolic rift" and of "metabolic shift" as a basis for updating his postcapitalism.
- Vrousalis (2020), *Socialism Unrevised: A Reply to Roemer on Marx, Exploitation, Solidarity, Worker Control*: the note reports a reply to Roemer on Marx, exploitation, solidarity and worker control (title-based description; no abstract in the record).
- Ypi (2020), *Democratic dictatorship: Political legitimacy in Marxist perspective*: reconstructs Marx's analysis of the dictatorship of the proletariat as a "limited legitimacy" theory of political authority, offered as an alternative to liberal and anarchist accounts.

#### 2021

- Barnes (2021), *Revisiting the 'Darwin-Marx correspondence': Multiple discovery and the rhetoric of priority*: re-examines, from archival sources, the belief that Marx proposed dedicating *Capital* to Darwin and the 1970s priority dispute over refuting it, as a case of multiple discovery.
- Harvey (2021), *The Political Economy of Health: Revisiting Its Marxian Origins to Address 21st-Century Health Inequalities*: surveys the political economy of health tradition and clarifies its Marxian legacy for public health (Marx is not the main topic; CrossRef gives 2021, Semantic Scholar 2020).
- Jefferies (2021), *Marx's Forgotten Transformation Solution: The Transformation of Values into Prices of Production in Marx's Grundrisse and Maksakovsky's The Capitalist Cycle*: argues that in the *Grundrisse* Marx treated the discontinuity in his transformation procedure as necessary, and links this to Maksakovsky's theory of conjuncture.
- Lazarus (2021), *The Lives of Marx: Hägglund and Marx's Philosophy after Pippin and Postone*: assesses Hägglund's *This Life* on time, value and freedom in relation to Pippin and Postone, and critically examines its concept of value through value-form theory.
- Macías Vázquez et al. (2021), *Class or Community? Marx, the Russian Commune, and Contemporary Critical Theory*: draws on Marx's writings on the Russian commune to relate class and community, rejecting a split between a categorical and an empirical Marx and questioning externalist postcolonial and decolonial theories.
- McNeill (2021), *Fetishism and the Theory of Value*: the title and subtitle indicate a reassessment of fetishism and value theory in Marx for the twenty-first century (title-based description; the abstract in the record belongs to another book).
- Musto (2021), *Karl Marx's Writings on Alienation*: the note describes a monograph tracing Marx's texts on alienation across his career (title-based description; no abstract in the record).
- Musto (2021), *The Experience of the Paris Commune and Marx's Reflections on Communism*: examines how the Paris Commune shaped Marx's reflections on communism (book chapter in *Marx, Engels, and Marxisms*; title-based description; no abstract in the record).
- Petrucciani (2021), *Is Marx Obsolete?*: the title poses the question of Marx's obsolescence, and the record tags it critical-skeptical (book chapter in *Marx, Engels, and Marxisms*; title-based description; no abstract in the record).
- Pires (2021), *Uberization of labor and Marx's Capital*: argues that Marx's analysis of piece-wage in *Capital* already anticipated Uberization and platform gig labour, including intensified work and indirect control of the labour process.
- Rauhala (2021), *The Neue Marx-Lektüre and the 'Monetary Theory of Value' in the East German Labour-Value Measurement Debate*: argues that East German economists in the early 1960s anticipated the monetary interpretation later ascribed to the Neue Marx-Lektüre (flagged INCOMPLETE in the record, which nonetheless holds an abstract).
- Øversveen (2021), *Capitalism and alienation: Towards a Marxist theory of alienation for the 21st century*: reinterprets the theory of alienation in Marx's later works as an objective process of appropriating production's results and turning them into capital, rather than a subjective experience.

#### 2022

- Alves (2022), *Fictitious capital, the credit system, and the particular case of government bonds in Marx*: updates Marx's theory of money and credit, arguing that fictitious capital represents no real capital and that government bonds are its keystone in financial markets.
- Basu (2022), *A Reformulated Version of Marx's Theory of Ground Rent Shows That There Cannot Be Any Absolute Rent*: builds a model decomposing rent into differential and absolute components and concludes that, under profit-maximising behaviour, absolute rent is zero.
- Chalmers (2022), *Free as A Bird: Nature as Freedom and Interval in Karl Marx's Capital*: reads Marx's *Vogelfreiheit* and "idyll" in the primitive accumulation chapters as satirical "natural-historical images" of free labour and the state of nature.
- Chrysis (2022), *The Marx of Communism*: the note describes a reconstruction of Marx's theory of communism across his works (monograph; title-based description; no abstract in the record).
- Das (2022), *Marx's Capital, Capitalism and Limits to the State*: examines the capitalist state in advanced and peripheral capitalism, arguing that the state is inherently capitalist and cannot sustainably meet mass needs (monograph; goes beyond Marx by drawing on later Marxism).
- Lindner (2022), *Marx, Marxism and the Question of Eurocentrism*: the title indicates a treatment of the Eurocentrism debate about Marx and Marxism (title-based description; no abstract in the record).
- Matysik (2022), *When Spinoza Met Marx*: a book pairing Spinoza and Marx, subtitled *Experiments in Nonhumanist Activity* (Marx is one of two subjects; title-based description; no abstract in the record).
- Raekstad (2022), *Karl Marx's Realist Critique of Capitalism*: reconstructs Marx's critique as realist, connecting his positive concept of freedom, his account of alienation as capitalist unfreedom, and democracy and socialism as its cures (monograph).
- Slater and Flaherty (2022), *Marx on the Reciprocal Interconnections between the Soil and the Human Body: Ireland and Its Colonialised Metabolic Rifts*: argues that Marx's writings on Ireland shaped his ecological thought, extending metabolic rift to a corporeal rift of depopulation and its demographic effects.
- Yaman (2022), *Roots of Social Reproduction Theory in Marx and Engels: A Total Analysis of Society and Life*: argues that the materialist conception of history in *The German Ideology* grounds social reproduction theory (Marx and Engels are joint subjects).

### 4.2 2023-2024

Several entries in these years contest Heinrich's readings of Marx's value theory and of the falling rate of profit (Balomenos 2023; Lietz and Schwarz 2023; Moseley 2023; Balomenos 2024), several return to the early Marx on the state and democracy (Barbour 2023; Flohr 2023; Halikias 2023; Flohr 2024), and two concern Saito's degrowth reading (Saito 2023; Napoletano 2024).

#### 2023

- Apostolidis (2023), *Night labour, social reproduction and political struggle in the 'Working Day' chapter of Marx's Capital*: reads night labour in the "Working Day" chapter as showing the destruction of workers' reproductive resources, and argues that struggles over social reproduction should be central to working-class politics, including for platform microworkers.
- Balomenos (2023), *Did Marx have Second Thoughts about the Law of the Falling Rate of Profit? An Archival Rejection of Heinrich's Arguments*: uses *MEGA* and *MECW* material to argue that the manuscripts and letters Heinrich cites show no doubt on Marx's part about the law and no 1870s shift toward circulation and credit.
- Barbour (2023), *'The intelligence of the people': Marx's early political thought and the young Hegelian concept of state*: contextualises the Young Hegelian theory of the state and argues that Marx began his critique of Hegel's philosophy of law in spring 1842, not summer 1843, as a contribution to Young Hegelian thought rather than a break.
- Flohr (2023), *Karl Marx's critique of the state as an alienation of society in his 1843 Critique of Hegel's Doctrine of State*: reconstructs Marx's critique of the modern sovereign state as an alienation of civil society rooted in private property, which also reinterprets his concept of alienation.
- Gan and Bai (2023), *Karl Marx's thoughts on critical pedagogy, reproduction, and aesthetic literacy in STEAM education and praxis*: draws on Marx's works and later Marxian scholars to develop critical pedagogy and aesthetic literacy for STEAM education under digital capitalism (Marx is an exemplar case; the main topic is education).
- Halikias (2023), *The Young Marx on Feudalism as the Democracy of Unfreedom*: examines Marx's early 1840s account of medieval feudalism, which integrated political and economic life, as a model of partial unity that "true democracy" will restore while rejecting feudal nostalgia.
- Lietz and Schwarz (2023), *Value, Exchange, and Heinrich's 'New Reading of Marx': Remarks on Marx's Value-Theory, 1867-72*: argues from Marx's revised value-form manuscript that abstract labour is a prerequisite of exchange rather than its result, against Heinrich (flagged INCOMPLETE in the record, which nonetheless holds an abstract).
- Lin and Wang (2023), *Back to Marx: reflections on the feminist crisis at the crossroads of neoliberalism and neoconservatism*: argues that a return to Marxist thought, tracing women's oppression to class oppression and reproductive labour, is needed to address feminism's crisis (the main topic is feminist theory).
- Moseley (2023), *Marx's Theory of Value in Chapter 1 of Capital*: the note indicates a critique of Heinrich's value-form interpretation of chapter 1 in defence of a macro-monetary reading (monograph; title-based description; no abstract in the record).
- Musto (2023), *A reappraisal of Marx's Ethnological Notebooks: family, gender, individual vs. state, and colonialism*: reappraises the 1880-81 notebooks, mainly on Morgan, Money, Phear and Maine, as fundamental to the concerns of the "late Marx" and as suggesting a reassessment of some key concepts.
- Oksala (2023), *Neoliberal Subjectivation: Between Foucault and Marx*: defends Foucault's account of subjectivation against Marxist critics and reads Foucault as a post-Marxist who extended Marx's views on power and the subject (Marx is one of two subjects and serves partly as a foil).
- Rotta and Kumar (2023), *Was Marx right? Development and exploitation in 43 countries, 2000-2014*: tests Marx's hypotheses on 43 economies and finds falling profit rates and rising unproductive activity in rich countries (tagged critical-skeptical, but the note and abstract report results consistent with Marx's hypotheses; year disputed between CrossRef and Semantic Scholar).
- Saito (2023), *Marx in the Anthropocene*: argues that Marx's ecology was marginalised by later Marxists and that new *MEGA* material on the late Marx supports an alternative to capitalism characterised as "degrowth communism" (monograph).
- Sommer and Stache (2023), *Marx's non-speciesist concept of labour*: answers human-animal studies charges of Cartesian dualism by arguing that Marx's concept of labour recognises non-waged and non-human labour without erasing qualitative distinctions.
- Spencer (2023), *Marx, Keynes and the future of working time*: compares Marx's and Keynes's visions of technology reducing working time and draws on both for the case for reducing work hours now (Marx is one of two subjects).

#### 2024

- Balomenos (2024), *Did Engels' editing of Capital, Volume 3 distort Marx's analysis of the 'tendency of the rate of profit to fall'?*: compares the 1864-65 manuscript with Part III of volume 3, sorts Engels's interventions into clusters, and concludes that his editing remains faithful to Marx's analysis.
- Brixel (2024), *The Unity of Marx's Concept of Alienated Labor*: argues that the features Marx ascribes to alienated labour follow from one feature of its formal motivational structure, that it is motivated by an incentive rather than by its product.
- Burns (2024), *Marx and the Concept of a Social Formation*: argues that the concept is not uniquely Althusserian but appears in Marx and Lenin, is central to classical Marxism, and can illuminate the debate on the transition from feudalism to capitalism.
- Copley (2024), *Monetary sovereignty and the 'Invisible Leviathan': the politics of Marx's theory of money*: uses Marx's monetary theory to argue that monetary sovereignty is a practice of depoliticisation that reproduces the law of value, not a vehicle for radical democracy.
- Dinerstein (2024), *On Motley feminism: A Decolonising Marxism for a thesis 14 of Marxism-feminism*: extends Frigga Haug's theses by addressing internal colonialism within feminism, proposing a decolonising Marxism drawing on the "late Marx" (Marx-centrality is moderate).
- Flohr (2024), *Beyond political theology: Marx's materialist theorization of democracy and constituent power*: reconstructs Marx's *Critique of Hegel's Doctrine of State* as a materialist theory of democracy and constituent power, and of "true democracy" as non-sovereign self-determination.
- Leipold (2024), *Citizen Marx*: traces three stages in Marx's relation to republicanism, from early republican commitments through communism to a synthesis prompted by the Paris Commune (monograph).
- Mueller (2024), *The theory and function of Marxian water rent in the United States*: the note indicates a development of a Marxian theory of water rent and its function in the United States (title-based description; no abstract in the record).
- Napoletano (2024), *Was Karl Marx a Degrowth Communist?*: assesses Saito's account of Marx's progression from productivism to degrowth communism, and argues for a sustainability-oriented dialectical ecosocialism rather than a break between growth and degrowth.
- Ricci (2024), *The Specter of Value: The Beginning of Marx's Capital and Hegel's Logic of Being*: argues that the opening of *Capital* draws on Hegel's *Logic of Being* and that value is a spectral real abstraction, an "absolute fetish" resting on primitive accumulation.
- Wapeemukwa (2024), *Primitive Speculation: Marx on Precapitalism, Social Relations to Land, and Indigenous Dispossession*: contrasts Marx's treatment of land relations in the *Grundrisse*, *Capital* and the late notebooks, and argues that the late Marx sees Indigenous relations to land as something communism must rehabilitate.
- Wills (2024), *Marx's Ethical Vision*: argues that Marx's writings contain a moral critique of capitalism compatible with his rejection of "preaching morality", centred on labour and workers' struggles (monograph).

### 4.3 2025-2026

Entries in 2025 include two on slavery in Marx's writings (Shafer 2025; Zambrana 2025) and several that set Marx beside another thinker (Alkazemi 2025; Edison 2025; Fuchs 2025; Montenegro Bralic 2025; Polleri 2025), while 2026 includes five books (Badger 2026; Blunden 2026; Kelly 2026; Roza 2026; Taufer 2026) and two on the late Marx's notebooks and writings on colonialism and gender (Yaman 2026; Zhang 2026).

#### 2025

- Alkazemi (2025), *Cry of the Earth, Cry of the Proletariat: Marx and Francis on Ecology and Inequality*: compares eco-Marxist work on Marx's metabolic rift with Pope Francis on ecology and inequality, examining where their diagnoses of present crises converge and diverge (Marx is one of two principal subjects).
- Clemesha (2025), *Karl Marx on the Jewish Question*: argues that *On the Jewish Question* answers Bruno Bauer's views on Jewish emancipation rather than analysing Judaism, and uses Bauer's views to reflect on political emancipation, the modern state and civil society.
- Cluley and Dunne (2025), *Taboo theorists: Karl Marx and marketing theory*: a hauntological reading, based on a review of 40 marketing journals, of why marketing theory treats Marx as taboo; it identifies four "spectres" of Marx there and proposes a Marxist marketing theory (the note tags it critical-skeptical, but the abstract examines marketing's hesitation towards Marx rather than attacking him).
- Edison (2025), *"None of It Matters Now": Leszek Kołakowski between Marx and Spinoza*: examines how Spinozism shaped Kołakowski's humanist Marxism between 1953 and 1968 (Marx is the tradition Kołakowski works within, not the sole subject).
- Fuchs (2025), *Antonio Negri, Spinoza, Marx, and Digital Capitalism*: combines Spinoza, Marx, Hegel and Negri to analyse the antagonism between digital labour and digital capital as one between potentia and potestas (Marx is one of four named thinkers).
- Huber (2025), *Reading Capital in an Age of Climate Change*: draws out climate implications of Volume 1 of *Capital* through value, surplus value, accumulation and primitive accumulation, and stresses the need for social control over production.
- Lawson (2025), *Social relations, social positioning theory and Marx*: compares social positioning theory with Marx's ontological and social-relational presuppositions, and argues that seeing Marx through this framework resolves several long-standing puzzles (Marx is one of two subjects).
- Montenegro Bralic (2025), *Marx, Heidegger, and the End of Philosophy*: the title indicates a comparison of Marx and Heidegger on the end of philosophy (title-based description; no abstract in the record; year given as 2025 by CrossRef and 2026 by Semantic Scholar).
- Oro and Perri (2025), *Between Sraffa and Marx: Pasinetti and the New Interpretation of the Labour Theory of Value*: re-reads Pasinetti's transformation of values into prices of production with Sraffian assumptions, bearing on the new macroeconomic interpretation of Marx's value theory (Sraffa and Marx are both subjects).
- Øversveen and Kelly (2025), *Marx's three modes of class analysis: A critical reconstruction*: argues that Marx's writings contain structural, sociological and political modes of class analysis, and that distinguishing them addresses long-standing problems in Marxist class theory.
- Polleri (2025), *Power(s) or Exploitation: On a 'Point of Heresy' between Foucault and Marx*: uses Balibar's "point of heresy" to bridge exploitation and relations of power, reading *Capital* Volume 1 as an analysis of the power relations that enable exploitation (Marx is one of two poles).
- Schuringa (2025), *Karl Marx and the Actualization of Philosophy*: a book arguing that Marx never turned away from philosophy but sought from his earliest writings to actualise it, reinterpreting his relation to Aristotle, Kant and Hegel.
- Shafer (2025), *Marx on Slavery and Capital in the American Civil War*: rereads Marx's Civil War journalism as a development of his theory of capitalism, against the view that he saw American slavery as precapitalist, and finds in it an analysis of the capitalist plantation.
- Siermiński (2025), *Marx Against Marx: An Outline of Mature Historical Materialism*: argues that Marx's thought was revolutionised in the 1850s, when he gave primacy to relations of production over productive forces and broke with his early technological determinism.
- Wang (2025), *Young Karl Marx: communism and critique of German philosophy before* Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844: studies Marx's transition to communism between March 1843 and February 1844, arguing that he reconstructed his philosophical base and criticised prevailing communism.
- Zambrana (2025), *Slavery and the Needs of Capital in Marx's Grundrisse*: the title and note indicate a treatment of slavery and the needs of capital in the *Grundrisse* (title-based description; no abstract in the record).

#### 2026

- Badger (2026), *Karl Marx's Ethics of Human Flourishing*: a book arguing that Marx developed an ethic of character development and human flourishing that resembles but diverges from Aristotle's, drawing on Hegel and Darwin and linking alienation and exploitation.
- Blunden (2026), *Marx's Capital*: a book whose note reports the argument that features of *Capital* (the commodity as germ cell, class as activity, immanent critique) were inspired by Hegel's *Logic* (title-based description; no abstract in the record).
- Boehncke (2026), *Shaping 'Grand Theories' Through Their Edition: The Case of the Marx-Engels-Gesamtausgabe (MEGA)*: uses the *MEGA* as a case study of how editions shape theories, distinguishing recovery, reconstruction and redefinition.
- Cheng (2026), *The Evolutionary Logic of Marx's Theory of the State*: traces Marx's theory of the state from the philosophy of law through civil society and real individuals to the critique of political economy, in four stages of critique.
- Kelly (2026), *Free Robot Labour*: an open-access book developing a roughly Marxist account of artificial labour and of how AI could lead to machines capable of labour.
- King (2026), *Rhetorical Materialism: Karl Marx and the Nineteenth-Century Writer as Weaver*: traces the weaver figure in nineteenth-century literature and argues that Marx used it for a theory of authorship's resistance to capitalist exploitation (Marx appears alongside literary writers such as Carlyle).
- Li and Huato (2026), *Marx's Law of the Tendency of the Profit Rate to Fall: A Re-Examination*: defends the logical core of the law, that rising capital composition with a constant rate of surplus value yields a falling profit rate, and extends it to some conditions of rising exploitation.
- Mau (2026), *Karl Marx and the Body: Towards an Eco-Marxist Philosophical Anthropology*: argues that the body is central to Marx's thought and reconstructs his dialectic of humans and nature as a basis for an eco-Marxist theory of human nature.
- Murdaco (2026), *Socialism or state capitalism? The transformation problem and the theoretical defense of Chinese "socialism"*: criticises Sraffian readings of the transformation problem and their defence of Chinese socialism, drawing on Moseley and comparing macro-monetary and temporal single-system interpretations.
- Pietrini (2026), *From Politics to Theory: The Early Public Reception of Marx in Britain, 1885-1887*: combines Google Ngram data with qualitative sources to argue that 1886 was a breakthrough year for mentions of Marx in England and that they shifted from generic to political.
- Roza (2026), *Marx versus Big Tech*: a book asking whether Marx's concepts, read through Humanist Marxism (Lukács, Lefebvre), still serve emancipation under platform capitalism, and treating platformisation as a new strategy of exploitation.
- Selwyn (2026), *Marx on the Circular Economy*: argues that mainstream uses of "circular economy" leave the present mode of production unchallenged and serve capitalist accumulation, and returns to Marx for an alternative account.
- Sobel (2026), *Spectacle and Commodity: Guy Debord and Marx*: analyses Debord's use of Marx's commodity analysis, comparing it with Anselm Jappe's value-critical reading, and asks whether the spectacle is reducible to the commodity (Marx is one of two subjects).
- Taufer (2026), *Marx's Anti-Practical Philosophy*: the note describes a monograph on the roots and aftermath of Marx's first critique of political economy (title-based description; no abstract in the record).
- Yaman (2026), *On Marx's Ethnological Notebooks: A Socialist Feminist Reconsideration*: argues for a socialist feminist rereading of the notebooks as a central part of Marx's late project, bearing on patriarchy, social reproduction and the variability of gender relations.
- Zhang (2026), *The late Marx's revolutionary roads: colonialism, gender, and Indigenous communism*: examines Marx's late thought on colonialism, gender and Indigenous communism as revolutionary roads, against stereotypes of Marx as a Eurocentric economist (the abstract in the record is truncated).

## Conclusion

The catalogue holds 104 entries for the 1990s, 110 for the 2000s, 134 for the 2010s and 98 for 2020-2026, a partial decade. These figures describe the search sample, not publication volume, and the patterns below are patterns in that sample.

Several 1990s entries respond to the collapse of state socialism (Krygier 1990; Hudelson 1993; Wood 1995; Cohen 1999), and Derrida's *Specters of Marx* (Derrida 1994) is followed by three entries that discuss it (Critchley 1995; Lewis 1996; Postone 1998). The justice and exploitation debate continues through the decade (Peffer 1990; Geras 1992; Wolff 1999). Value theory appears in several forms, including the temporal single-system interpretation (Kliman and McGlone 1999), which Mongiovi (2002) and Veneziani (2004) then criticise, and the systematic-dialectical readings of *Capital* (Arthur 2002; Campbell and Reuten 2002; Reuten 2004). Ecological readings appear from the mid-1990s (Burkett 1996; Foster 1999; Burkett 1999; Foster 2000).

Crisis titles appear from 2008 (Rasmus 2008) and cluster in 2010-2016 (Resnick and Wolff 2010; Giacché 2011; Smith and Butovsky 2012; Carchedi and Roberts 2013; Corell and Herzog 2014; Kliman 2015; Toporowski 2016). In the 2010s the catalogue also contains more items on ecology (Foster 2013; Saito 2016; Saito 2017), colonialism and race (Anderson 2010; Pradella 2013; Anderson 2017; Sorentino 2019), gender and social reproduction (Wendling 2013; Federici 2018; Giménez 2019) and digital technology (Fuchs 2014; Koloğlugil 2015; Pitts 2017). Anniversary items include Musto (2008) on the *Grundrisse*, Stedman Jones (2017) on 1867, Musto (2018; 2019) and van der Linden and Hubmann (2019) on *Capital*, Fine and Saad-Filho (2018) on the bicentenary, and biographies (Sperber 2013; Stedman Jones 2016).

For 2020-2026, items on alienation (Kandiyali 2020; Øversveen 2021; Brixel 2024), ethics (Wills 2024; Badger 2026), republicanism (Leipold 2024; earlier Roberts 2019; Thompson 2019), degrowth and the Anthropocene (Saito 2023; Napoletano 2024), and slavery (Shafer 2025; Zambrana 2025) are visible. So are items on platform labour and artificial intelligence (Pires 2021; Kelly 2026; Roza 2026) and on the late Marx's notebooks (Musto 2023; Yaman 2026; Zhang 2026). Several entries also dispute Heinrich's readings (Balomenos 2023; Lietz and Schwarz 2023; Moseley 2023). Themes were not counted, so no theme-level figures are given.

The limitations stated in the Introduction still apply. The catalogue is English only, and database gaps in OpenAlex, CORE and PhilPapers mean it is a sample, not a census. Many entries are title-based descriptions, and DOI-less books are marked "[unverified]". Year headings and per-year counts should not be read as measures of scholarly activity.
