## Section 2: The 2000s (2000-2009)

### 2.1 2000-2004

Several entries in these years return to Hegelian and systematic-dialectical readings of *Capital* (Arthur 2002; Campbell and Reuten 2002; Bell 2003; Reuten 2004), while others continue the post-1989 reassessment (Burawoy 2000; Godelier 2000; Wolff 2002) and the debate over the temporal single-system interpretation (Mongiovi 2002; Veneziani 2004).

#### 2000

- Barrow (2000), *The Marx Problem in Marxian State Theory*: argues that Marxist state theory (instrumentalist, structuralist, derivationist) has diverged from Marx's own texts, raising a "Marx problem" about what his writings actually say on the state.
- Burawoy (2000), *Marxism after Communism*: assesses the prospects and reconstruction of Marxism after the fall of communism; the note reports only this topic (title-based description; concerns Marxism more than Marx's texts).
- Burns (2000), *Materialism in Ancient Greek Philosophy and in the Writings of the Young Marx*: asks about the young Marx's view of mind and body, tracing his and Feuerbach's position to Aristotle's middle path between idealism and materialism.
- Cohen (2000), *Karl Marx's Theory of History: A Defence*: expanded edition of the 1978 book, keeping the original defence of historical materialism and adding an introduction, "Reflections on Analytical Marxism", with reservations about traditional historical materialism (title-based description). [unverified]
- Daly (2000), *Marx and Justice*: takes up the question of Marx and justice, reviewing whether Marx's critique of capitalism can be understood in terms of justice.
- Foster (2000), *Marx's Ecology: Materialism and Nature*: argues that Marx's critique of capitalism sits within a materialist conception of nature and history, drawing on his writings on soil ecology, Epicurus and Darwin (title-based description). [unverified]
- Godelier (2000), *The Disappearance of the 'Socialist System': Failure or Confirmation of Marx's Views on the Transition from One Form of Production and Society to Another?* (book chapter): asks whether the collapse of the socialist system fails or confirms Marx's views on periods of transition; tagged critical-skeptical, though the note reports only the question.
- Hudis (2000), *The Dialectical Structure of Marx's Concept of 'Revolution in Permanence'*: analyses the dialectical structure of Marx's concept of revolution in permanence.
- Lapavitsas (2000), *On Marx's Analysis of Money Hoarding in the Turnover of Capital*: shows that Marx treats money hoarding as a necessary feature of capitalist production and circulation rather than an individual motive, and derives from it a monetary account of the turnover of capital.
- Mawhinney (2000), *Marx, Nature, and the Ethics of Nonidentity*: the title indicates a treatment of Marx's view of nature and an ethics of nonidentity (title-based description).
- McLaren and Farahmandpur (2000), *Research News and Comment: Reconsidering Marx in Post-Marxist Times: A Requiem for Postmodernism?*: reconsiders Marx for critical education in post-Marxist times, addressing postmodern critiques (title-based description).
- Murray (2000), *Marx's "Truly Social" Labour Theory of Value: Part I, Abstract Labour in Marxian Value Theory*: argues that abstract labour in Marx is "truly social" labour, constituted only under capitalist social relations (Part I of a two-part study).
- Tabak (2000), *Marx's Theory of Proletarian Dictatorship Revisited*: reads the Paris Commune, in Marx's view, as a form of proletarian dictatorship based on direct working-class rule with coercive and constructive functions.

#### 2001

- Burnham (2001), *Marx, International Political Economy and Globalisation*: notes that until 1989 Marxism in international relations was largely restricted to Marxism-Leninism, and considers Marx's relevance for international political economy and globalisation.
- Davidson (2001), *Marx and Engels on the Scottish Highlands*: treats Marx's and Engels's writings on the Scottish Highlands as an index of their changing positions on progress in societies facing capitalist development (Marx and Engels are joint subjects).
- Dussel (2001), *The Four Drafts of Capital: Toward a New Interpretation of the Dialectical Thought of Marx*: the title indicates a new interpretation of Marx's dialectical thought through the four successive drafts of *Capital* (title-based description).
- Filho (2001), *The Value of Marx*: argues for a reading of Marx's value theory that engages new-dialectic and value-form approaches, and assesses embodied-labour, Sraffian and Rubin-tradition interpretations for contemporary capitalism.
- Fine (2001), *The Marx-Hegel Relationship: Revisionist Interpretations*: discusses revisionist interpretations of the Marx-Hegel relationship.
- Fleetwood (2001), *What Kind of Theory Is Marx's Labour Theory of Value? A Critical Realist Inquiry*: uses critical realism to argue that Marx's labour theory of value is a causal-explanatory rather than deductivist theory, so that quantitative reconstructions lose explanatory power.
- Foster and Burkett (2001), *Marx and the Dialectic of Organic/Inorganic Relations*: the title indicates a discussion of Marx's dialectic of organic and inorganic relations, with metabolism as the topic (title-based description).
- Haug (2001), *From Marx to Gramsci, from Gramsci to Marx: Historical Materialism and the Philosophy of Praxis*: compares Marx and Gramsci on historical materialism and the philosophy of praxis (Marx and Gramsci are joint subjects).
- Howard (2001), *Where Marx Was Right: Towards a More Secure Foundation for Heterodox Economics*: tagged critical-skeptical, but the note reports that, despite the collapse of socialist movements, Marx still offers a more secure foundation for heterodox economics than neoclassical theory.
- Nelson (2001), *The Poverty of Money*: draws on Marxian insights about money to argue for ecological economics (title-based description; Marx supplies a framework rather than being the sole topic).
- Nelson (2001), *Marx's Theory of the Money Commodity*: argues that Marx's theory is a distinctive commodity theory of money that grew out of his critique of the utopian socialists' notions of money and labour time.

#### 2002

- Anderson (2002), *Marx's Late Writings on Non-Western and Precapitalist Societies and Gender*: the title indicates a discussion of Marx's late writings on non-Western and precapitalist societies and on gender (title-based description).
- Arthur (2002), *The New Dialectic and Marx's Capital*: a book on the "new dialectic" and Marx's *Capital* (title-based description).
- Brobjer (2002), *Nietzsche's Knowledge of Marx and Marxism*: documents what Nietzsche knew of Marx and Marxism, a reception study in which Nietzsche is the reader (title-based description).
- Campbell and Reuten, eds. (2002), *The Culmination of Capital*: edited volume in which four philosophers and four economists interpret the third volume of *Capital*, taking up themes such as competition and the formation of prices (edited collection).
- Fairclough and Graham (2002), *Marx as a Critical Discourse Analyst*: identifies elements in Marx's economic and political writings that anticipate contemporary critical discourse analysis, and argues that Marx engaged in a form of discourse analysis.
- Geoghegan (2002), *'Let the Dead Bury Their Dead': Marx, Derrida and Bloch*: reads Marx with Derrida and Bloch on the inheritance of the revolutionary past (title-based description; Marx is one of three subjects).
- Jani (2002), *Karl Marx, Eurocentrism, and the 1857 Revolt in British India* (book chapter): considers Marx's Eurocentrism in relation to the 1857 revolt in British India and postcolonial studies.
- Mongiovi (2002), *Vulgar Economy in Marxian Garb: A Critique of Temporal Single System Marxism*: criticises Temporal Single System Marxism, arguing that Marx did err in his transformation algorithm but that this does not undermine his labour theory of value; critical of the TSS defence.
- Roth (2002), *The Author Marx and His Editor Engels: Different Views on Volume 3 of Capital*: contrasts Marx as author with Engels as editor in their differing views on Volume 3 of *Capital* (title-based description).
- Thoburn (2002), *Difference in Marx: The Lumpenproletariat and the Proletarian Unnamable*: examines the lumpenproletariat in Marx's writings as a figure of difference within class analysis.
- Wolff (2002), *Why Read Marx Today?*: a short book arguing that Marx still has something to say to us after the failure of Marxist regimes.

#### 2003

- Bell (2003), *From Hegel to Marx to the Dialectic of Capital* (book chapter): argues that a systematic account of capitalism is possible because capitalism reifies economic relations into impersonal commodity relations, tracing a route from Hegel to Marx's dialectic of capital.
- Brown (2003), *The Problem with Marx on Rights*: criticises Marx's position on rights in *On the Jewish Question* and beyond.
- Gailey (2003), *Community, State and Questions of Social Evolution in Marx's "Ethnological Notebooks"*: argues that after *Capital* Marx turned in the *Ethnological Notebooks* to class and state formation in precapitalist contexts, abandoning state-dependent evolutionary theory.
- Hall (2003), *Marx's Notes on Method: A 'Reading' of the '1857 Introduction'*: a close reading of Marx's 1857 Introduction to the *Grundrisse* as notes on method (title-based description).
- Macdonald (2003), *Inaugurating Heterodoxy: Marx's Eighteenth Brumaire and the "Limit-Experience" of Class Struggle*: reads the *Eighteenth Brumaire* as a "limit-experience" of class struggle that inaugurates a heterodox Marxism.
- Mahoney (2003), *Aron, Marx, and Marxism*: presents Raymond Aron's fifty-year engagement with Marx and Marxism as a balanced liberal exposition and critique of Marx's work; a liberal, critical-skeptical reading (Aron is the main subject).
- Manley (2003), *Marx in America: The New Deal*: tagged critical-skeptical; the note reports that it contests the claim that Marx's failed prediction and weak U.S. class conflict make his class theory irrelevant, using the New Deal as a case.
- Perri (2003), *The Counterfactual Method of Marx's Theory of Surplus*: shows that Marx supports his theory of surplus value with a counterfactual argument comparing the normal capitalist economy with a hypothetical one without surplus, in three steps.
- Rikowski (2003), *Alien Life: Marx and the Future of the Human*: the title indicates an exploration of Marx and the future of the human under capitalist alienation (title-based description).
- Ricoy (2003), *Marx on Division of Labour, Mechanization and Technical Progress*: reconstructs Marx's analysis of the forces of production in terms of division of labour, mechanisation and technical progress.
- Sayers (2003), *Creative Activity and Alienation in Hegel and Marx*: compares Hegel's and Marx's accounts of creative activity and alienation, developing a Hegelian reading of Marx's theory (Hegel and Marx are joint subjects).

#### 2004

- Burkett (2004), *Marx's Reproduction Schemes and the Environment*: the title indicates a discussion of Marx's reproduction schemes in relation to the environment (title-based description).
- Dimoulis and Milios (2004), *Commodity Fetishism vs. Capital Fetishism: Marxist Interpretations vis-a-vis Marx's Analyses in Capital*: compares Marxist interpretations of commodity fetishism and capital fetishism with Marx's own analyses in *Capital* (title-based description).
- Gimenez (2004), *Connecting Marx and Feminism in the Era of Globalization: A Preliminary Investigation*: a preliminary investigation of how Marx's theory can be connected to feminist analysis in an era of globalization.
- Hunt (2004), *Getting Marx and Foucault into Bed Together!*: a contribution to a journal series on a book that influenced the author, framed around bringing Marx and Foucault together in socio-legal thought (Marx is one of two subjects; based on title and series description).
- Koskenniemi (2004), *What Should International Lawyers Learn from Karl Marx?*: argues that early Marxian theory challenges the secular, state-centred and human-rights focus of modern international law, and asks what lawyers can learn from Marx despite his lack of interest in the field.
- Panayotakis (2004), *A Marxist Critique of Marx's Theory of History: Beyond the Dichotomy between Scientific and Critical Marxism*: applies Marxism to itself to move beyond Gouldner's dichotomy between scientific and critical Marxism, taking Marx's 1859 Preface as the paradigm of scientific Marxism.
- Reuten (2004), *"Zirkel vicieux" or Trend Fall? The Course of the Profit Rate in Marx's Capital III*: argues from Marx's 1864-65 manuscript that the profit-rate discussion supports neither a simple trend-fall nor a purely cyclical reading, and traces the inconsistency to Engels's edition.
- Rikowski (2004), *Marx and the Education of the Future*: outlines an anti-capitalist education of the future drawing on Marx's writings on education and his conception of communism as the real movement abolishing the present state of things.
- Roth (2004), *Retrieving Marx for the Human Rights Project*: argues that Marxian thought retains relevance not as a replacement for liberal human-rights theories but as a source of critique based on the values of freedom and dignity.
- Screpanti (2004), *Freedom and Social Goods: Rethinking Marx's Theory of Communism*: rethinks Marx's theory of communism in terms of freedom and social goods.
- Veneziani (2004), *The Temporal Single-System Interpretation of Marx's Economics: A Critical Evaluation*: critically evaluates the temporal single-system interpretation, arguing that TSS models lack a clear equilibrium concept and obtain Marx's value propositions tautologically; critical of the TSSI.
